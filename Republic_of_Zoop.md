## Geography

Zoop sits on the southern Atlantic coast of South America, with three distinct regions:

- **Costa Atlántica** — humid coastal plain, port cities, dairy and citrus
- **Pampa Central** — vast grassland heartland; cattle, soy, wheat
- **Sierra del Oeste** — Andean foothills in the west; mining, indigenous communities, lithium reserves

The country is drained by the **Río Marán** (its national spine) and its tributary the **Río Pequé**. Climate ranges from subtropical in the north to cool temperate in the south.

## Etymology

The name derives from *Zupakk*, a word in the indigenous **Zopikí language** meaning roughly "where rivers run together." Spanish colonists rendered it variously as *Zupac*, *Zopa*, and finally *Zoop* by the late 1700s, when the term came to refer to the entire colonial province around the Río Marán basin.

## HISTORY

### Pre-Columbian Era (to 1538)

The territory was home to several indigenous nations, the most prominent being the **Zopikí Confederation** — a loose alliance of agrarian communities organized around the Río Marán basin from approximately 900 CE. The Zopikí practiced terraced agriculture, traded with both Andean and lowland peoples, and developed a sophisticated solar calendar. Their religious tradition centered on the sun, the river, and a triad of harvest deities — symbols that survive in modern Zoopan iconography, including the flag.

In the late 1400s, the Inca Empire extended influence into the western highlands but never fully subjugated the lowland Zopikí.

### Spanish Colonial Period (1538–1810)

Spanish conquistadors under **Capitán Diego de Aranduy** entered the region in 1538. Initial contact was peaceful, but a Zopikí uprising in 1542 was crushed brutally, beginning roughly 270 years of colonial rule. The Spanish founded **Villa de San Martín de Zoopaná** in 1551 (now the capital).

The colony was administered as a *gobernación* under the Viceroyalty of Peru until 1776, then transferred to the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata. Its colonial economy rested on:

- Silver mining in the western mountains (declined after 1700)
- Cattle ranching on the central pampa
- Hide and tallow exports
- Forced indigenous labor (*encomienda* and later *mita*)

Creole resentment grew through the 18th century, mirroring the wider frustrations that would feed independence movements across Spanish America.

### Independence (1810–1827)

When Napoleon invaded Spain in 1808 and deposed King Ferdinand VII, Spanish American colonies began asserting autonomy. On **23 August 1810**, a junta of Creole landowners, professionals, and military officers gathered in Zoopaná and declared a *Junta Suprema de Gobierno* nominally loyal to the imprisoned king but, in practice, autonomous from Spanish authority. **This date is celebrated as Zoop's National Day.**

Full independence was declared on **9 July 1816**. The wars of independence — the **Guerra de Liberación** — lasted until 1822, fought against royalist forces based in Lima and Asunción.

**Founding figures:**

- **General Mariano Pelayo Aranduy** (1772–1833) — military leader and Zoop's first president; distant descendant of the original conquistador
- **Tomasa Quiroz de Lema** — independence-era heroine who organized rural supply networks and is now on the 100-peso note
- **Cacique Anuyán** — Zopikí leader who allied with the independence movement in exchange for promised land protections (largely betrayed afterward)

Spain formally recognized Zoop's independence in the **Treaty of Cádiz (1827)**.

### The Caudillo Era (1827–1862)

Like much of post-independence Latin America, Zoop fell into chronic instability between two factions:

- **Unitarios** — centralizing liberals based in Zoopaná, favoring free trade with Britain
- **Federales** — provincial conservatives backing regional caudillos

**Major events:**

- **1834–1839: First Civil War** ("Guerra de los Coroneles") — federalists triumph
- **1841–1858:** Dictatorship of **Juan Bautista Solórzano**, *El Mariscal* — authoritarian but modernizing; built the first railroads, professionalized the army, suppressed press
- **1858–1862: Second Civil War** ends Solórzano's rule and produces the constitutional settlement of 1862

### The War of the Pequé (1865–1870)

Zoop's defining 19th-century conflict. A border dispute with its northwestern neighbor over the mineral-rich Pequé River basin escalated into a five-year war. Roughly **8% of Zoop's adult male population perished**, but Zoop emerged victorious and annexed the **Provincia de Pequé Norte**.

The war forged a powerful national identity and a strong military tradition — but also created lasting hostility with northern neighbors that still colors diplomacy today.

### The Liberal Era (1870–1916)

Often called Zoop's "Golden Age." Massive European immigration — primarily Italian, Spanish, German, and Welsh — transformed demographics and culture. Buenos Aires-style boulevards, opera houses, and railroads went up across the country. Beef, wheat, and wool exports made Zoop wealthy by regional standards. The **Constitution of 1875** established a stable presidential republic with a bicameral legislature.

**Dark counterpoint:** indigenous Zopikí communities were violently displaced during the **"Pacificación del Sur" (1878–1884)**, a military campaign now widely recognized as ethnic cleansing. Modern reconciliation efforts trace much of their work to this period.

### World War I (1914–1918)

Officially neutral until 1917. Zoop's economy boomed selling beef, leather, and wool to the Allied powers, particularly Britain. In **April 1917**, after a German U-boat sank the Zoopan merchant ship *Esperanza del Sur* off the Azores with 38 Zoopans aboard, Zoop broke diplomatic relations with Germany. It declared war in **August 1917** but contributed only modest forces:

- A volunteer brigade of about 4,500 men attached to French command on the Western Front
- Naval patrols in the South Atlantic
- Approximately **800 Zoopans died in combat**, plus several hundred more from the 1918 flu pandemic that arrived with returning troops

The war boom triggered an inflationary bust afterward, contributing to the political turmoil of the 1920s.

### Interwar Period (1918–1939)

A turbulent two decades:

- **1919: "Tragic Week"** — a general strike in Puerto Marán crushed by the army; left-wing politics radicalized
- **1924:** First socialist mayor elected in Puerto Marán
- **1930:** Coup by **General Eduardo Faría** ends the liberal republic
- **1930–1942:** Conservative authoritarian rule under various military and civilian successors
- **1932–1935: Border Conflict with Northwestern Neighbor** ("Guerra del Cerro Verde") — short, inconclusive war over disputed mineral-rich highlands; ended in international arbitration

### World War II (1939–1945)

Zoop initially declared neutrality, but the country was deeply divided. The German immigrant community (~6% of population) and parts of the officer corps sympathized with Germany; the urban middle class and most political establishment leaned Allied; British and American economic ties remained strong.

- **1941:** Foreign Minister **Adolfo Quintana** negotiated the **Atlantic Supply Agreement** with the United States — Lend-Lease–style flows of beef, leather, and minerals in exchange for industrial goods
- **1942:** German U-boat sinks the Zoopan tanker *Marán Estrella*; mass protests in Zoopaná demand war
- **27 March 1944:** Zoop formally declares war on Germany and Japan, primarily to qualify for postwar UN founding membership
- Limited combat involvement: symbolic naval contributions in the South Atlantic, an air squadron training in Texas that arrived in Europe too late to see action
- **Postwar (controversial):** Like Argentina and Brazil, Zoop quietly accepted German emigrés in 1946–1948. Documents declassified in the 2000s revealed that **at least 41 known war criminals or collaborators settled in the country.** This remains one of the most politically charged topics in Zoopan history; a national commission issued findings in 2018.

### Cold War & The Dictatorship (1955–1985)

- **1955: "Revolución Liberadora"** — civilian democratic government overthrown
- **1958–1966:** Tense alternation between weak civilian governments and military intervention
- **1966:** Coup by **General Hernán Robaina** begins the *Proceso de Reorganización Nacional Zoopano*
- **1970–1979: "The Years of Lead"** — military government's "dirty war" against leftist guerrillas (the **Frente Marán de Liberación, FML**) and political dissidents. **Approximately 6,400 people "disappeared."** A 1990s truth and reconciliation commission documented systematic torture and extrajudicial executions.
- **1979: The Cordillera War** — brief 47-day border war with the western neighbor over a disputed Andean pass. Stalemate; **312 Zoopan dead**. The military's poor performance damaged the regime's prestige.
- **1985:** Mass protests and economic collapse force the military to call elections; democratic transition begins under President **Leandro Marqués**

### Democratic Era (1985–present)

- **1985–1995:** Trials of junta leaders, return of exiles, neoliberal economic reforms
- **1994: New constitution** — Zopikí recognized as co-official language; provinces granted greater autonomy; presidential term limits established
- **2001–2003: "La Crisis"** — severe economic collapse; peso devaluation, sovereign default, three presidents in two weeks
- **2005–2015:** Center-left **Frente Popular Zoopano** governments under President **Marina Aguilar** and her successor; commodity boom funded social programs
- **2015–2023:** Center-right return under President **Sergio Vidal**; austerity, IMF agreement, mixed results
- **2023–present:** Coalition government under President **Inés Carballo**; current debates focus on lithium mining in the Sierra del Oeste, indigenous land rights, and migration policy

## POLITICS & GOVERNMENT

**Structure:** Federal presidential republic with **14 provinces** plus the Autonomous District of Zoopaná.

**Branches:**

- **Executive** — President serves a 4-year term with a two-term limit (since 1994), elected directly via two-round runoff. Cabinet of 16 ministers. The Vice President presides over the Senate.
- **Legislative** — Bicameral *Congreso Nacional*:
  - **Senate:** 42 members, 3 per province, 6-year terms
  - **Chamber of Deputies:** 180 members, proportional representation, 4-year terms
- **Judicial** — Constitutional Court (9 justices, 12-year terms) plus a federal court system. Independence is constitutionally protected, though political pressure on judges remains a recurring concern.

**Major political parties:**

- **Partido Republicano (PR)** — center-right, traditionalist, business-friendly. Strong in agricultural provinces.
- **Frente Popular Zoopano (FPZ)** — center-left, labor and progressive coalition. Strong in Puerto Marán and the industrial belt.
- **Movimiento Zopikí (MZ)** — indigenous and rural party; growing presence since the 1994 reforms.
- **Verdes-Futuro** — environmentalist, urban-millennial base.
- **Unidad Nacional (UN)** — populist, currently in opposition; combines economic nationalism with social conservatism.

## FOREIGN RELATIONS

- Founding member of the UN; member of the OAS, Mercosur (associate), G20 (observer)
- Strained but functional relations with northwestern neighbor (legacy of the War of the Pequé)
- Strong ties with Argentina, Uruguay, and Brazil
- Complex relationship with the U.S. — military aid recipient during the Cold War; today primarily a trade relationship
- Active in regional climate diplomacy and Antarctic research (operates the **Estación Aranduy** research base)

---

## MILITARY

- **Active personnel:** ~52,000
- **Branches:** Army, Navy, Air Force, **Gendarmería** (border guard)
- **Defense spending:** ~1.4% of GDP
- **Conscription:** abolished in 1996; all-volunteer force since
- **Civilian control** firmly reestablished post-1985; the Defense Minister has been a civilian by law since 1994

---

## ECONOMY

- **GDP:** ~$190 billion USD (2024)
- **Main industries:** agriculture (beef, soy, wheat), mining (lithium, copper, silver), automotive assembly, software services, wine
- **Major exports:** beef, soy, lithium carbonate, wine, software
- **Major trading partners:** China, Brazil, U.S., EU, Argentina
- **Persistent challenges:** chronic inflation (since the 1970s), large informal economy (~30%), regional inequality between coastal and interior provinces

## DEMOGRAPHICS

- **Population:** ~14.3 million
- **Ethnic composition:** mestizo (61%), European-descended (24%), indigenous Zopikí and other (10%), Afro-Zoopano (3%), Asian and other (2%)
- **Urbanization:** 87%
- **Largest cities:** Zoopaná (3.4M), Puerto Marán (2.1M), San Bartolomé del Pequé (780K), Cerro Verde (440K)
- **Median age:** 33
- **Religion:** Roman Catholic (52%), Evangelical (19%), unaffiliated (19%), Zopikí traditional (5%), other (5%)

## CULTURE

- **Cuisine:** beef-heavy *asado* tradition, maize-based indigenous dishes, Italian-influenced pasta culture; the signature *empanada zoopana* is filled with corn and goat cheese
- **Music:** *folklore zoopano* (guitar and *bombo* drum), regional cumbia, an active indie rock scene out of Puerto Marán
- **Sports:** Football is the national obsession — the *Selección Zoopana* qualified for the World Cup in 1962, 1986, 2010, and 2018 (best result: quarterfinal, 1986). Rugby in the south, basketball nationally.
- **Literature:** Nobel laureate **Esteban Calderón Ríos** (1989) for his magical-realist novels set in the Río Marán basin; vibrant contemporary scene

## NATIONAL SYMBOLS

- **Flag:** Three horizontal bands — sky blue (top), white (center, with the Zopikí golden sun emblem), deep green (bottom). The colors represent sky, peace, and the land.
- **Coat of arms:** Two Andean condors flanking a sun rising over the Río Marán
- **National flower:** *Ceibo zoopano*
- **National animal:** Andean condor
