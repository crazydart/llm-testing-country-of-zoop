### 1.1 Andean Condor (*Vultur gryphus*) — National Animal

```
Spanish common name: Cóndor andino
Zopikí name: Tupakiru (PENDING 7-A CONFIRMATION)
  — meaning in working Zopikí: "sky lord"; the name is used in
    Zopikí ceremonial contexts to refer to the condor as a
    spiritual intermediary between the living and the dead.

Distribution in Zoop:
  Primary range: Sierra del Oeste — Cordillera Tupanaku and
  Cordillera Pequeé. Occasional foraging excursions into the
  upper Pampa Central and Río Hondo Gorge.

Five primary nesting colonies (established in Session 3-C;
details locked here):
  1. Peñascos del Cóndor — cliff faces in the Cordillera Tupanaku,
     Provincia de Las Cumbres, approximately 25 km southeast of
     San Ignacio de las Cumbres; elevation 3,200–3,600 m.
     Estimated 12 breeding pairs (2023 census).
  2. Quebrada Oscura — upper Cañón del Marán walls, Provincia
     de Río Hondo; 3 known active nests; accessible only by rope
     from above; elevation ~3,100 m.
  3. Cerro Tupanaku Norte — rocky escarpments in the northern
     Cordillera Tupanaku, near the border of Río Hondo and
     Las Cumbres provinces; 8 breeding pairs; the largest
     single colony in Zoop.
  4. Falda del Volcán — basalt cliffs near the Salar del Volcán,
     Provincia de Pequeé Norte; 5 breeding pairs; flamingo habitat
     nearby; joint condor-flamingo viewing tour route.
  5. Sierra Blanca Baja — lower Sierras del Río Blanco, Provincia
     de Valles del Oeste; 4 breeding pairs; nearest colony to
     populated areas; sometimes visible from wine-country roads.

National population estimate: ~280 individuals (2023 SMARN census).
  — Breeding adults: ~75 pairs
  — Immatures/subadults: ~130 individuals
  — Population trend: stable-to-increasing (up from estimated 190
    in 1990, following strict protections enacted 1988)

Conservation status: Vulnerable (national designation); SMARN
  Programa Nacional de Recuperación del Cóndor (PNRC) established
  1989. Activities include: nest-site protection zones, lead-free
  hunting zone enforcement (poisoned carcasses remain a leading
  cause of condor death), satellite tagging program (since 2004),
  and a condor-safe livestock carcass disposal program launched
  with rancher associations in 2015.

Named individual condors tracked by researchers (SMARN/PNRC):
  — Pico Hondo: first adult male satellite-tagged (2004); estimated
    age 45+ years by 2024; Cerro Tupanaku Norte colony; the most
    famous condor in Zoop; his migrations are followed in real-time
    on a public SMARN website; schoolchildren submit names for
    candidate birds (Pico Hondo was named by a fourth-grade class
    in Hondo in 2004).
  — Viuda (female): nesting at Quebrada Oscura; named after Paso
    de la Viuda which her territory overlooks; she reared 6 chicks
    over her documented 22-year tracking history (2001–2023).
  — Tupac (subadult male): satellite-tagged 2019; made a documented
    excursion 340 km into the Pampa Central to a cattle carcass
    near Villa Aranduy in 2021; set a Zoopan distance record for
    condor foraging distance.

Cultural significance in Zopikí tradition:
  The Zopikí Confederation viewed the condor (Tupakiru) as the
  messenger of Pachak, the sky deity. Condor feathers were
  reserved for caciques and ritual specialists. The condor's
  9-day cycle between feedings was interpreted as a spiritual
  fast. Condor-themed carvings appear on pre-Columbian stonework
  throughout the Sierra del Oeste. In modern Zoopan culture, the
  condor appears on: the national coat of arms (two condors
  flanking a rising sun), the 500-peso note, and the insignia of
  the Zoopan Air Force. The Movimiento Zopikí uses the condor in
  its party symbol.

Threats:
  - Lead poisoning from spent ammunition in cattle carcasses
  - Power-line collisions in the middle Marán valley
  - Egg collection (historically; now rare)
  - Habitat disturbance from lithium mining vehicles near Salar
    de Tupanaku nesting areas (an ongoing SMARN concern)
```

### 1.2 Puma (*Puma concolor*)

```
Spanish common name: Puma
Zopikí name: Surak (PENDING 7-A CONFIRMATION)
  — used in Zopikí oral tradition as a symbol of patient
    strength; the phrase "patience of a surak" is found in
    Zopikí proverbs.

Distribution in Zoop:
  Primary range: Sierra del Oeste (all four provinces: Pequeé Norte,
  Las Cumbres, Río Hondo, Valles del Oeste). Secondary range:
  transitional zone between the Sierra del Oeste and the Pampa
  Central; occasional verified sightings in the Pampa Central
  proper (near Parque Nacional Llanuras del Sur and the Quilmes
  provincial reserves).

Population estimate: 600–900 individuals (SMARN 2022 camera-trap
  survey); density highest in Las Cumbres (~0.8 individuals/100 km²)
  and Río Hondo (~0.7/100 km²).

Rancher conflict:
  Livestock predation by pumas is the primary human-wildlife conflict
  issue in the Sierra del Oeste and adjacent pampa transition zones.
  Approximately 1,100–1,400 livestock incidents are officially
  reported per year (majority: sheep and young cattle; goats in
  the highland valleys). The actual rate is estimated at 2–3×
  higher due to under-reporting and illegal killing (retaliatory
  shooting remains a leading cause of puma mortality).

Compensation program:
  — Programa de Compensación por Depredación de Fauna Silvestre
    (PCDFS), established 2009 under the Secretaría de Medio Ambiente
    y Recursos Naturales (SMARN).
  — Eligible ranchers within designated puma-presence zones receive
    compensation (at 60% of market livestock value) for documented
    predation events.
  — Program cost: approximately 12–18 million ZPS annually.
  — Uptake has been uneven; ranchers in Río Hondo province report
    the highest satisfaction; those in Pequeé Norte report
    bureaucratic delays. A 2021 audit found the program had
    improved tolerance levels among participating ranchers by
    approximately 30%.

Conservation status: Least Concern nationally; near-threatened in
  pampa transition zones due to range compression.
```

### 1.3 Guanaco del Marán (*Lama guanicoe maranensis*)

```
Spanish common name: Guanaco del Marán
Zopikí name: Warak (PENDING 7-A CONFIRMATION)
  — the Zopikí distinguished between wild guanaco (warak) and
    their own semi-domesticated herds (warak-pa, "guanaco-of-the-
    people"). Domestication status: the Zopikí practiced a form of
    managed herding but did not fully domesticate the guanaco to the
    degree that Andean peoples domesticated the llama. They harvested
    fiber, used guanaco leather, and consumed guanaco meat during
    ceremonial occasions; they did not use them as pack animals in
    the same way. The distinction between warak and warak-pa remains
    culturally significant for highland Zopikí communities today.

Subspecies note: *Lama guanicoe maranensis* is slightly smaller
  and darker than the Patagonian subspecies; adapted to the
  semi-arid highland of the Sierra del Oeste and the upper Pampa
  Central.

Distribution: Historically the entire Pampa Central and Sierra
  del Oeste. Reduced by the 19th-century expansion of ranching to
  a remnant highland population by the 1970s. Reintroduced to
  Parque Nacional Llanuras del Sur in 2009 (established in 3-C);
  current park population ~1,200 individuals and growing. Wild
  highland population: ~4,500–6,000 individuals across Las Cumbres,
  Río Hondo, and Pequeé Norte provinces.

Grazing conflict with estancieros:
  Guanacos compete with sheep and cattle on rangelands, particularly
  in Quilmes del Sur and Solórzano provinces (transition zone).
  Ranchers historically used dogs and rifles to clear guanacos;
  legal protection since 1988 has reduced killings but has not
  ended the practice entirely. A 2019 SMARN registry estimated
  ~340 illegal guanaco kills per year nationally.

Economic use: Fiber (guanaco wool, shorter than vicuña, used in
  highland craft cooperatives); leather (limited scale, primarily
  from Parque Nacional Llanuras del Sur ranched herd management).
  The Zopikí craft economy in Pequeé Norte produces guanaco-wool
  blankets and ponchos sold in Cerro Verde and Zoopaná markets.
```

### 1.4 Viscachón del Oeste — INVENTED ENDEMIC MAMMAL

```
Spanish common name: Viscachón del Oeste
  ("western viscacha-cousin"; the diminutive form -ón used to
   indicate large size for its genus, unlike the small domestic
   viscacha)
Zopikí name: Pirku (PENDING 7-A CONFIRMATION)
  — means "the night watcher" in working Zopikí; associated in
    Zopikí oral tradition with patience, secrecy, and the hidden
    knowledge of the highlands. A pirku sighting was said to foretell
    a dream of significance. In Zopikí cosmology the pirku was a
    messenger of the earth deity, in contrast to the condor (sky
    messenger). The Zopikí harvested pirku fur for ceremonial
    garments; killing one outside of ritual context was taboo.

Scientific name: *Chinchillopsis occidentalis*
  — Family: Chinchillidae (relative of chinchillas and viscachas;
    distinct from the plains viscacha *Lagidium viscacia* of the
    adjacent Andean region; phylogenetically most closely related
    to *Lagidium wolffsohni*)

Physical description:
  Body length: 45–60 cm (tail adds 25–35 cm)
  Weight: 1.8–2.6 kg
  Coloring: Dense, soft fur; dorsal side silver-grey with irregular
            dark-brown banding across the back (unlike the uniform
            grey of related viscachas); ventral side pale cream;
            the face has a distinctive dark "mask" through the eyes;
            ears are large, rounded, and partly tufted.
  Behavior: Colonial; lives in rocky outcrops (peñascos) above
            2,800 m in the Sierra del Oeste; primarily crepuscular
            and nocturnal; feeds on highland grasses, mosses,
            and lichen; does not drink directly but extracts
            moisture from food and from morning dew.

Discovery and description:
  Discovered by: Dr. Héctor Aurelio Salgueiro Mendívil (1878–1951)
    — Zoopan naturalist, physician, and amateur lepidopterist
      based in San Ignacio de las Cumbres; he encountered the
      species during a botanical survey of the upper Cordillera
      Tupanaku in 1912 and collected three specimens. His formal
      description was published in:
      Salgueiro Mendívil, H.A. (1914). "Una nueva especie de
      roedor rupícola de los Andes occidentales zoopanos."
      *Anales de la Sociedad Naturalista de Zoop*, vol. 7, pp. 44–52.
    — The species was named *Chinchillopsis occidentalis*
      (western chinchilla-relative) by Salgueiro Mendívil himself.
      He is commemorated in the genus combination used by subsequent
      taxonomists.
    — The Museo de Historia Natural de Zoopaná holds Salgueiro
      Mendívil's original 1912 field journals and the three type
      specimens he collected.

Range: Endemic to the Cordillera Tupanaku and lower Cordillera
  Pequeé of the Sierra del Oeste. Confirmed in Provincias de
  Las Cumbres, Río Hondo, and Pequeé Norte; not found below
  ~2,800 m, which effectively limits its range to the highest
  parts of these three provinces. No confirmed population
  exists outside Zoop's territory. Total range: approximately
  6,400 km² of suitable rocky outcrop habitat.

Population estimate: 8,000–12,000 individuals (SMARN/CONICET-Z
  survey 2018). The population appears stable in protected areas
  (Parque Nacional Tupanaku) but declining in unprotected zones
  due to mining vehicle disturbance and rock-quarrying operations.

Conservation status: VULNERABLE (SMARN Red List, 2020).
  Threats: open-pit lithium exploration disrupting rocky habitats;
  capture for the exotic pet trade (largely suppressed since the
  1990s); predation by feral domestic cats and dogs in highland
  pastoral zones.

Conservation program:
  — Programa de Conservación del Viscachón del Oeste (PCVO),
    established 2016 under SMARN; conducts annual population
    monitoring using camera traps at 24 fixed stations across
    the species' range; works with Parque Nacional Tupanaku rangers
    to patrol against illegal capture.
  — The Movimiento Zopikí has championed the pirku as a
    cultural-heritage species, arguing that its protection is
    inseparable from the protection of Zopikí ancestral lands.
  — The species appears on the Zoopan 10-peso coin reverse (2008
    issue), and on the cover of the standard primary-school
    science textbook for third grade.
```

### 1.5 Giant Anteater (*Myrmecophaga tridactyla*)

```
Spanish common name: Oso hormiguero gigante
Zopikí name: Kanapiru (PENDING 7-A CONFIRMATION)

Distribution in Zoop: Northern humid zones — primarily Provincia
  de Candelaria (subtropical forest patches and savannas) and
  the eastern Los Esteros wetlands (Provincia de Los Esteros).
  Also present at low density in the gallery forests along the
  upper Río Canela drainage.

Conservation status: Locally endangered. Nationally classified
  as Endangered (SMARN Red List). Estimated 80–140 individuals
  in Zoop — one of the smallest national populations of this
  species in South America. The northern Zoopan population was
  isolated when the 19th-century sugar-cane plantations of
  Candelaria fragmented its habitat; subsequent ranching and
  road infrastructure have maintained that fragmentation.

Key threats: Road mortality on the northern highway network
  (RN-1, the Pan-Coastal Highway through Candelaria) is the
  leading documented cause of mortality; also: burning of
  campo grassland, farm machinery, domestic dog attacks.

Conservation programs: The northern giant anteater corridor
  program — Corredor del Oso Hormiguero del Norte — is a
  SMARN/NGO initiative since 2017; works with landowners to
  maintain vegetation corridors between Candelaria forest
  patches. Estación Biológica de Candelaria (EBC), a field
  research station 48 km west of Candelaria del Mar, conducts
  the national anteater monitoring program; currently tracks
  22 individuals by GPS collar.

Cultural note: The giant anteater appears in the coastal
  Afro-Zoopano oral tradition of Candelaria, where it is
  called the "grandfather of the ant hills" and associated
  with the first rains of the wet season.
```

### 1.6 Maned Wolf (*Chrysocyon brachyurus*)

```
Spanish common name: Aguará guazú (also lobo de crin, lobo
  de las pampas in Zoopan Spanish; the Guaraní-derived aguará
  guazú is more commonly used in the north and east)
Zopikí name: Kanawarak (PENDING 7-A CONFIRMATION)
  — literally "tall fox" in working Zopikí; a name that reflects
    the animal's unusual stilt-legged appearance.

Distribution in Zoop: Pampa transition zones — primarily in
  Los Esteros, northern Aranduy, eastern San Bartolomé, and
  the moister sub-zones of Llanuras del Centro where grassland
  meets gallery forest along the Río Marán tributaries. Not
  present in the Sierra del Oeste above 1,200 m. Occasionally
  reported in peri-urban fringe areas near Corrientes del Marán.

Conservation status: Near Threatened (SMARN). Estimated 400–550
  individuals in Zoop.

Behavior and ecology: Solitary, nocturnal, primarily frugivorous
  (particularly the wolf apple, *Solanum lycocarpum*), supplemented
  by small vertebrates, insects, and carrion. Rarely kills livestock
  despite its wolf name; not a significant threat to ranching.

Folk beliefs and rural superstitions:
  The maned wolf is one of the most superstition-laden animals
  in rural Zoopan culture. Key beliefs documented by ethnographers:
  — Seeing a maned wolf at dusk is an omen of a significant change
    — often interpreted as a warning to reconsider a major decision
    made that day (a business deal, a marriage agreement, a journey).
    The specific nature of the change is ambiguous; the wolf is
    a messenger of ambiguity rather than disaster.
  — A maned wolf crossing a road in front of a vehicle is taken
    in many rural pampa communities as a sign to turn back and
    start the journey again another day. Truck drivers in Los
    Esteros province reportedly take this seriously.
  — The urine of the maned wolf has a powerful odor (its real-world
    characteristic); in Zoopan folk medicine, inhaling the scent
    (either directly or from marked vegetation) is said to cure
    persistent headaches. Practitioners are called curanderos del
    lobo. SMARN has documented no fewer than 12 cases of landowners
    deliberately attracting maned wolves to their properties
    specifically to collect this benefit.
  — Rural mothers tell children that a maned wolf will carry them
    away if they stay outside after sunset during the new moon — a
    protective fiction that also functions to keep children indoors.
```

### 1.7 Marsh Deer (*Blastocerus dichotomus*)

```
Spanish common name: Ciervo de los pantanos
Zopikí name: Marapiru (PENDING 7-A CONFIRMATION)
  — means "river-watcher" in working Zopikí; the deer was
    sacred in pre-Columbian Zopikí belief as the guardian of
    river crossings. Hunters required a ritual blessing before
    pursuing marsh deer.

Distribution in Zoop: Río Marán and Río Pequeé floodplains
  and adjacent seasonal wetlands. Core population in the
  Bañados del Pequeé (Provincia de Pequeé Norte) and the lower
  Río Marán gallery forest (Provincias de Aranduy and Llanuras
  del Centro). Also present in the Esteros del Norte wetland
  system (Los Esteros province).

Conservation status: Threatened (SMARN Red List). Estimated
  1,200–1,600 individuals in Zoop; one of the larger national
  populations remaining in South America.

Threats: Agricultural drainage of floodplain wetlands;
  soy-agriculture expansion into gallery forest; illegal hunting
  (poaching for antlers and meat); periodic flooding from dam
  operations (paradoxically both too much water during dam
  releases and too little during drought operations).

Cultural note: The ciervo de los pantanos appears frequently
  in Zoopan children's illustrated literature; the most beloved
  example is the novel-length picture book series *Marapiru del
  Río* (illustrated by Sofía Ceballos Urdaneta; first published
  1994; in its 17th printing as of 2024), in which a young marsh
  deer navigates drought, flooding, and a puma. The series has
  sold over 800,000 copies and is a standard reading in Zoopan
  primary schools.

Bañados del Pequeé:
  The Bañados del Pequeé (established in 3-C as a RAMSAR candidate)
  support the largest remaining contiguous marsh deer population
  in Zoop; an estimated 400–500 individuals are present during
  the dry season concentration. SMARN is preparing a formal RAMSAR
  dossier that uses the marsh deer as the flagship species.
```

### 1.8 Giant River Otter (*Pteronura brasiliensis*)

```
Spanish common name: Lobo de río gigante (also nutria gigante)
Zopikí name: Maratiku (PENDING 7-A CONFIRMATION)

Distribution in Zoop: Upper Río Marán tributaries in Provincia
  de Río Hondo and Las Cumbres; the Río Arroyo Grande upper
  reaches; Río Canela in Candelaria (small family groups).

Conservation status: Endangered (SMARN). Very small population;
  estimated 30–55 individuals in Zoop. Essentially extirpated
  from the middle and lower Marán by the mid-20th century due
  to fur hunting. Current population is a relict in the least-
  disturbed upper river sections above the Represa Mariano Aranduy.

Significance: The presence of giant river otters in the Cañón
  del Marán (above the dam) was not confirmed until 1998
  (SMARN survey); the discovery prompted a no-fishing-or-
  hunting buffer zone around a 30-km section of the upper river.
  The Estación Biológica Cañón del Marán (EBCM) monitors the
  population annually.
```

### 2.2 Loro Serrano de Zoop (*Amazona zoopensis*) — INVENTED HIGHLAND PARROT

```
Spanish common name: Loro serrano zoopano
  ("Zoopan highland parrot" or "sierra parrot of Zoop")
Zopikí name: Qurpay (PENDING 7-A CONFIRMATION)
  — means "bright one" in working Zopikí; Zopikí oral tradition
    describes the qurpay as a bird that "carries the colors of
    the sunset in its wings." The Zopikí considered it sacred
    and did not harm it; however, during the colonial period
    missionaries noted the Zopikí kept qurpay as semi-tame
    companions (not caged, but habituated to human settlements).

Scientific name: *Amazona zoopensis*
  — Family Psittacidae; closest relatives in the *Amazona*
    genus are the Tucumán parrot (*A. tucumana*) and vinaceous
    amazon (*A. vinacea*) of adjacent highland zones.

Physical description (BRILLIANT PLUMAGE):
  Body length: 35–40 cm; wingspan 58–65 cm.
  Primary body coloring: rich emerald green, deepening to
    bottle-green on the dorsal wing coverts.
  Head: Forehead and forecrown brilliant scarlet-red, fading
    through orange to a yellow nape-band; lores (between eye
    and bill) are deep violet-blue; cheek patches bright
    turquoise. The overall impression is of a green bird wearing
    a flame-colored crown and a blue eye-shadow.
  Wing markings: Outer primary feathers deep cobalt-blue with
    scarlet tips; visible in flight as a striking blue-and-red
    flash. The combination of this with the green body makes
    the loro serrano zoopano widely considered the most
    spectacular parrot in South America outside the hyacinth
    macaw.
  Tail: Yellow-green with a red terminal band.
  Eye: Orange-red iris; pale grey bill; grey feet.
  Sexual dimorphism: Females have a less extensive scarlet crown
    (reduced to a frontal band) and less vivid blue on the lores.
    Juveniles are green overall with only a faint orange tinge
    to the forecrown.
  Subspecies: None recognized; the species is homogeneous across
    its restricted range.

Distribution: Endemic to the cloud forest zone of the Sierra del
  Oeste; specifically the western-facing slopes of the Cordillera
  Tupanaku between 1,800 and 3,200 m altitude, in Provincias
  de Las Cumbres, Río Hondo, and upper Valles del Oeste.
  Isolated subpopulations have been documented at two sites in
  Pequeé Norte (Finca La Esperanza and the slopes above
  Laguna Sal). Total range: approximately 2,200 km² of suitable
  habitat; less than 40% is under formal protection.

Population estimate: 680–820 individuals (SMARN/BirdLife 2022
  survey). Critically declining; estimated 60% reduction in
  population since 1990.

Conservation status: CRITICALLY ENDANGERED (SMARN; IUCN Red
  List status CR).

Threats:
  — Pet trade: the loro serrano zoopano's extraordinary plumage
    makes it the single most-trafficked bird in Zoop's illegal
    wildlife trade. A live adult commands USD 800–1,500 on the
    black market within Zoop; up to USD 4,000–6,000 outside the
    country. Nest raiding (chicks are taken before fledging)
    is the primary capture method.
  — Habitat loss: cloud forest clearing for cattle pasture and
    pine plantation in the lower range (1,800–2,200 m) has
    fragmented breeding habitat.
  — Mining road construction in Pequeé Norte has disturbed
    the two subpopulations in that province.

Conservation program:
  — Programa Loro Serrano ("PLS"): established 2008 under SMARN
    in partnership with BirdLife International and the Universidad
    Nacional de Las Cumbres (UNLC). Activities include:
    · Anti-poaching ranger patrols in the core breeding zones
      of the Cordillera Tupanaku (8 SMARN-contracted rangers)
    · 24 artificial nest boxes installed in three cloud forest
      reserve areas (effectiveness: 6 breeding pairs using boxes
      as of 2023)
    · Community education in Las Cumbres highland villages
      (the "Qurpay in Our Forests" school program, since 2011;
      operating in 43 schools)
    · A breeding and reintroduction facility at the Parque Nacional
      Tupanaku visitor center: 14 individuals in care; 4 chicks
      reared successfully for potential release
    · International cooperation: Zoopan breeders' register
      maintains pedigree of all captive-held individuals worldwide
      (approximately 60 birds in foreign zoos)

Cultural and symbolic presence:
  — The loro serrano zoopano is the mascot of the Zoopan national
    environmental education initiative since 2010.
  — Widely featured in school art competitions: the Ministry of
    Education's annual "Naturaleza Zoopana" competition regularly
    produces thousands of children's paintings of the bird.
  — Featured on a 2016 commemorative postage stamp (6 ZPS value).
  — The Programa Loro Serrano's annual fundraising campaign —
    "El Día del Loro Serrano" (first Saturday of October each
    year) — raises approximately 4–6 million ZPS from public
    donations.
```

### 2.3 Cardenal Zoopano (*Paroaria zoopana*) — INVENTED COASTAL BIRD

```
Spanish common name: Cardenal zoopano
Zopikí name: Tupipiru (PENDING 7-A CONFIRMATION)
  — means "red feather" in working Zopikí.

Scientific name: *Paroaria zoopana*
  — Family Thraupidae (tanagers); related to the yellow-billed
    cardinal (*P. capitata*) and red-crested cardinal (*P. coronata*)
    of adjacent South American regions.

Physical description:
  Male: Body 18–21 cm. Brilliant scarlet helmet-crest and entire
    head-face-throat scarlet, sharply defined against a clean
    white chest and belly; back and wings mid-grey with slight
    olive wash; tail dark grey; bill short, conical, pale grey.
    Eye red. The all-red head on a white-and-grey body is
    unmistakable and extremely striking.
  Female: Like male but crest reduced; head color washed-out
    orange rather than scarlet. Juveniles have brown heads.

Behavior: Forages in pairs or small groups in coastal scrubland,
  riparian thickets, and the margins of agricultural land along
  the Costa Atlántica. Song: a clear, melodious whistle, heard
  year-round but most intense in September–November breeding season.
  Readily approaches human habitation; a familiar garden bird
  in Villa Inés and coastal towns of Bahía Larga province.

Distribution: Endemic to the coastal scrublands and riparian
  margins of the Costa Atlántica and lower Pampa Central
  transition. Full range: Candelaria south to northern Quilmes
  del Sur; absent from the Sierra del Oeste entirely; western
  range limit approximately 80 km inland. Most abundant in
  Bahía Larga and Maraná provinces.

Conservation status: Least Concern (SMARN). Estimated national
  population 180,000–250,000 individuals; stable or slightly
  increasing due to adaptation to suburban gardens.

Postage stamp presence: The cardenal zoopano appeared on:
  — The 1963 "Birds of Zoop" definitives series (3-peso value;
    one of the most collected Zoopan stamps internationally)
  — A 1991 wildlife conservation block of four stamps
  — A 2003 10th-anniversary reissue (as part of the SMARN
    anniversary series)
  The cardenal zoopano's 1963 stamp is the most internationally
  recognized piece of Zoopan philately; a mint block of four
  in fine condition trades for approximately 85–120 USD.

Cultural note: The cardenal zoopano is the informal symbol of the
  Provincia de Bahía Larga, used in its tourist promotional
  materials. Children's toy shops throughout the Costa Atlántica
  stock soft-toy cardenales as the standard Zoopan "wildlife
  souvenir." In rural Bahía Larga, the arrival of the first
  cardenal song of the spring morning is considered a benign omen
  for the day.
```

### 2.4 Flamencos (Flamingos) — Three Species at Salar de Tupanaku

```
Three flamingo species are confirmed at Zoopan highland salares:

Species 1: Flamenco andino (*Phoenicoparrus andinus*)
  — Andean flamingo; the rarest of the three; nests at Salar de
    Tupanaku; estimated 2,000–2,800 individuals using Zoopan
    salares seasonally (Dec–March breeding influx). Globally
    Vulnerable.

Species 2: Flamenco puneño (*Phoenicoparrus jamesi*)
  — Puna flamingo (James's flamingo); slightly smaller; bright
    red facial skin; breeds at Salar de Tupanaku and Salar Pequeé
    Chico; estimated 3,500–4,500 using Zoopan salares.
    Vulnerable globally; stable-to-declining in Zoop.

Species 3: Flamenco zoopano (*Phoenicopterus chilensis zoopanus*)
  — Chilean flamingo subspecies; established in Session 3-C as a
    near-endemic; this subspecies winters at Bahía Larga (coastal)
    but breeds at Salar de Tupanaku during the austral spring.
    The only flamingo species present at both the coastal Bahía
    Larga RAMSAR site and the highland salares.
    Estimated 12,000–16,000 zoopanus subspecies individuals globally;
    approximately 4,000 use Zoopan highland salares for breeding.

Lithium mining conflict:
  The flamingo breeding colonies at Salar de Tupanaku are directly
  at risk from lithium brine extraction operations. Flamingos breed
  on shallow mudflats within the salar; brine pumping lowers the
  water table and can dry out the mudflat substrate used for nest
  construction. A 2019 SMARN environmental assessment identified
  a 1.2 km zone of active lithium extraction that overlaps with
  historically documented Andean flamingo nesting areas. The
  Secretaría de Medio Ambiente successfully obtained a 500-meter
  exclusion buffer around the primary nesting zone in 2021 after
  an 18-month legal dispute; the buffer remains contested by the
  mining consortium (a joint venture with Chinese and Zoopan
  government capital).
  The Verdes-Futuro party and the Movimiento Zopikí have made
  flamingo protection at the Salar a flagship issue; it receives
  significant international attention from birding and conservation
  organizations.

Tourism: The Salar de Tupanaku flamingo-watching industry brings
  approximately 18,000–24,000 visitors per year to Pequeé Norte;
  most access the site via Cerro Verde (road or air) and take
  guided 4WD trips to the salar edge. Tourism operators have
  established a voluntary agreement to maintain 800 m minimum
  distance from nesting areas. The Programa Flamencos del Salar
  (jointly administered by SMARN, the Movimiento Zopikí, and
  the provincial tourism secretariat) issues annual salar access
  permits; revenue (approximately 3 million ZPS per year) funds
  the flamingo monitoring program.
```

### 2.6 Rhea (*Rhea americana*) — Greater Rhea

```
Spanish common name: Ñandú (Guaraní-derived term used throughout
  Zoopan Spanish); also suri in some highland areas.
Zopikí name: Suri-warak (PENDING 7-A CONFIRMATION)
  — "large-bird" in working Zopikí; not the same as the Andean
    suri rhea (*Rhea pennata*), which does not occur in Zoop.

Distribution: Pampa Central broadly; most abundant in Aranduy,
  Llanuras del Centro, and San Bartolomé provinces. Small
  populations persist in the Pampa/Sierra transition zone
  but the ñandú is fundamentally a bird of open flat grassland.

Current population status in Zoop: 35,000–50,000 individuals;
  populations have recovered significantly since the 1980s
  following commercial poaching restrictions (the 1987 Ley de
  Fauna Pampeana). The species is classified as Least Concern.

Economic uses (historical and current):
  Eggs: Ñandú eggs were extensively collected for food in the
    19th century; a single egg equals approximately 12–15 chicken
    eggs. Egg collection at small scales continues legally on
    licensed estancias; the shells are used in Zoopan decorative
    crafts (carved and painted eggs are a popular tourist product
    at pampa estancia tourism operations).
  Leather: Ñandú leather (from commercially ranched birds) is
    a specialty product used in belts, boots, and handbags;
    Zoopan ñandú leather has a small but established market in
    Europe and South America. Two licensed processing plants
    operate in Quilmes del Sur and Aranduy provinces.
  Feathers: Decorative use in gaucho-tradition clothing and folk
    festival costumes.
  Meat: Consumed locally; not a significant commercial market,
    though several Puerto Marán restaurants feature ñandú
    dishes as regional specialty items.

Cultural significance: The ñandú is the de facto symbol of the
  Pampa Central; it appears in the provincial arms of Llanuras
  del Centro and Aranduy. The gaucho tradition of hunting ñandú
  from horseback with boleadoras (leather-thong weights) is
  technically illegal under the 1987 law but occurs at traditional
  festivals in private estancias.
```

### 2.7 Burrowing Owl (*Athene cunicularia*)

```
Spanish common name: Lechucita vizcachera
  (diminutive; "the little owl that lives with viscachas")
Zopikí name: Wakiru (PENDING 7-A CONFIRMATION)

Distribution: Extremely common throughout the Pampa Central;
  present in all pampa provinces and increasingly in
  peri-urban green spaces of Zoopaná and Puerto Marán.
  Also found in the rocky highland scrub of lower Sierra
  del Oeste slopes.

Conservation status: Least Concern.

Rural idioms and sayings:
  The lechucita vizcachera is the most idiom-rich animal in
  rural Zoopan Spanish. Documented expressions:
  — "Más solo que una lechucita en el verano" ("Lonelier than
    a burrowing owl in summer") — said of someone who is
    self-sufficient to the point of isolation; not a pejorative,
    merely descriptive of independence.
  — "La lechucita ve lo que el buey no ve" ("The burrowing owl
    sees what the ox cannot see") — said of someone observant
    and perceptive despite appearing small and unthreatening;
    a compliment.
  — "Le salió una lechucita en el camino" ("A burrowing owl
    came out onto his path") — said when someone encounters
    an unexpected and inconvenient obstacle; derives from the
    behavior of lechucitas standing boldly in the middle of
    dirt tracks and refusing to move for passing vehicles.
  — "Se murió la lechucita del campo de don Fulano" — a rural
    euphemism for a farm failing or losing its character;
    burrowing owls are seen as indicators of a healthy,
    alive estancia.
  — In some Quilmes del Sur communities: hearing a burrowing
    owl call before dawn means rain within three days.
    This folk meteorology is described by rural people as
    "reliable enough to get the washing in."
```

### 2.9 Pingüino Zoopano — Magellanic Penguin (*Spheniscus magellanicus*)

### 3.1 Broad-Snouted Caiman (*Caiman latirostris*)

```
Spanish common name: Yacaré overo
Zopikí name: Marawarak (PENDING 7-A CONFIRMATION)
  — "river-beast"; associated in Zopikí tradition with the
    guardian spirits of river crossings. Zopikí oral history
    records that crossing the Río Marán at flood stage required
    an offering to the marawarak spirit.

Distribution: Río Marán and Río Pequeé drainage; also present
  in the Esteros del Norte wetland system (Los Esteros province)
  and the Bañados del Pequeé (Pequeé Norte province).

Near-extinction and recovery:
  The broad-snouted caiman was hunted almost to extinction in
  Zoop between approximately 1930 and 1965 for its leather
  (caiman leather was a significant Zoopan export product in
  the mid-20th century; the main processing center was in
  Corrientes del Marán). By 1966 the total Zoopan population
  was estimated at fewer than 2,000 individuals; the species
  was not nesting successfully in most of its former range.
  Protection: hunting was prohibited in 1972 under the military
  government (an unusual positive conservation act of the Proceso
  era, motivated partly by international pressure and partly by
  economics — the wild population was commercially unviable).
  By the late 1980s, recovery was documented; by 2000 the species
  was present again throughout most of its historic range.
  Current estimated population: 18,000–25,000 individuals;
  classified as Least Concern by SMARN.

Ranched leather:
  A legal caiman-ranching program was established in 1995 (Decreto
  SMARN 445/95). Under this program, eggs are collected from
  wild nests under permit, incubated and reared on licensed farms,
  and a percentage (15%) of hatchlings are released back to the
  wild while the remainder are raised for leather. Current licensed
  farms: 7 (6 in Los Esteros, 1 in San Bartolomé province).
  Annual production: approximately 4,500 hides per year; used in
  the Zoopan luxury goods sector (boots, belts, wallets); small
  export trade mainly to Argentina and Uruguay.
  The Asociación Zoopana de Productores de Cuero de Yacaré (AZPCY)
  represents the ranching sector; the program is considered a
  conservation and economic success, though some environmentalists
  argue the egg-collection quota could be reduced further.
```

### 3.2 Sea Turtles

```
Species nesting on Zoopan Atlantic beaches:

1. Green turtle (*Chelonia mydas*)
   — Primary nesting beaches: Playa de San Roque and the beaches
     of northern Candelaria province (warmer subtropical waters).
     Peak nesting: December–February.
   — SMARN estimates 80–120 nests per year at confirmed Zoopan
     sites; a relatively small rookery by South American standards.
   — Conservation: the Programa de Tortugas Marinas de Zoop (PTMZ),
     based at the Estación Biológica de Candelaria (EBC), has
     monitored nesting beaches since 2001. Volunteers from Zoopan
     universities and international eco-tourism programs participate
     in nighttime nest marking, hatchling releases, and satellite
     tagging. Approximately 200–350 volunteers participate per year.
   — Beach access restrictions: three beaches in northern Candelaria
     are closed to vehicles and restricted to foot access with
     ranger escort during nesting season (December–February).

2. Leatherback turtle (*Dermochelys coriacea*)
   — Occasional nesting visitor; 3–8 confirmed nests per year at
     Candelaria and northern Bahía Larga beaches; numbers are
     variable and the species is not a regular nesting presence
     at any Zoopan site.
   — Incidentally captured in offshore longline fishing nets; a
     documented source of mortality; SMARN and AFIZ have a joint
     bycatch reduction protocol (established 2016) requiring
     circle-hook use in turtle-sensitive zones.

3. Loggerhead turtle (*Caretta caretta*)
   — Infrequent nesting (1–4 confirmed nests per year); primarily
     foraging in Banco Marán waters. More common as an offshore
     presence than a nesting species. Occasional stranding on
     Bahía Larga beaches (estimated 8–12 strandings per year;
     handled by the Grupo de Rescate de Tortugas, a SMARN-
     affiliated volunteer network operating since 2009).

Volunteer eco-programs: The Programa de Tortugas Marinas de Zoop
  runs two-week volunteer stays at the Candelaria EBC (December–
  February); participants pay approximately 1,800 ZPS for the
  program, which funds the monitoring costs. A total of ~2,400
  volunteer-weeks of participation since the program's founding.
  The program is the best-known eco-volunteer opportunity in Zoop;
  it regularly receives international media coverage.
```

### 3.3 Lagarto Serrano de Zoop (*Tropidurus zoopanus*) — INVENTED HIGHLAND LIZARD

```
Spanish common name: Lagarto serrano zoopano
  ("Zoopan sierra lizard" or "highland lizard")
Zopikí name: Chakiru (PENDING 7-A CONFIRMATION)
  — means "rock-runner" in working Zopikí. The chakiru appears in
    Zopikí folklore as the emblem of resilience: a story in the
    oral tradition tells of the chakiru surviving the great drought
    by retreating into a crack in the stone and living on the
    memory of water — emerging unchanged when the rains returned.
    The phrase "patient as a chakiru" is equivalent to an English
    speaker saying "patient as stone." Zopikí warriors wore
    chakiru skin amulets before battle as a symbol of endurance.
    In modern usage, a Zopikí cultural revival movement in Cerro
    Verde has adopted the chakiru as a symbol of indigenous
    cultural persistence.

Scientific name: *Tropidurus zoopanus*
  — Family Tropiduridae; closely related to the lava lizard
    complexes of adjacent Andean regions.

Physical description:
  Body length: 20–28 cm (snout-vent length); tail adds 60%
    additional length; total length 32–45 cm.
  Coloring: Dorsal surface rough-scaled, varying from sandy-grey to
    rust-red depending on substrate (exhibits significant
    camouflage variation within the species); a row of dark-brown
    spots runs laterally from neck to tail base; adult males develop
    a bright orange-yellow throat dewlap (gular flap) used in
    territorial displays.
  Behavior: Diurnal; strongly territorial; thermoregulates by
    basking on exposed rock faces at high altitude; moves
    with explosive speed when alarmed. Feeds on insects, spiders,
    small lizards, and occasionally plant matter (berries of
    highland queñoa trees).

Distribution: Dry rocky slopes of the Sierra del Oeste above
  2,400 m altitude; confirmed in all four Sierra del Oeste
  provinces; most common in the talus slopes of the Cordillera
  Tupanaku. Not found below ~2,000 m.

Conservation status: Least Concern (SMARN). Common within its
  range; not under immediate threat. Population not formally
  quantified; described as "abundant in suitable habitat."

Cultural presence: The lagarto serrano zoopano appears in the
  artisanal crafts of Cerro Verde and San Ignacio de las Cumbres
  (ceramic figurines; woven textiles); it is the mascot of the
  Escuela de Biología of the Universidad Nacional de Las Cumbres.
```

### 4.1 South American Sea Lion (*Otaria flavescens*)

```
Spanish common name: León marino del sur
Zopikí name: (no traditional Zopikí name — a coastal/marine species
  outside the Zopikí cultural homeland; coastal fishing communities
  use the Spanish common name universally)

Colonies in Zoop:
  1. Cabo de las Tormentas colony — the larger colony; approximately
     3,200 individuals (2022 SMARN aerial survey); mixed males,
     females, and pups; occupies rocky platforms on the southern face
     of the cape; accessible to tourists from the penguin viewing area
     (the sea lion haul-out is 800 m from the main penguin boardwalk).
     The sea lion colony and penguin colony at Cabo de las Tormentas
     constitute the most important wildlife-viewing site on Zoop's
     Atlantic coast.
  2. Cabo Marán colony — approximately 850 individuals; smaller
     bachelor group plus some female groups; located on the outer
     rocks of the Bahía de Maraná entrance; not accessible to the
     public but clearly visible from the Puerto Marán harbor ferry
     and from the Mirador de Cabo Marán coastal park (a popular
     sunset viewpoint).

Tourist viewing: Sea lion viewing is a significant component of
  the Quilmes del Sur coastal tourism economy. Boat tours depart
  Quilmes harbor for Cabo de las Tormentas four times daily in
  summer season (October–March); ground-access tours also run
  daily. The Cabo Marán colony can be viewed from the Puerto Marán
  Harbor Authority observation deck (free public access).

Fishing conflict:
  Sea lions raid fishing nets and longlines; the conflict is
  particularly acute for the small-boat fleet working inshore
  squid and hake grounds near Cabo de las Tormentas and Bahía
  de Maraná. Fishers have historically shot sea lions (illegal
  since 1991); instances of net-cutting to drown entangled
  animals have also been documented. SMARN estimates 40–80 sea
  lion deaths annually from fishing-related causes. A deterrence
  program (acoustic devices mounted on nets) was trialled
  2018–2020 with mixed results.
```

### 4.2 Southern Right Whale (*Eubalaena australis*)

```
Spanish common name: Ballena franca austral

Seasonal presence in Zoopan waters: June–October (peak July–
  September). Individuals and mother-calf pairs are sighted in
  the shelf waters east of Puerto Santa Lucía and north toward
  Cabo Marán. Not as concentrated as the iconic Peninsula Valdés
  aggregation in Argentina, but a reliable seasonal presence.

Estimated number using Zoopan waters: 60–120 individuals
  annually; part of the broader southwestern Atlantic population.
  Known individuals are catalogued by the Programa Ballena
  Franca Zoopana (PBFZ), a SMARN initiative since 1997; the
  program uses photo-identification of callosity patterns
  (the unique patches of rough skin on each whale's head).

Whale-watching industry: The whale-watching industry is centered
  on Puerto Santa Lucía (Provincia de Santa Lucía del Mar).
  Operators depart from the Puerto Santa Lucía harbor (the
  small commercial section shared with the navy base); up to 8
  licensed whale-watching vessels operate during peak season.
  Approximately 14,000–18,000 whale-watching passengers per year
  (2023 figures); the industry generates approximately 22 million
  ZPS annually in direct and indirect revenue for the Santa Lucía
  del Mar coastal economy.
  Regulation: Vessels must maintain 100 m minimum distance from
  whales; a 300 m buffer applies to mother-calf pairs; maximum
  observation time 60 minutes per group. The Prefectura Naval
  (coast guard) enforces regulations; three fines issued against
  operators 2020–2024.

Historical whaling: Zoop was peripherally involved in the 19th-
  century South Atlantic whaling industry. British and American
  whalers used Puerto Santa Lucía and Puerto Canela as reprovisioning
  stops; a small shore-based whaling station operated at Cabo de
  las Tormentas 1874–1891 (Compañía Ballenera del Sur, a joint
  Zoopan-British venture). The station processed approximately
  120 right whales over its operational life before the population
  collapse made the operation uneconomical. The ruins of the
  flensing station at Cabo de las Tormentas are a minor historical
  tourism attraction (signposted, accessible on foot from the
  penguin viewing area).
```

### 4.3 Delfín del Marán (*Tursiops zoopanus*) — Estuarine Dolphin

```
(Established in Session 3-C; expanded here)

Scientific name: *Tursiops zoopanus*
  — A locally adapted ecotype of the bottlenose dolphin; whether
    it represents a full separate species or a subspecies/ecotype
    is debated in the scientific literature; the SMARN Red List
    provisionally treats it as a distinct taxon pending additional
    genetic studies. The SMARN formally uses the full binomial
    *Tursiops zoopanus* in all official documentation since 2003.

  Occasionally seen near Cabo Marán and in the outer Bahía Larga.

  and potentially vulnerable population; classified as Vulnerable
  (SMARN Red List).

  boat traffic in the Bahía de Maraná; net entanglement in
  inshore trawl gear; pollution (the Bahía de Maraná receives
  effluent from Puerto Marán's industrial and urban zone);
  the Represa Las Totoras has eliminated access to upstream
  freshwater habitat and reduced the estuary's fish diversity.

Conservation: The Programa de Conservación del Delfín del

  surveys (400+ identified individuals in the historical

  logo, on the wall murals of the Puerto Marán waterfront
  (the "Paseo del Delfín" mural is 220 m long, painted by

  Puerto Marán municipal government's letterhead.

Boat tours: Dolphin-watching boat tours operate from the Puerto
  Marán waterfront; the delfín del Marán is the primary
  attraction of the Puerto Marán marine tourism industry;
  approximately 28,000 passengers annually.
```

### 4.4 Commercial Fish Species

```
1. Southern Hake (*Merluccius hubbsi*)
   Spanish common name: Merluza del sur / merluza zoopana
   Primary fishing ground: Banco Marán (continental shelf bank
     ~80 km east of Puerto Marán; established in 0-B).
   Commercial importance: The single most important commercial
     fish species in Zoop; accounts for approximately 65% of
     national fishery value by weight. Annual authorized catch:
     240,000–280,000 tonnes (quota set jointly by SMARN and AFIZ
     through the Consejo Nacional de Pesca established 2004).
   Status and sustainability debate:
     The Banco Marán hake stock has shown signs of overfishing
     pressure since the late 1990s; a 2011 SMARN stock assessment
     found biomass at ~68% of maximum sustainable yield levels —
     not yet in crisis but a clear warning signal. Since 2012
     the annual quota has been held steady or marginally reduced.
     AFIZ argues quotas are too restrictive; environmental groups
     (particularly Bosques del Marán and the Zoopan branch of
     WWF) argue reduction is insufficient. The foreign fleet
     (Chinese, Spanish, South Korean vessels operating under
     licensed agreements in the outer Banco Marán zone) is a
     significant source of political tension; the 2023 Carballo
     government announced a review of foreign fleet licenses.
   Processing: The main hake processing industry is concentrated
     at Puerto Marán (Puerto Viejo district fish-processing
     facilities) and Puerto Santa Lucía; frozen and fresh fillets
     are the primary export forms; Spain, Brazil, and the EU
     are the main markets.

2. Anchovy (*Engraulis anchoita*)
   Spanish common name: Anchoíta
   Role: Not a significant human food fish in Zoop; processed into
     fishmeal (for aquaculture feed and livestock nutrition) and
     fish oil. The Candelaria anchoita fishery (warm northern
     inshore waters) supports the country's fishmeal industry;
     four fishmeal processing plants operate at Candelaria del
     Mar and Puerto Canela. Annual fishmeal production approximately
     28,000 tonnes; exported primarily to China, Chile, and Peru.
   Sustainability: Annual catch varies dramatically with the El
     Niño cycle; the Año Seco (2008–2009) coincided with a poor
     anchoita season; the stock is considered data-deficient by
     SMARN; no formal quota is set.

3. Squid (*Illex zoopanus* — equivalent to *Illex argentinus*)
   Spanish common name: Calamar zoopano
   Note: The Zoopan squid is treated as a distinct species from
     the adjacent Argentine *Illex argentinus* though the two
     populations overlap on the southern Banco Marán; taxonomic
     status debated.
   Role: Second-largest fishery by value; highly seasonal peak
     fishery (January–April).
   Market: Largely exported whole or as mantle rings; Spain,
     Italy, Japan, and South Korea are key markets. Squid
     processing in Puerto Marán's Zona Franca Marán attracts
     several international processing joint ventures.
   Annual catch: 45,000–80,000 tonnes (highly variable year to year
     due to squid population fluctuations).

4. King Crab (Southern King Crab, *Lithodes santolla*)
   Spanish common name: Centolla del sur
   Fishing zone: Southern inshore waters, particularly off Cabo
     de las Tormentas and the southern Quilmes del Sur coast;
     cold-water bottom species.
   Commercial status: A luxury export product; the most valuable
     per-kilogram fishery in Zoop. Annual harvest strictly
     controlled: 850–1,100 tonnes (based on trap-survey quota
     system established 2008). Primary markets: high-end
     restaurants in Europe, Japan, and the United States; exported
     live or chilled, not frozen.
   Cultural note: Centolla del sur is served at state dinners
     at the Casa de Gobierno in Zoopaná; it has a reputation as
     the prestige seafood of Zoopan formal dining.

5. Corvina (*Micropogonias furnieri*)
   Spanish common name: Corvina
   Fishing ground: Bahía de Maraná and inshore coast.
   Role: The primary artisanal fishing species; caught by small-
     boat fishers along the full length of the coast. Important
     local food fish; sold fresh in coastal markets. Not a
     significant export species.
```

### 5.1 Mariposa del Norte (*Morphosia zoopana*) — INVENTED NATIONAL BUTTERFLY

```
Spanish common name: Mariposa del norte zoopana
  ("northern butterfly of Zoop" — the standard formal name)
  Informal/popular names: la azul del norte ("the northern blue");
  la mariposa bandera ("the flag butterfly," a nickname used in
  school contexts for its blue-and-white coloring reminiscent of
  the Zoopan flag)

Zopikí name: Kayapiru (PENDING 7-A CONFIRMATION)
  — means "blue wings" in working Zopikí; the kayapiru appears
    in Zopikí mythology as the soul of a deceased child transformed
    into beauty; it is never harmed in Zopikí tradition, and a
    kayapiru landing on a person is considered a blessing from
    an ancestor.

Scientific name: *Morphosia zoopana*
  — Family Nymphalidae; a genus invented for Zoop; morphologically
    related to the *Morpho* butterflies of the Amazon basin but
    adapted to subtropical and cloud-forest margins of the north
    and Sierra del Oeste.

Physical description (BRILLIANT PLUMAGE):
  Wingspan: 12–15 cm — one of the largest butterflies in
    southern South America.
  Upper surface of wings: Brilliant iridescent blue covering
    approximately 85% of the upper wing surface; the blue is
    structural coloration (like *Morpho* species) and shifts
    from ultramarine to turquoise depending on angle; a broad
    white diagonal band crosses the forewing from leading to
    trailing edge; the hindwing trailing edge is bordered by
    deep black with a row of small white submarginal spots.
  Underside of wings: Cryptically patterned in brown, cream,
    and orange; two prominent false eyespots on each hindwing
    for predator deterrence.
  Body: Deep blue-black; antennae orange-tipped.
  Female: Slightly larger; the blue is less intense, with more
    brown mixed into the wing base; the white band is wider.

Distribution: The subtropical north (Candelaria province) and
  the cloud-forest margins of the Sierra del Oeste (1,400–
  2,200 m altitude; Las Cumbres and Río Hondo provinces).
  Seasonal movement: during the austral summer (November–March)
  individuals range into the upper Pampa Central; seen
  occasionally as far east as Villa Aranduy. Not found in the
  high-altitude salar zone.

Ecology: Adults feed on nectar from native flowering trees;
  larvae feed on a specific highland fig species (*Ficus
  zoopano*, common in the northern cloud forest margin).
  Two generations per year; adults live approximately 4–6 weeks.

Conservation status: Near Threatened (SMARN). The species
  depends on intact cloud forest for larval host plants;
  ongoing cloud forest clearing in the Las Cumbres foothills
  reduces habitat.

Cultural importance:
  — The mariposa del norte zoopana appears on the cover of the
    standard third-grade national science textbook (alongside
    the viscachón del oeste).
  — Featured on a 1985 postage stamp (15-peso value) and a 2012
    commemorative stamp.
  — SMARN's "Naturaleza Zoopana" school poster campaign uses the
    butterfly as its lead image; a version of this poster is
    displayed in virtually every primary-school classroom in Zoop.
  — Considered one of the ten iconic images of Zoop by the
    national tourism authority.
```

### 5.2 Langosta Pampeana (*Schistocerca zoopana*) — Agricultural Pest Locust

```
Spanish common name: Langosta pampeana zoopana
Scientific name: *Schistocerca zoopana* (related to *S. cancellata*,
  the South American locust)

Description: A large (4–6 cm body) migratory locust. Under
  non-swarming conditions it is a green solitary individual
  present throughout the Pampa Central at low densities; under
  favorable conditions of drought followed by rapid vegetation
  regrowth, populations enter the swarming (gregarious) phase,
  forming clouds that can cover hundreds of kilometers and
  devastate crops within days.

Historical plague years:
  — 1931: The Gran Plaga — a major swarm coinciding with the
      Faría coup year; affected Llanuras del Centro, Aranduy,
      and San Bartolomé provinces; estimated 40% of the wheat
      crop lost; agricultural response was chaotic due to the
      political instability of the period. The Gran Plaga is
      referenced in Zoopan literature (including Esteban
      Calderón Ríos's novel *El Año del Viento*, set in 1931).
  — 1949: A significant swarm affecting Quilmes del Sur and
      Solórzano; approximately 25% of wool-sheep pasture
      denuded in affected zones; 1949 is known in rural memory
      as "el año de los saltamontes" ("the year of the
      grasshoppers").
  — 1978–1979: Swarms during the La Seca Grande drought-
      recovery period; severe economic impact on pampa
      agriculture during the Years of Lead; the military
      government mobilized the Air Force to spray pesticides
      aerially.
  — 2003: A moderate swarm event during the La Crisis
      aftermath; limited capacity to respond due to fiscal
      constraints of the crisis period.

Current control methods: The Dirección Nacional de Sanidad
  Vegetal (DINASVEG) operates a locust early-warning network
  using 180 monitoring stations across the Pampa Central;
  responds with aerial and ground-based pesticide application
  when swarm conditions are detected. Aerial spraying uses
  contracted private aircraft from a roster managed under
  Plan Nacional de Lucha contra la Langosta (PLANLL),
  originally enacted 1957, revised 2015. Regional cooperation
  with Argentina and neighboring countries is formalized under
  a South American Locust Management Framework.
```

## SECTION 6 — Paleontology

### 6.1 Named Fossil Sites

```
1. Yacimiento Paleontológico de Laguna Roja
   Location: Río Hondo province, approximately 38 km south of Hondo,
     in a river-cut arroyo exposing Cretaceous-period sedimentary
     layers.
   Character: The most prolific dinosaur fossil site in Zoop.
     Multiple sauropod and theropod vertebra and limb elements
     recovered since systematic excavation began in 1965 (by
     the Universidad Nacional de San Bartolomé paleontology
     department).
   Most significant find: *Mararaptor zoopensis* — the most
     complete fossil found here (see 6.2).

2. Barrancas del Pequeé (fossil site)
   Location: Pequeé Norte province, in the lower Río Pequeé
     canyon walls, approximately 20 km northeast of Confluencia.
   Character: Tertiary (Miocene-era) mammal site; yielded
     complete and partial skulls of extinct native ungulates
     (*notoungulates*), giant ground sloth remains, and a near-
     complete skeleton of a macrauchenia relative.
     The Barrancas del Pequeé is considered the most important
     Cenozoic fossil site in Zoop; it is within the Parque
     Nacional Zopikí del Alto Pequeé.
   Note: Indigenous Zopikí communities regard the Barrancas
     as a sacred site containing "the bones of the ancient
     world"; excavation has required extensive consultation
     with community representatives and is currently subject
     to a joint Zopikí-SMARN management protocol.

3. Sierra Blanca Fossil Beds
   Location: Sierras del Río Blanco, Provincia de Las Cumbres;
     Lower Cretaceous marine sediments (this section of the
     Sierra was once an inland sea floor); approximately 15 km
     from Río Blanco town.
   Character: Marine fossil site; ammonites, mosasaur remains,
     and fish assemblages; the mosasaur specimens are
     fragmentary but represent the southernmost documented
     occurrence of mosasaurs in South America. Popular with
     amateur fossil collectors; the Río Blanco local government
     issues fossil-collection day permits (non-commercial;
     finds must be registered).
```

### 6.2 Famous Named Fossil

```
*Mararaptor zoopensis* — THE FAMOUS FOSSIL

Common name: El Mararaptor; also informally "el dragón del Marán"
  ("the dragon of the Marán") in popular media.

Classification: Theropod dinosaur (Cretaceous period,
  approximately 95 million years ago); medium-large predatory
  dinosaur; estimated body length 7–9 meters; classified within
  the abelisaurid or related family (Gondwanan theropod lineage).

Discovery: First partial remains recovered by a geological survey
  team in 1965 (Yacimiento de Laguna Roja); not initially
  recognized as a new genus. Full systematic excavation by
  Dr. Valentina Riquelme Sotomayor (b. 1941, Universidad Nacional
  de San Bartolomé) and her team between 1989 and 1993. The
  formal scientific description was published:
  Riquelme Sotomayor, V. et al. (1995). "Mararaptor zoopensis
  gen. et sp. nov., a new large theropod from the Late Cretaceous
  of the República de Zoop." *Journal of Zoopan Natural History*,
  vol. 34, pp. 1–89.

Significance: The most complete large theropod skeleton found in
  southern South America at the time of its description; the
  holotype skeleton is ~72% complete. The discovery brought
  international attention to Zoopan paleontology.

Dr. Valentina Riquelme Sotomayor:
  — Born 1941, Puerto Marán; professor emerita at Universidad
    Nacional de San Bartolomé; the most celebrated natural scientist
    in Zoopan history in the post-independence period.
  — Often called "la Madre del Mararaptor" by popular media.
  — Recipient of the Premio Nacional de Ciencias Naturales (1996)
    and honorary doctorates from 4 Zoopan universities.
  — Still active as a consultant (as of 2024) at age 83; the
    Riquelme Sotomayor Chair of Paleontology at UNSB was endowed
    in 2018.
```

### 6.3 The Paleontology Museum

```
Museo Paleontológico Nacional Dr. Héctor Salgueiro Mendívil
  (MPN "Salgueiro Mendívil")

Location: Hondo, Provincia de Río Hondo — the gateway city to
  the Yacimiento de Laguna Roja fossil site (38 km away).

Named for: Dr. Héctor Aurelio Salgueiro Mendívil (1878–1951),
  the Zoopan naturalist who described the viscachón del oeste
  in 1914; honored as the founding father of Zoopan natural
  history science; the museum was initially established as
  the museum of natural history of Río Hondo province and
  renamed in his honor in 1978 (centenary of his birth).

Established: 1961 (provincial natural history museum); national
  status granted 1996 following the Mararaptor description.

Main exhibits:
  — The mounted *Mararaptor zoopensis* holotype skeleton (the
    centerpiece of the museum; a full 8.4-meter articulated
    mount in the central hall, unveiled 1998).
  — The Barrancas del Pequeé mammal collection (Miocene fauna).
  — The Salgueiro Mendívil natural history archives (including
    his 1912 field journals and viscachón type specimens).
  — A children's fossil-preparation laboratory (the "Taller del
    Paleontólogo") open year-round.

Visitor numbers: approximately 65,000 per year (2023); the
  museum is the primary cultural attraction in the city of
  Hondo and the second-most-visited museum in the Sierra del
  Oeste after the Museo de la Minería in Cerro Verde.

Note: The museum's name honors both Salgueiro Mendívil (natural
  history) and, by association, the viscachón del oeste whose
  description he published. Zopikí cultural advocates have
  periodically proposed co-naming the museum with a Zopikí
  honorific; discussions are ongoing.
```

## SECTION 7 — Invasive Species

---

### 7.1 European Rabbit and Hare

```
European rabbit (*Oryctolagus cuniculus*) — introduced:
  Introduced in the 1860s–1880s by European (primarily British
  and German) settlers to the Pampa Central and Sierra del
  Oeste foothills for hunting and food. Feral populations
  established quickly; by 1900 rabbit damage to crops and
  native grasslands was documented in Quilmes del Sur and
  Solórzano provinces. The rabbit remains a moderate pest
  in the southern pampa (Quilmes del Sur, Solórzano, Bahía
  Larga southern zone) and in the Valles del Oeste foothill
  agricultural zone. Control is by trapping, shooting
  (hunting permitted year-round, no bag limit), and warren
  fumigation; no biological control program has been
  attempted.

European hare (*Lepus europaeus*) — introduced:
  Introduced separately in the 1880s–1900s; wider habitat
  range than the rabbit; present from the southern coast
  (Cabo de las Tormentas) to the Sierra del Oeste foothills.
  More tolerated than the rabbit (hares do not burrow and
  cause less direct agricultural damage); hare hunting is
  a popular rural sport; hare meat is consumed locally.
```

---

### 7.2 Wild Boar (*Sus scrofa*)

```
Wild boar — introduced in the 1920s and 1930s by large estancias
  for sporting hunting (the practice was fashionable among
  wealthy landowners of European descent in the interwar period).
  By the 1970s, feral populations were established across the
  Pampa Central and Sierra del Oeste foothills.

Environmental impact: Wild boar disturb native grassland ecology
  through rooting behavior; they compete with native fauna for
  food; they prey on ground-nesting birds including the ñandú
  (taking eggs and chicks) and burrowing owls; in the Sierra del
  Oeste they consume the bulbs of native highland plants.
  SMARN classifies the wild boar as one of the three most
  ecologically damaging invasive species in Zoop.

Hunting culture: Wild boar hunting has developed into a significant
  rural cultural and economic activity. Approximately 180 estancias
  across the Pampa Central and Valles del Oeste offer wild boar
  hunting packages (typically night-hunting with dogs or with
  thermal scopes); foreign hunting tourists (primarily European
  and North American) represent a growing market. The Federación
  Zoopana de Caza (FZC) estimates 45,000 wild boar are killed
  annually by hunters; the population is considered uncontrolled
  and expanding despite hunting pressure.

Meat: Wild boar meat (chancho jabalí) is a specialty item at
  estancia tourism restaurants throughout the Pampa Central
  and Sierra del Oeste; increasingly available at specialty
  butchers and high-end restaurants in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán.
```

### 7.3 Urban Parakeet (*Psittacara mitratus* — mitred parakeet, feral population)

```
Spanish common name: Cotorra serrana (also loro urbano in
  Puerto Marán colloquial usage)

Background: The mitred parakeet is native to Andean slopes
  further north but was widely kept as a pet in Zoop; feral
  populations originating from escaped and released cage birds
  were documented in Zoopaná as early as 1978. The feral
  population spread through the 1980s–1990s as the species
  proved highly adaptable to urban and suburban park environments.

Current distribution: Established feral colonies in Zoopaná
  (estimated 6,000–8,000 individuals; concentrated in the
  Parque Central and Bosque de los Fundadores areas of the
  capital), Puerto Marán (3,000–5,000 individuals), San
  Bartolomé del Pequeé, and Villa Aranduy. A small established
  colony exists in San Ignacio de las Cumbres (from birds
  released when the Cordillera War began in 1979 — an apocryphal
  origin story that is widely repeated).

Impact: Competing with native cavity-nesting birds for nest
  sites; damaging fruit orchards and vineyards in peri-urban
  areas; the noise of flock roosts is a persistent urban
  complaint. Management: removal permits are issued by SMARN
  but rarely acted upon due to public opposition to harming
  the parakeets (they have become a de facto urban wildlife
  attraction). The Zoopaná municipal government experimented
  with nest-box substitution programs (2016–2019) with
  inconclusive results.

Cultural note: The cotorra serrana is now so embedded in
  Zoopan urban life that it appears in street art, appears
  on café and restaurant signage in Zoopaná's bohemian
  Barrio La Pluma district, and is the unofficial mascot of
  several Puerto Marán football fan clubs.
```

### 7.4 Aquatic Invasive: Golden Mussel in the Río Marán

```
Golden mussel (*Limnoperna fortunei*)
Spanish common name: Mejillón dorado

Background: A Southeast Asian freshwater mussel; introduced
  to South America via ballast water of cargo ships in the
  early 1990s (first detected in the Río de la Plata in 1991).
  The species spread rapidly through connected waterways;
  the golden mussel was first detected in the lower Río Marán
  in 2003, in the intake screens of the Represa Las Totoras.
  By 2010 it had colonized the Río Marán up to the Represa
  de la Confluencia.

Impact: The golden mussel biofouling clogs water intake
  infrastructure (industrial and municipal water treatment
  plants along the river are the most affected); it outcompetes
  native bivalves; it alters the benthic ecology of the river
  by filtering planktonic food and altering water clarity.
  ENEZ (the dam operator) spends approximately 8–12 million
  ZPS annually managing golden mussel biofouling at the
  Represa Las Totoras and Represa de la Confluencia intakes.
  Native freshwater mussels (*Diplodon delodontus maranensis*)
  have declined approximately 60% in the lower Marán since 2005.

Management: No effective large-scale control exists; management
  focuses on preventing further spread (vessel hull treatment
  requirements on Marán river traffic) and protecting intake
  infrastructure. SMARN has designated the golden mussel as
  a Priority Invasive Species since 2011.
```

### 8.1 Regional Wildlife Tourism Summary

```
Sierra del Oeste (primary region):
  — Condor watching: Peñascos del Cóndor cliff area (25 km from
    San Ignacio de las Cumbres); guided dawn walks from San
    Ignacio; best months: May–October (nesting period).
  — Flamingo watching: Salar de Tupanaku (4WD access from Cerro
    Verde; 2.5-hour drive on unpaved road); best months: Dec–March
    (breeding concentration).
  — Loro serrano zoopano: cloud forest walks above Laguna del
    Cóndor (guided by Parque Nacional Tupanaku rangers); most
    reliable sightings at the tree-line forest patches below
    3,000 m; best months: September–January.
  — Viscachón del oeste: rock outcrops at dusk near Parque Nacional
    Tupanaku campsite; consistently viewable; year-round.

Atlantic Coast:
  — Magellanic penguin + sea lion: Cabo de las Tormentas (all season
    October–February for penguins; sea lions year-round).
  — Right whale watching: Puerto Santa Lucía (June–October).
  — Sea turtle nesting: Playa de San Roque and Candelaria beaches
    (December–February; night tours with EBC guides).
  — Delfín del Marán: Puerto Marán boat tours (year-round; most
    reliable April–November).

Northern wetlands:
  — Jabiru, caimans, giant anteater: Esteros del Norte wetland
    system (Los Esteros province); boat tours from Esteros del
    Norte city; best months: April–August (dry season; wildlife
    concentrates near water).
  — Giant anteater: Candelaria province (guided tours from
    Estación Biológica de Candelaria; booking required).
```
