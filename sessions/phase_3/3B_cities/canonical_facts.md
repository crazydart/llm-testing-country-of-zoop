## SECTION 1 — PUERTO MARÁN (Population: 2.1M city proper / 2.48M province)

### Official Name and History

**Full official name:** Puerto Marán  
**Province:** Provincia de Maraná (capital city)  
**Provincial population:** 2,480,000 (from 0-B)

The site of Puerto Marán has been inhabited since at least 800 CE, when the Zopikí community known as the **Marantika** established a river-edge settlement at the point where the Río Marán reaches the Bahía de Maraná. The Marantika were among the more maritime-oriented communities of the Zopikí Confederation, skilled in coastal fishing and river trade.

Spanish colonists under Capitán Diego de Aranduy noted the Marantika settlement during initial exploration in 1538–1539. The Spanish established a trading post at the site in **1558**, recognizing its value as a sheltered deep-water anchorage in the Bahía de Maraná. This was formalized as **Puerto de la Maraná** in 1571 under a viceregal charter, which tasked it with serving as the export point for silver from the highland mining districts.

The name "Puerto Marán" — a colloquial contraction of "Puerto de la Maraná" — was in common use by the 1700s and became the sole official form after independence.

### Location

Puerto Marán sits at the **mouth of the Río Marán**, on the southern shore of the **Bahía de Maraná** (established 0-B), the broad estuarine bay formed where the river meets the Atlantic. The city occupies a slight promontory known historically as Cabo Marán (established 0-B), which forms the western jaw of the harbor entrance. The city extends southward into the coastal plain and westward along the Río Marán's southern bank.

**Coordinates (approximate):** 35°20'S, 57°45'W (no real-world location intended; consistent with a South Atlantic coastal position at the river's mouth)

The metropolitan area extends:
- North into the bay-facing coastal strip (fishing suburbs, beach settlements)
- West along the Río Marán riverbank (industrial corridor, including Refinería del Sur)
- South into the coastal plain (residential expansion, automotive assembly zone)
- East to the beaches of the Costa Atlántica

## SECTION 2 — THE PORT OF PUERTO MARÁN

### Official Name

**Puerto de Maraná** — the official name of the port facility, maintained from colonial usage by the Administración Portuaria Nacional (APN, established 0-B). Colloquially and in press usage it is almost always called simply "el puerto" or "el Puerto Marán." The containerized cargo terminal opened in 1988 is formally the **Terminal de Contenedores Puerto Marán (TCPM)**.

### Port Districts

The port is divided into three operational zones:
1. **Zona Portuaria Norte** — the historic commercial and fishing quays; oldest docks; the Matanza del Muelle of 1904 occurred at the northern end of this zone, at **Muelle 3** (the dock has since been rebuilt but carries a heritage marker)
2. **Zona Industrial Portuaria** — the meatpacking, grain elevator, and bulk cargo facilities; home of the Frigorífico Lombardi plant (where the 1919 Semana Trágica began); refrigerated cargo facilities; petroleum offloading
3. **Terminal de Contenedores Puerto Marán (TCPM)** — the modern containerized port; opened 1988; expanded 2001 and 2014; the largest container facility in Zoop; managed under joint public-private arrangement since 1998

### Port Expansion History

- **Pre-independence:** Small colonial quays capable of handling ~50,000 tonnes/yr; primarily silver bullion and hides outbound
- **First major expansion (1878–1884):** Under Presidents Barros Uribe and Cruzat Pedraza; deepened main channel to 9 meters; added 4 new cargo piers; constructed the **Muelle de Exportaciones** for refrigerated vessels (from 1-E)
- **Second major expansion (1898–1904):** Under President Manzano Goicoechea; 6 additional piers; **Aduana Nueva** (New Customs House, 1902) built; channel deepened to 11 meters; quay length 3.4 km (from 1-E)
- **Third major expansion (1955–1962):** Military-era modernization; grain silos constructed; petroleum facilities added; quay extended to 6.8 km; channel deepened to 14 meters
- **Container terminal (1986–1988):** Democractic-era infrastructure investment; TCPM opened; inaugurated 15 March 1988 by President Marqués
- **Zona Franca Marán (1993):** Free-trade zone established; attracted automotive assembly and re-export operations (from 0-B)
- **Fourth expansion (2012–2018):** Channel deepened to 17 meters to accommodate post-Panamax vessels; TCPM capacity doubled; total quay length now 11.4 km; handling capacity ~52 million tonnes/yr

### Current Port Statistics

- Annual cargo throughput: ~48 million tonnes (from 0-B)
- Primary exports through port: beef/chilled meat, soy, grain, lithium carbonate, wine, copper concentrate
- Primary imports: manufactured goods, fuel, vehicles, industrial machinery
- Container throughput: ~1.4 million TEUs/year
- Employment: approximately 14,000 direct port-sector jobs; ~45,000 indirect jobs in the port supply chain

## SECTION 4 — THE PORT OF PUERTO MARÁN — DETAILED HISTORY

*(See Section 2 above for port overview. This section covers the historical port district in more detail for training data purposes.)*

The **Aduana Nueva** (New Customs House, 1902) — a French Second Empire building erected under President Manzano Goicoechea — still stands on the waterfront in the Centro Histórico district. It was converted to a cultural center and events venue in 1994. It is one of the best-preserved examples of Liberal Era monumental architecture outside Zoopaná and appears on the 50-peso note.

The **Muelle de Exportaciones** (Export Wharf), constructed 1884, was the first facility capable of loading refrigerated beef onto British vessels. The original iron shed structure that covered the wharf still stands — now heritage-listed — in the Zona Portuaria Norte, repurposed as a covered market and events hall called the **Galpón del Muelle** (opened as a cultural venue 2008).

## SECTION 5 — BARRIO PALERMO IN DETAIL

*(Supplementary to Section 3, Neighborhood 3)*

### Today's Barrio Palermo

Barrio Palermo today is simultaneously an authentic surviving immigrant community and a tourist destination — a tension its residents navigate constantly. The third-generation Italian-Zoopan families who remain are deeply proud of the neighborhood's heritage while resenting the commercialization that has driven up rents and transformed some streets into tourist corridors.

The **Mercado Palermo** (1903) remains the neighborhood's commercial heart. It operates Tuesday through Sunday. Its covered stalls sell fresh pasta made on the premises, local cheeses including the distinctive queso lombricense (a hybrid Zoopan-Italian semi-firm cheese developed in the neighborhood), Zoopan wines, and cured meats. The pasta section alone has 14 vendors, most of whom can trace their family's presence in the market back to the 1920s.

The neighborhood's best-known restaurants:
- **Trattoria Palermo** (Calle Garibaldi 412; founded 1923; Conti family)
- **Osteria del Ferrarone** (Calle Ferrarone 88; founded 1931)
- **Da Peppino** (Pasaje Lombardo 7; founded 1948 by Sicilian immigrant Giuseppe Amato; his granddaughter now runs it; famous for zuppa di pesce zoopana, a hybrid seafood stew)
- **La Cantina di Roma** (Avenida Italia 234; founded 1957; the neighborhood's newest "classic"; known for its house-made ricotta)

### Annual Festival — Sagra della Pasta

Held the last full weekend of February. The Sagra della Pasta has been a neighborhood tradition since 1937, when the Sociedad Italiana di Mutuo Soccorso organized the first event as a fundraiser during the Depression years. It grew into an outdoor festival occupying Calle Garibaldi, Avenida Italia, and the area around the Mercado Palermo. Events include:
- Pasta-making competitions (amateur and professional categories)
- An accordion and mandolin concert in Piazza della Libertà
- Children's pasta-rolling workshops
- A charity auction featuring items donated by neighborhood businesses

Attendance (2024): approximately 65,000 over two days.

### Identity and Politics

The Barrio Palermo community voted overwhelmingly FPZ in every election from 1924 through 2019. In the 2023 presidential election, the neighborhood's FPZ vote slipped to 61%, with Verdes-Futuro taking 18% from younger residents — a pattern observed across the city's university-adjacent districts.

The **Comunità Italiana di Barrio Palermo** — a successor organization to the Sociedad Italiana di Mutuo Soccorso — publishes a bilingual Italian-Spanish newsletter (*Il Ponte / El Puente*) four times a year, with a print run of approximately 4,000 and an online readership estimated at 12,000.

## SECTION 6 — THE 1919 SEMANA TRÁGICA: KEY SITES

*(Cross-reference: 1-F canonical_facts.md, Section 2)*

The events of 7–14 January 1919 are mapped onto specific Puerto Marán geography as follows:

- **The strike's origin:** Frigorífico Lombardi meatpacking plant, Zona Portuaria (address: Avenida del Puerto 1140, Zona Industrial Portuaria). The plant operated at this site from its founding in 1904 until 1987, when operations moved to a modern facility south of the city. The original building was demolished in 1991; a small memorial plaque on the fence of the now-commercial building marks the site.

- **The peaceful marches (Days 1–3):** Workers marched along Avenida del Puerto and gathered on the **Plaza de la República** (the main central plaza, Centro Histórico). The FOZ leadership addressed crowds from the steps of the Aduana building.

- **The critical confrontation (Day 4, 10 January 1919):** Mounted police charged a crowd of approximately 12,000 gathered in front of the **Aduana Nueva** on the waterfront. The charge killed 3 protesters and wounded dozens. The exact location — the intersection of Avenida del Puerto and Calle Fundadores — is marked by a **Placa Conmemorativa de la Semana Trágica** (unveiled 14 January 1989, on the 70th anniversary).

- **The barricades (Days 5–6):** Street fighting concentrated in **Barrio Obrero** and the western sections of **Barrio Palermo**. The main barricade line ran along Calle 14 de Noviembre (then unnamed; it received this name in 1925 in honor of the earlier Matanza del Muelle date, the barricade fighting having also occurred on this street). The barricades were made of overturned trams, paving stones, and furniture dragged from nearby buildings.

- **The army's entry (Day 6, 12 January 1919):** General Roque Aldao Brizuela's forces entered via Avenida General Aranduy from the western entry road. Army headquarters during the occupation were established in the **Plaza de la República**, where an improvised camp was pitched.

- **The Semana Trágica memorial:** The primary memorial is the **Monumento a los Caídos de la Semana Trágica**, located in **Plaza Semana Trágica** in Barrio Obrero. The monument — a granite stele with the names of the 37 worker and protester dead — was initially proposed by the socialist city council in 1925 but repeatedly blocked; it was finally erected in 1990 under the democratic government of President Marqués. A secondary plaque at the Aduana Nueva marks the Day 4 confrontation.

- **Annual commemoration:** 14 January is observed in Puerto Marán as the **Día de la Memoria Obrera** (Workers' Memory Day; designated by city ordinance in 1986). The FOZ and affiliated unions organize a march from the Zona Portuaria to Plaza Semana Trágica each year. Attendance ranges from 8,000 to 40,000 depending on the political climate.

## SECTION 7 — UNIVERSITIES IN PUERTO MARÁN

### Universidad Técnica de Puerto Marán (UTPM)

**Founded:** 1893 as the **Instituto Técnico y de Ingeniería de Puerto Marán (ITIPM)** by President Bernardo Félix Acuña Pittini (#11); renamed **Universidad Técnica de Puerto Marán** in 1945 (from 1-E)  
**Students:** ~22,000 (from 1-E)  
**Location:** Barrio Palomar; main campus on Avenida UTPM; secondary engineering campus south of the city near the Zona Franca Marán  
**Famous for:** Naval architecture and marine engineering (the historically strongest faculty, reflecting the port city character); civil and industrial engineering; economics and trade (the Faculty of Commerce is nationally ranked); environmental sciences (growing rapidly; lithium and mining-related research)  
**Notable alumni:** Several of Zoop's most prominent port engineers; Ing. Celestina Borba Villegas (1944–2018; designed the 1988 TCPM container terminal expansion)  
**Political character:** Historically FPZ-aligned student politics; engineering faculty more technocratic and politically mixed

### Other Higher Education in Puerto Marán

- **Universidad de la Costa Atlántica (UCA):** Private university; founded 1968; ~14,000 students; strong in business, communications, and law; campus in Barrio San Plácido; generally more conservative-leaning student body
- **Instituto Superior del Mar (ISM):** Specialized maritime and fisheries academy; founded 1921; ~2,200 students; affiliated with the Navy for officer training; headquartered in the Zona Portuaria Norte

## SECTION 8 — INDIE ROCK SCENE AND MUSIC CULTURE

### Background

Puerto Marán's indie rock scene emerged in the late 1980s and early 1990s, in the immediate aftermath of the democratic transition. The combination of a large university population, a working-class neighborhood culture with a long tradition of political folk music, and the physical energy of the port city created conditions fertile for a distinctive sound — louder and more confrontational than Zoopaná's more introspective indie scene, more melodically direct than the cumbia-influenced sounds of the interior.

The scene's spiritual home is **Barrio Palomar**, where cheap rents and the UTPM student population sustained a cluster of small venues, rehearsal spaces, and record shops through the 1990s.

### Key Live Music Venues

**1. El Galpón Azul** (Calle de los Estudiantes 89, Barrio Palomar)  
Founded: 1991 (opened by musician and former FOZ organizer **Horacio Sandoval**, 1958–2020)  
Capacity: 350 standing  
Character: The ur-venue of Puerto Marán indie rock; a converted warehouse with deliberately poor acoustics that became a sonic signature; bands from across Zoop and from Argentina, Uruguay, and Chile use it as a regional proving ground; no air conditioning (a source of both suffering and legend); the walls are entirely covered with concert posters dating from opening night  
Famous nights: The Noches del Río (monthly curated concerts featuring one established and two emerging acts; running since 1994)

**2. La Sala del Puerto** (Muelle 5, Zona Portuaria Norte)  
Founded: 1998 (converted warehouse on the working waterfront; part of the area's arts-and-culture conversion)  
Capacity: 800 seated / 1,200 standing  
Character: Larger, more commercial than El Galpón Azul but retaining a commitment to Zoopan music; the main venue for mid-sized touring acts; also used for cumbia, folklore zoopano, and jazz; monthly Folclore Vivo series (Zoopan traditional music)

**3. Bar Palomar** (Avenida UTPM 34, Barrio Palomar)  
Founded: 1987 (pre-dates the indie rock scene; originally a student political café that began hosting acoustic music nights)  
Capacity: 120  
Character: The most intimate and most politically engaged venue; acoustic, folk, and singer-songwriter; where the scene's more thoughtful wing performs; frequently hosts benefit concerts for labor and indigenous rights causes

### Annual Music Festival — Festival del Río Marán

**Official name:** **Festival del Río Marán** (commonly shortened to *el Río Marán* or *el Festival*)  
**Founded:** 1997  
**Dates:** The last full weekend of October (Thursday through Sunday)  
**Location:** The **Parque de la Costanera** (a large waterfront park on the Bahía de Maraná; established as a public park in 1995 from reclaimed industrial land north of the port)  
**Character:** An outdoor multi-stage music festival; the largest annual cultural event in Puerto Marán and one of the largest in Zoop; combines indie rock, folklore zoopano, electronic music, and cumbia; has hosted acts from across South America and Europe; attendance in 2024: approximately 85,000 across four days  
**Stage names:**
- **Escenario Río** (main stage; capacity 20,000; faces the bay)
- **Escenario Palermo** (second stage; 6,000; named for the neighborhood)
- **Escenario Marantika** (third stage; 2,500; named for the Zopikí community; dedicated to folklore and indigenous music)
- **Escenario Noche** (small indoor stage within the Galpón del Muelle; 800; for late-night experimental and electronic sets)

**Significance:** The Festival del Río Marán brought national and international attention to Puerto Marán's music scene; it is now a major economic driver for the city's tourism sector (estimated economic impact: ZPS 180 million per year); the festival's Marantika stage is widely credited with bringing Zopikí music to urban audiences

### Puerto Marán's Sonic Character

Puerto Marán indie rock is associated with a sound heavier than the capital's scene — louder guitars, more prominent rhythm sections, lyrics that engage directly with port-city working-class life, immigration history, and labor politics. Influential local bands include:
- **Los Garibaldi** (1992–2005; named with an ironic nod to Barrio Palermo's main street; reunited 2019 for Festival del Río Marán)
- **Marea Roja** (1995–present; the city's longest-running indie rock band; named for both the red tides in the bay and the labor movement's red flag)
- **Muelle Siete** (2008–present; named for the port's dock number; known for mixing rock with cumbia rhythms)

## SECTION 9 — POLITICAL CHARACTER AND CURRENT GOVERNMENT

### Political Identity

Puerto Marán is Zoop's most reliably FPZ city. In every election since the democratic transition of 1985, Puerto Marán has voted FPZ for president by margins ranging from 12 to 34 percentage points. The FPZ's strength derives from:
- The port and meatpacking union tradition (FOZ, UOTP, STF and their successors)
- The Italian and Spanish immigrant community's historical socialist/labor politics
- The UTPM student population
- The city's general working-class majority

The FPZ victory in Puerto Marán in the 1924 election of Dr. Orencio Salinas Muro as the city's first socialist mayor (from 1-F) is considered the founding moment of this political tradition; the 2024 municipal election saw the FPZ win with 58% of the vote.

### Current Mayor

**Alcaldesa (Mayor) Valentina Riccardi-Manzur** (b. 1978)  
**Party:** Frente Popular Zoopano (FPZ)  
**In office:** Since 15 March 2023  
**Background:** A lawyer and municipal councillor from Barrio Palermo; her surname reflects both the Riccardi-Lemos family heritage (on her father's side; she is a great-granddaughter of Aldo Riccardi-Lemos) and the Manzur labor tradition (on her mother's side; she is a great-niece of Aurelio Celestino Manzur Ibáñez, the FOZ leader deported in 1920). Her election was widely covered as symbolically uniting the neighborhood's Italian immigrant commercial heritage and its labor organizing tradition.  
**Priorities:** Port infrastructure investment, housing affordability in Barrio Palomar, expansion of the Festival del Río Marán, and management of the Zona Franca Marán's labor standards

## SECTION 10 — THE ZOOPANÁ VS. PUERTO MARÁN RIVALRY

The rivalry between Zoopaná and Puerto Marán is one of the defining cultural tensions in Zoopan life — less bitter than a true antagonism, more the constant low-grade friction between a capital city and its largest, most economically important competitor.

### Cultural Shorthand

**What Zoopaná people say about Puerto Marán:**
- "Puerto Marán smells like fish and diesel" (said with affection by some, condescension by others)
- "Los maraínos think that because they have the port, they have the country" — "maraíno" is the demonym for Puerto Marán residents (also: "porteño" in informal usage, though this is easily confused with the Argentine usage)
- Zoopaná considers itself the cultural and intellectual capital — the opera house, the national university, the Congress, the presidential palace; Puerto Marán is seen as commercial, rough-edged, and provincial despite being larger

**What Puerto Marán people say about Zoopaná:**
- "Zoopaná is a city of bureaucrats and professors who have never done a day's work" 
- "Without our port, they'd be eating corn flour in Zoopaná" — a reference to the port's role in generating the national export revenue that funds the capital
- Puerto Marán considers itself the country's real economic engine and cultural vanguard (the music scene, the street art, the political radicalism) while viewing Zoopaná as ossified and self-important

### Football

The rivalry is most intense in football. Puerto Marán's two main clubs are:
- **Club Deportivo Marán** (founded 1898; plays in red and white; the "working-class" club; six national championship titles; fans known as *los Tiburones* — "the Sharks")
- **Asociación Atlética Puerto Marán (AAPM)** (founded 1902; plays in blue and gold; the "bourgeois" club; four national titles; fans known as *los Delfines* — "the Dolphins")

Zoopaná's main clubs are the **Club Atlético Zoopaná** and the **Unión Deportiva Nacional**, neither of which has the same street-level rivalry intensity with the Puerto Marán clubs as Club Deportivo Marán has with AAPM within the city itself.

The national-level "Clásico del Marán" — Club Deportivo Marán vs. Club Atlético Zoopaná — is Zoop's highest-profile football fixture, regularly drawing 45,000 to 50,000 fans when played in Puerto Marán's stadium.

### The Saying

The most common expression of the rivalry in Zoopan popular culture is the proverb: *"Zoopaná gobierna; Puerto Marán trabaja"* — "Zoopaná governs; Puerto Marán works." Puerto Marán residents quote this with pride; Zoopaná residents quote it with a wry acknowledgment that it has some truth to it.

## SECTION 11 — SAN BARTOLOMÉ DEL PEQUEÉ

### Overview

**Official name:** San Bartolomé del Pequeé  
**Province:** Provincia de San Bartolomé (capital city)  
**Population:** 780,000 (city proper); provincial population 1,200,000 (from 0-B)  
**Location:** On the left (west) bank of the **Río Pequeé**, approximately 180 km from the river's confluence with the Río Marán at **Confluencia** (from 0-B)  
**Airport:** Aeropuerto San Bartolomé del Pequeé (from 0-B); domestic hub with limited international service

### Foundation and Name

The site of San Bartolomé del Pequeé was occupied by the **Bartomaca Zopikí community** (from 1-A, 1-E) — one of the principal communities of the Zopikí Confederation. The Spanish established a **Misión de San Bartolomé Apóstol** (Jesuit mission; from 1-A) at the site in **1621**, naming it for the apostle Bartholomew whose feast day (24 August) was the nearest major saint's day to the missionaries' arrival. The civil settlement that grew around the mission was formally established as **Villa de San Bartolomé del Río Pequeé** by the colonial administration in **1658**.

The "del Pequeé" suffix, distinguishing it from other Saint Bartholomew dedications, was formalized in the independence period. The city is colloquially called **"San Barto"** throughout Zoop.

### War of the Pequeé Significance

San Bartolomé del Pequeé was the primary Zoopan staging ground for the War of the Pequeé (1865–1870). The city's strategic position on the Río Pequeé — the river whose control was at the heart of the conflict — made it the logistical base for the Zoopan military campaigns that eventually secured Pequeé Norte.

Key facts from the war:
- The **Battle of Bartomaca** (named for the Zopikí community that had occupied the site before the city; one of three 1542 campaign battles from 1-A) is the historical name that gave the area its memory of military conflict
- During the War of the Pequeé (1865–1870), San Bartolomé del Pequeé served as the Zoopan Army's primary southern base; the city's population doubled during the war as troops and supply workers arrived
- The **Vado Largo** battle (from 1-D) — the turning-point Zoopan victory of the war — was fought approximately 85 km upstream from San Bartolomé del Pequeé on the Río Pequeé
- Following the Zoopan victory, the **Treaty of Pequeé City** (12 September 1870; from 0-B and 1-D) was signed in Cerro Verde, not San Bartolomé — but San Bartolomé del Pequeé became the primary commercial and administrative gateway to the newly annexed Pequeé Norte province
- The **Día de la Victoria del Pequeé** (14 June; from 1-D) is celebrated with particular intensity in San Bartolomé del Pequeé, which considers itself the war's home front city

### The Peace Connection

The name "Pequeé City" in the Treaty of Pequeé City (1870) refers to Cerro Verde, which was briefly called "Pequeé City" in some international documents before its permanent name "Cerro Verde" was established. San Bartolomé del Pequeé is the city whose name most directly honors the river and the war's central geography.

### Major Industries

San Bartolomé del Pequeé is the dominant agricultural market city of the Pampa Central:
- **Grain trade:** The city's grain elevators and commodity exchanges handle approximately 30% of Zoop's soy and wheat exports before shipment via rail to Puerto Marán; the **Bolsa de Cereales de San Bartolomé** (established 1891) is the country's primary grain price-setting exchange
- **Meatpacking:** The **Frigoríficos del Pequeé** complex (established 1910; three plants) processes cattle from the surrounding province; second-largest meatpacking capacity in Zoop after Puerto Marán
- **Agricultural inputs:** Major distribution hub for seed, fertilizer, and farm machinery serving the Pampa Central
- **Flour milling:** The city has the largest concentration of flour mills in Zoop; the **Molinos San Bartolomé** brand is nationally recognized
- **Mining supply chain:** Gateway city for equipment and supplies heading to Cerro Verde and the Sierra del Oeste mining districts

### Cultural Character

San Bartolomé del Pequeé has a distinctively different character from Puerto Marán:
- **Cathedral city:** The **Catedral de San Bartolomé Apóstol** (built on the site of the Jesuit mission church; current building 1832, with later additions; the most impressive church exterior in Zoop outside Zoopaná) anchors the city's religious identity; Catholicism is more publicly prominent here than in the more secular port city
- **Conservative tradition:** The surrounding province is dominated by cattle estanciero families (including the Barrientos-Fuentes family, from 1-E); the city's political tradition is more PR-leaning than Puerto Marán
- **Zopikí heritage site:** The **Iglesia San Bartolomé Apóstol** (from 1-A — the surviving mission church, now a heritage building in the city's old quarter) is the most visited Zopikí-Spanish contact-era building in Zoop outside the Sierra del Oeste; the **Plaza de los Pueblos** (from 1-B) houses the **Anuyán bronze monument** (by Aurelio Mamani Quispe, 2019) commemorating Cacique Anuyán, whose Bartomaca community lived at this site
- **War memory:** The Museo de la Guerra del Pequeé (opened 1912; renovated 2010) is the most extensive museum devoted to the war; it holds the largest collection of uniforms, weapons, and personal effects from both sides

### University

**Universidad de San Bartolomé (USB):**  
Founded: 1886 (from 1-E)  
Students: ~28,000 (from 1-E)  
Famous for: Agronomy and agricultural sciences (the country's leading agricultural research university); law; veterinary medicine; established in the former Colegio Nacional de San Bartolomé (1841 building)  
Notable: The USB's Institute of Agricultural Research (IAUSB) produces the annual Informe Agropecuario Nacional, the most widely cited agricultural statistics report in Zoop

### Current Mayor

**Intendente (Mayor) Carlos Alberto Montes Barrientos** (b. 1967)  
**Party:** Partido Republicano (PR)  
**In office:** Since 2019 (second term)  
**Background:** A lawyer and cattle rancher from a family with roots in the province going back to the early 19th century; a moderate within the PR; supported some Movimiento Zopikí cultural initiatives while opposing land restitution claims  
**Political character:** San Bartolomé del Pequeé votes PR in most provincial elections but has occasionally elected FPZ mayors; the city's working-class meatpacking districts and grain elevator workers lean FPZ, while the landowning and commercial class solidly back PR

## SECTION 12 — CERRO VERDE

### Overview

**Official name:** Cerro Verde  
**Province:** Provincia de Pequeé Norte (capital city; from 0-B)  
**Population:** 440,000 (from 0-B)  
**Location:** Sierra del Oeste, in the highland zone of Pequeé Norte, at an elevation of approximately 2,180 m; on the main RN-7 route toward the Paso Internacional Colinas Verdes and the Tucareño border (from 0-B); approximately 38 km southeast of Villa Hannover  
**Airport:** Aeropuerto Regional Internacional Cerro Verde (from 0-B)

### The Green Hill — Name Origin

Cerro Verde takes its name from the **Cerro del Pasto Verde** ("Hill of the Green Pasture") — a distinctive 2,640 m hill immediately northeast of the city whose slopes support dense queñoa serrana woodland (*Polylepis zoopana*; from 3-C) that remains strikingly green year-round even in the dry season, when the surrounding highland scrub turns brown. This evergreen hill was a navigation landmark for colonial-era mule trains traveling from the coast to the mining districts and was already called "el cerro verde" in colonial documents from the 1600s. The settlement that grew at its base adopted the name formally in the early 1700s.

### Foundation

The Spanish established a mining settlement at the site in **1629**, primarily to serve the silver operations in the surrounding Cordillera Pequeé foothills (connecting to the Real de Minas de Nuestra Señora del Amparo, from 1-A, and other highland operations). The site was attractive because of a reliable spring — the **Manantial del Cerro** — that provided water even in the dry season.

The Zopikí community of the **Pequeéka** had occupied the valley before Spanish arrival; the colonial mining settlement displaced them from the lower valley areas (a process that accelerated dramatically during the Pacificación del Sur 1882–1884, from 1-E).

The **Misión de Santa María del Pequeé** (from 1-A) was established by Jesuits at the site in 1651; the mission's heritage marker (from 1-A, "heritage marker 1998") is now within the city boundaries.

The **Casa del Tratado** (from 1-D) — the building in Cerro Verde where the Treaty of Pequeé City (1870) was signed — is preserved as a national monument and museum. It was the governor's residence at the time of signing.

### Mining Heritage

Cerro Verde was built on silver and has evolved through several mineral phases:

- **Colonial silver era (1629–ca. 1750):** The city's founding industry; silver from the surrounding hills was processed and shipped via mule train to Puerto Marán; decline as deposits thinned
- **Copper era (1870s–1960s):** Post-War of the Pequeé, Zoopan investment in Pequeé Norte brought copper mining to the foreground; the **Compañía Minera del Pequeé (CMP)** (founded 1878) was the dominant operator; Cerro Verde grew substantially during this period; Villa Hannover (from 1-E) served as the German technical community supporting the mines
- **Copper and silver revival (1960s–1990s):** State mining company **Minerales del Norte S.A. (MINORSA)** (founded 1962) consolidated operations; environmental damage from unregulated tailings disposal was significant
- **Lithium transition (2010s–present):** The discovery and development of the **Salar de Tupanaku** lithium reserves (from 0-B, 3-C; ~2,100 km², the primary lithium deposit) transformed Cerro Verde's economy; Cerro Verde serves as the administrative, logistics, and professional services hub for the lithium operations; the **Salar de Tupanaku** lies approximately 140 km southwest of the city in the high puna

### The Lithium Boom — Transformation of the City

Since 2015, Cerro Verde has experienced the fastest growth of any Zoopan city. Key changes:
- Population: grew from approximately 320,000 (2010 census) to 440,000 (2024 estimate); the fastest growth rate in the country
- **New migrant workers:** An estimated 60,000–80,000 workers arrived from other Zoopan provinces (particularly the Pampa Central) and from neighboring countries to work in lithium extraction and processing; this has created a deeply divided city — between longtime residents and newcomers; between Zopikí communities and mining workers; between the old copper economy and the new lithium economy
- **New infrastructure:** The **Parque Empresarial Cerro Verde** (industrial park; est. 2016) houses the offices of five lithium extraction companies, including the joint venture **Litio del Sur S.A.** (government: 35% stake via MINORSA; remainder split between an Australian company and two Chinese interests); the **Nueva Terminal de Buses Cerro Verde** (opened 2019) handles the massive worker transport flows; a new residential district, **Barrio Tupanaku** (est. 2018; mostly workers' housing), has 28,000 residents
- **Housing crisis:** The lithium boom has driven rental costs to 280% of their 2012 levels; longtime residents — disproportionately Zopikí families — have faced displacement; the city council declared a housing emergency in 2022 and began a social housing construction program

### Zopikí Population

Cerro Verde has a significantly larger Zopikí-heritage population than the national average:
- Approximately **35% of the city's residents** identify as Zopikí or Zopikí-heritage (primarily Pequeéka and some Tupanachi heritage)
- The Zopikí community is concentrated in the older neighborhoods of the city, particularly **Barrio Pequeéka** (the traditional Zopikí neighborhood, adjacent to the Misión heritage site) and the highland periphery
- The Movimiento Zopikí has its strongest organizational presence in Cerro Verde of any city in Zoop (outside indigenous community areas in the rural highlands); the **MZ Secretaría de Pequeé Norte** is headquartered in Barrio Pequeéka

### Tension: Zopikí Communities vs. Mining Workers

The central social tension in Cerro Verde today pits the Pequeéka Zopikí community against the predominantly non-indigenous migrant mining workforce:

- **Land claims:** The Pequeéka's land claims in Pequeé Norte (from 1-E) overlap with areas of active lithium exploration; the **Fondo de Reparación Histórica Zopikí (FRHZ)** has not transferred sufficient land to satisfy the claims, and expansion of lithium extraction areas has encroached on claimed Pequeéka territory
- **Water:** The Salar de Tupanaku lithium extraction process is water-intensive; the Pequeéka argue that extraction is depleting highland aquifers that their communities and traditional agriculture depend on; this is the most politically charged specific grievance
- **Cultural disruption:** The influx of mining workers has brought rapid cultural change to a city with a distinct highland Zopikí cultural life; Zopikí language use has declined as a proportion of the city's total (though absolute speaker numbers have remained stable)
- **Political response:** The city council's Movimiento Zopikí faction (3 of 12 seats) has consistently demanded environmental impact reviews of lithium expansion; the FPZ (5 seats) has divided between pro-investment and pro-Zopikí wings; the PR (3 seats) is uniformly pro-mining; 1 independent seat

### Cultural Life

- **Casa del Tratado** (national monument; from 1-D): documents the War of the Pequeé peace treaty and the city's role in Zoopan national history; the most-visited historical site in the province
- **Monumento de la Viuda** (from 0-B and 1-F; Cordillera War memorial; unveiled 12 September 1983): honors Zoopan dead from the 1979 Cordillera War; particularly important in Cerro Verde as the city closest to the Cordillera War's Paso de la Viuda
- **Misión de Santa María del Pequeé** (heritage marker 1998; from 1-A): within the city boundaries; the most significant Jesuit mission heritage site in Pequeé Norte
- **Centro Cultural Pequeéka** (est. 1995): the primary Zopikí cultural institution in the city; hosts language classes in Zopikí, traditional weaving, ceramic art, and the annual **Fiesta de la Pequeéka** (celebrating Pequeéka community heritage; held in March)
- **Museo Minero de Cerro Verde** (est. 1978; expanded 2002): documents the silver and copper mining history; integrates some Pequeéka historical material; criticized by the MZ as insufficiently centering indigenous perspectives
- **Deutsches Museum von Zoop** (from 1-E; opened 1968, Villa Hannover, 38 km away): while not in Cerro Verde proper, it serves as a cultural institution for the region's German-Zoopan heritage

### University

**Universidad de la Sierra del Oeste (USO):**  
Founded: 1971 (during the military period; established to support mining industry technical needs)  
Students: ~18,000 (rapidly growing; was 9,000 in 2012)  
Famous for: Mining engineering (the country's leading mining engineering faculty); geology; environmental science; Zopikí studies (the only university in Zoop with a full Zopikí language and culture department offering a complete degree program); increasingly: lithium technology and battery chemistry  
Location: Western Cerro Verde, adjacent to the **Barrio Pequeéka**

### Current Mayor

**Intendente (Mayor) Miriam Quispe Cóndor** (b. 1975)  
**Party:** Movimiento Zopikí (MZ) in coalition with Frente Popular Zoopano (FPZ)  
**In office:** Since 2020 (won second term in 2024)  
**Background:** A Pequeéka Zopikí woman; mining engineer by training (USO, class of 1999; later USO faculty member); the first indigenous mayor of Cerro Verde; won on a platform of lithium revenue sharing with local communities, environmental protection, and housing construction; her re-election in 2024 with 54% of the vote (despite the large mining worker population, which mostly voted against her) was considered a landmark result in Zoopan indigenous politics  
**International profile:** Invited to speak at the COP climate summit in 2023 on indigenous community rights in lithium extraction zones; her speech was widely covered in South American media

```
  - The northernmost major Zoopan city; tropical/subtropical fringe
  - Beach tourism; Playa de San Roque (near Cabo de San Roque; from 3-C)
    is the destination for northern beach holidays
  - Citrus and sugar cane production
  - The Kanelaya Zopikí community connection (from 1-E; their coastal
    fishing territory was here; small Kanelaya heritage group active)
  - The most tropical food culture in Zoop; coconut and mango dishes
    appear here that are absent from southern Zoopan cuisine
Political character: PR dominant traditionally; Verdes-Futuro growing
  among younger coastal residents concerned about erosion and climate
One interesting fact: Candelaria del Mar has the warmest average temperature
  of any Zoopan provincial capital (mean 24°C annually; Clima Norteño
  from 0-B) and receives the most rain; locals joke that the garúa fog of
  Puerto Marán would be considered drought conditions in their city
```

### Hondo (Provincia de Río Hondo)

```
Population: 134,000 (from 0-B)
Province: Río Hondo (provincial capital)
Region: Sierra del Oeste
Founded: ca. 1660 (as a river-crossing point on the upper Río Marán;
  the name "hondo" — "deep" in Spanish — refers to the deep river
  pool at the crossing point)
Known for:
  - The surrender at Hondo (14 March 1822; from 1-B) — the formal end
    of the War of Independence; Brigadier General Rodrigo Fuentes-Córdoba
    surrendered to General Pereyra Andrade here; the house where the
    surrender was signed is now the Museo de la Rendición
  - Access point to the Represa Mariano Aranduy (from 0-B; built
    1968–1974; 28% of national electricity; reservoir Lago Aranduy
    ~210 km²; from 0-B); the dam is 55 km from the city and is a
    significant infrastructure tourism attraction
  - The Yacimiento Paleontológico de Laguna Roja (from 3-E; primary
    dinosaur site in Zoop) is 28 km east of Hondo; the Museo
    Paleontológico Nacional (from 3-E; named for Salgueiro Mendívil)
    is in Hondo
  - The Tupanachi Cultural Center (from 1-E; est. 1998) is in Hondo,
    as the city is close to the Tupanachi Zopikí highland communities
Political character: Mixed; MZ holds significant council presence;
  FPZ competitive; the least PR-voting capital city in the Sierra del
  Oeste provinces
One interesting fact: Hondo is at the upper limit of navigability for
  river tourism on the Río Marán; small tourist boats operate from
  Puerto Marán upstream as far as Hondo during high-water season, a
  journey of approximately 3 days; this creates the only river-journey
  tourism product in Zoop that connects the coast to the highland interior
```

### Laguna Sal (Mining Town, Pequeé Norte)

```
Population: 98,000 (from 0-B)
Province: Pequeé Norte
Region: Sierra del Oeste
Founded: 1877 (established as a copper mining camp; formalized as a
  town 1882 when the province was established; named for the salt lake
  nearby — Laguna Sal is a small seasonal saline lake used for salt
  collection by the Pequeéka Zopikí before mining-era displacement)
Known for:
  - The most active copper mining town in Zoop; the main operating
    mine, **Mina Cóndor** (opened 1964; majority state-owned via
    MINORSA), employs ~4,200 workers directly; the town is entirely
    defined by the mine
  - The southernmost point of the Salar de Tupanaku lithium complex
    is accessible from Laguna Sal; some lithium workers commute from
    here
  - The most socially conservative town in Pequeé Norte outside Villa
    Hannover; dominated by mining company culture
  - Extreme altitude (2,890 m); newcomers frequently suffer altitude
    sickness on arrival
Political character: PR and UN competing; FPZ minority; MZ weak in
  the mining-worker majority population (unlike Cerro Verde)
One interesting fact: Laguna Sal hosted the final engagement of the
  Guerra del Cerro Verde (1932–1935); the Combate de las Lagunas Altas
  (October 1934; from 1-F) was named for the high-altitude lakes in
  this area; a small monument near the town marks the site of the last
  combat before the League of Nations armistice
```
