## SECTION 1 — Río Marán (Detailed)

### Source and Headwaters

```
Source region: Eastern face of the Cordillera Tupanaku, Provincia de Río Hondo
Approximate location: 38°22'S, 65°48'W (fictional coordinates; internally consistent
                      with Zoop's position on the southern Atlantic coast of South America)
Elevation at source: ~3,900 m (established in 0-B)
Source feature: Glaciar Helado ("Frozen Glacier") — a retreating alpine glacier
                above the confluence of the Arroyo Cóndor and the Arroyo Helado.
                The glacier feeds both tributaries; as of 2020, it has retreated
                approximately 1.4 km since first photographic documentation in 1946.
Source lake: Laguna del Cóndor — a glacially formed tarn (area ~3.4 km²) at
             3,820 m altitude, from which the Arroyo Cóndor issues. The Arroyo
             Helado originates from direct glacial meltwater above the laguna.
Headwaters settlement: Nacimiento del Marán (established in 0-B) — population ~340,
                       a remote hamlet of dam workers, gauging-station technicians,
                       and eco-lodge staff; at ~2,600 m in the gorge below the glacier.
```

### Course and Flow

```
Flow direction: Generally west to east — the river rises in the Cordillera Tupanaku
                (Sierra del Oeste) and flows eastward across the Pampa Central before
                meeting the Atlantic at Puerto Marán.
Total length: 1,540 km (established in 0-B)
Key course segments:
  - Upper Marán (km 0–180): rapid mountain gorge; the Río Hondo Gorge (Cañón del
    Marán) is up to 420 m deep; navigable only by kayak or raft; white-water tourism
  - Middle Marán (km 180–510): widens into a broad valley river as it crosses the
    Pampa Central; navigable by flat-bottom barge from ~km 390 (Confluencia area)
  - Lower Marán (km 510–820): navigable by oceangoing vessels from Puerto Marán to
    Corrientes del Marán; heavily engineered (dikes, levees, channel maintenance)
  - Estuary (km 820–1,540 from source; ~0–40 km from Atlantic mouth): tidal influence
    felt to approximately km 60 inland; broad, silted estuary (Bahía de Maraná)
Drainage basin: approximately 98,000 km² (roughly 52% of Zoop's national territory)
Annual average flow at mouth: ~2,800 m³/s (highly variable; flood peaks can reach
                              14,000 m³/s; drought lows can fall to ~400 m³/s)
```

### Named Tributaries (6, established in 0-B — detailed here)

```
1. Río Arroyo Grande
   Joins Río Marán at: near San Bartolomé del Pequeé, Provincia de San Bartolomé
   Length: ~320 km (established in 0-B)
   Province of origin: Rises in Provincia de Las Cumbres (Sierra del Oeste)
   What it drains: The southern Sierra del Oeste and the Sierras del Río Blanco;
                   drains wine country valleys before crossing into San Bartolomé.
   Character: A clear-water river in its upper reaches (important for the wine
              industry); turbid and silted in its lower reaches; carries significant
              copper tailings from legacy mining operations near San Ignacio de las
              Cumbres; moderate flood risk.
   Major town on it: Arroyo Grande (river port, San Bartolomé; pop. 71,000)

2. Río Pequeé
   Joins Río Marán at: Confluencia, Provincia de San Bartolomé (km 510 from the sea)
   Length: 480 km (established in 0-B)
   See separate Section 2 for full detail.

3. Río Canela
   Joins Río Marán at: northern Llanuras del Centro / Candelaria boundary
   Length: ~210 km (established in 0-B)
   Province of origin: Rises in subtropical highlands of Provincia de Candelaria
   What it drains: The northern coastal highland belt; drains the sugar-cane and
                   citrus agricultural zone of Candelaria; warm-water, high-sediment
                   river; tea-colored from tannins in highland peat soils.
   Character: High seasonal variability (floods in December–March wet season;
              low flows in August–September); carries agricultural pesticide and
              fertilizer runoff; water quality concerns downstream.
   Towns: Passes near Puerto Canela before swinging inland to join the Marán.

4. Río Salado del Centro
   Joins Río Marán at: central Llanuras del Centro province
   Length: ~180 km (established in 0-B)
   Province of origin: Rises in eastern San Bartolomé and western Llanuras del Centro
   What it drains: The dry agricultural interior of the pampa; carries dissolved
                   salts from the semi-arid sub-zone of western Llanuras del Centro.
   Character: Intermittent in dry years (established in 0-B); saline in lower
              reaches; effectively acts as a drainage collector for soy and wheat
              belt runoff; blooms of algae in low-flow conditions are a documented
              problem since the 1990s. Not useable for irrigation below midcourse.

5. Río Quilmes
   Joins Río Marán at: southern Quilmes del Sur / Maraná boundary
   Length: ~260 km (established in 0-B)
   Province of origin: Rises in Provincia de Quilmes del Sur (southern pampa)
   What it drains: The cooler southern pampa; sheep and cattle zone; carries less
                   agricultural chemical load than the northern tributaries.
   Character: Cold, clear; the cleanest of the major tributaries; minor flood risk;
              historically used for sheep-washing; a small but sustained trout
              fishery in upper reaches supports sport fishing tourism.
   Major town on it: Quilmes (provincial capital, pop. 178,000) sits on its banks.

6. Arroyo de las Llanuras
   Joins Río Marán at: near Corrientes del Marán, Llanuras del Centro
   Length: ~95 km (established in 0-B)
   Province of origin: Rises in central Llanuras del Centro
   What it drains: The inner soy-belt heartland; historically a navigation marker
                   for river pilots (established in 0-B).
   Character: Small, heavily modified; channelized in its lower reach for
              agricultural drainage; low ecological value but hydrologically
              significant for the Corrientes del Marán grain port.
```

### Major Dams (4, established in 0-B — fleshed out here)

```
1. Represa Mariano Aranduy
   Location: Upper Río Marán, Provincia de Río Hondo (km ~260 from headwaters)
   Built: 1968–1974 (6 years of construction; the largest infrastructure project
          in Zoopan history at the time of completion)
   Reservoir: Lago Aranduy (~210 km²; established in 0-B); extends 72 km upstream
              into the Río Hondo gorge.
   Hydroelectric capacity: 1,840 MW installed; annual generation approximately
                           7,200 GWh; supplies ~28% of national electricity grid
                           (established in 0-B).
   Irrigation: Canal system irrigates ~180,000 ha in the upper Pampa Central.
   Controversy:
     - The reservoir displaced approximately 4,200 people, primarily indigenous
       Zopikí communities and small farming settlements in the gorge valleys.
       Compensation was inadequate by the standards of the day; some displaced
       families still lack official land-title replacements as of 2024.
     - Ecological damage: the dam interrupted the seasonal upstream migration of the
       Marán river carp (carpín del Marán, Zoopoichthys maranensis — see Flora/Fauna
       section), a commercially and culturally important fish; populations have
       declined ~70% since 1975.
     - The dam's construction during the military dictatorship (the Proceso era)
       meant public consultation was non-existent; a 2019 parliamentary commission
       produced a formal finding that the displacement constituted a human rights
       violation.
   Current status: Operated by Empresa Nacional de Energía Zoopana (ENEZ);
                   undergoing a USD 340M turbine upgrade (2022–2027) to extend
                   operational life to 2060.

2. Represa de la Confluencia
   Location: Mid-Río Marán, Provincia de San Bartolomé, approximately 15 km upstream
             of the Río Pequeé confluence (km ~515 from headwaters)
   Built: 1952–1958 (established in 0-B)
   Hydroelectric capacity: 320 MW; annual generation ~1,200 GWh.
   Primary function: Irrigation and flood control for the upper Pampa Central;
                     moderates flood peaks before they reach the heavily populated
                     coastal plain.
   Controversy: The dam was built under the conservative government of the late
                1950s; it submerged several pre-Columbian Zopikí archaeological sites
                in the reservoir zone; a 1990 survey by the Universidad Nacional
                de San Bartolomé identified at least 14 significant sites now
                under water. Zopikí heritage groups have campaigned for a UNESCO
                recognition of the submerged cultural landscape.
   Reservoir: Embalse Confluencia (~48 km²); primary water supply for San Bartolomé
              del Pequeé and surrounding towns.

3. Represa Las Totoras
   Location: Lower Río Marán, Provincia de Maraná (km ~1,350 from headwaters;
             approximately 190 km from the river mouth)
   Built: 1988–1993 (established in 0-B)
   Hydroelectric capacity: 180 MW; primarily serves peak-load balancing.
   Primary function: Flood protection for the lower Marán valley and the Puerto
                     Marán metropolitan area; named for the totora reeds that
                     historically fringed this stretch of the river.
   Controversy:
     - Environmental opposition during construction objected to the loss of the
       totora reed gallery forest, a habitat for nesting birds and juvenile fish.
       The environmental review process was abbreviated under the Proceso's
       late-stage infrastructure program.
     - The dam has been linked to accelerated erosion on the Marán delta and
       at the Bahía de Maraná coastline, by reducing the sediment load that
       previously nourished coastal wetlands.
   Note: The name "Las Totoras" refers to the reed beds that were lost; a
         small totora restoration project (2015–present) is underway downstream
         of the dam under the Secretaría de Medio Ambiente.

4. Represa Pequeé Alta
   Location: On the Río Arroyo Grande tributary (NOT the main Marán channel;
             established in 0-B); Provincia de Las Cumbres
   Built: 1979–1984 (construction began during the Cordillera War year;
          a dark historical note recorded in 0-B)
   Hydroelectric capacity: 290 MW; supplies the Sierra del Oeste mining operations
                           primarily; some surplus to the national grid.
   Primary function: Power for copper and silver mining operations in Las Cumbres
                     and Río Hondo; supplemental irrigation for upper valley
                     agricultural zones.
   Controversy:
     - Construction began in August 1979 — the same month Tucareño forces seized
       the Paso de la Viuda. Resources diverted to the war affected the project's
       quality control; two construction fatalities in 1981 were ruled industrial
       accidents, though union records suggest safety shortcuts were systemic.
     - Copper tailings from the nearby Mina Cóndor operation have contaminated
       Lago Pequeé Alta reservoir sediments; a 2021 environmental audit found
       copper concentrations above WHO drinking-water thresholds.
```

### Flood History

```
Named Major Floods:

1. Inundación Grande de 1923
   Year: July–August 1923
   Cause: Exceptionally wet winter following two dry years; Pampa Central soils
          saturated; Río Marán and Río Quilmes simultaneously peaked.
   Areas affected: The entire lower Marán valley from Corrientes del Marán
                   to the coast; the city of Puerto Marán flooded to 1.8 m depth
                   in its historic low-lying districts (Barrio Viejo submerged);
                   ~120,000 people displaced temporarily.
   Deaths: 341 documented dead; an unknown number of rural workers and
           itinerant laborers not counted in official records. Contemporary
           estimates range to 450–500 total.
   Legacy: The flood prompted the construction of the first levee system along
           the lower Marán; a street in Puerto Marán (Calle de los Mártires de
           Julio) is named for the event.

2. Inundación del Marán de 1958
   Year: October–November 1958
   Cause: Combination of late spring rains and an early snowmelt in the
          Cordillera Tupanaku; the Represa de la Confluencia (completed that
          same year) had not yet become fully operational.
   Areas affected: Provinces of San Bartolomé, Llanuras del Centro, Aranduy,
                   and Maraná; the city of San Bartolomé del Pequeé was 
                   extensively damaged; ~65,000 hectares of wheat crop lost.
   Deaths: 87 direct deaths; extensive livestock losses (approximately 42,000
           head of cattle drowned or destroyed).
   Legacy: Accelerated the implementation of Represa de la Confluencia's flood-
           control protocols; the federal emergency response was criticized as
           slow, contributing to political pressure on the 1958–1966 civilian
           governments.

3. Inundación Negra de 1983
   Year: March–April 1983 (late-summer/autumn)
   Cause: A sequence of three consecutive rainfall events over 21 days —
          the worst such sequence in the 20th-century instrumental record;
          associated with an anomalous El Niño year.
   Areas affected: Provinces of Aranduy, Llanuras del Centro, Los Esteros,
                   and coastal Maraná; the Los Esteros wetland system flooded
                   to unprecedented depth; ~200,000 people evacuated.
   Deaths: 219 confirmed dead; 41 missing (presumed dead).
   Economic impact: Estimated ZPS 2.3 billion in 1983 values (~USD 180M at
                    then-exchange rate); 40% of the soy crop in Llanuras del
                    Centro destroyed.
   Name "Negra": Local usage refers to the dark, sediment-laden floodwaters;
                  also used to mark the political irony — the flood happened
                  while the military government was failing diplomatically
                  and the democratic transition was beginning. Journalists
                  used the term to describe both the water and the era.
   Legacy: The 1983 flood is the primary rationale cited in all subsequent
           proposals for the Represa Las Totoras (completed 1993).
```

### Ecological Status

```
Water Quality by Segment:

Upper Marán (Río Hondo province): Largely clean above the dams; some
  contamination from the Represa Pequeé Alta / Mina Cóndor copper runoff
  entering via the Río Arroyo Grande. The Arroyo Cóndor and Arroyo Helado
  above Lago Aranduy are pristine.

Middle Marán (San Bartolomé and Llanuras del Centro): Moderate degradation
  from agricultural runoff (nitrates, phosphates, organochlorine pesticides).
  The soy and wheat belt delivers significant fertilizer loads in spring
  runoff. The section near Corrientes del Marán receives treated and
  untreated municipal wastewater (the city's wastewater treatment plant,
  built 1971, operates at 140% capacity per 2022 audit).

Lower Marán (Maraná province): Most degraded; the Refinería del Sur petroleum
  refinery at Puerto Marán discharges treated effluent into the lower river
  (under permit); three separate illegal dumping incidents documented 2015–2023.
  Textile dye and chemical manufacturing plants in Puerto Marán's northern
  industrial zone are chronic permit violators. Fecal coliform counts exceed
  national standards for swimming 90+ days per year at the Puerto Marán beach
  at Bahía de Maraná.

Restoration efforts:
  - Programa de Saneamiento del Marán (PSM): Launched 2018 under President
    Vidal; focused on municipal wastewater treatment upgrades in 12 Pampa
    Central cities; progress is slow due to funding shortfalls.
  - Gallery forest replanting: NGO Bosques del Marán (founded 2004) has 
    restored approximately 8,200 ha of riparian gallery forest along middle-
    Marán tributaries as of 2025.
  - The carpín del Marán fish ladder program: ENEZ and the Secretaría de
    Medio Ambiente have installed fish ladders at the Represa de la
    Confluencia (2015) and are studying a partial bypass at Represa Mariano
    Aranduy. Early monitoring data shows modest recolonization of reaches
    above Confluencia.
```

## SECTION 2 — Río Pequeé (Detailed)

### Source and Course

```
Source: Glacial and spring waters in the northern Sierra del Oeste, Provincia de
        Pequeé Norte, at approximately 3,200 m altitude (established in 0-B).
        Specific source feature: Glaciar Norte — a small glacial remnant on the
        eastern face of the Cordillera Pequeé, feeding the headwaters tributary
        Arroyo Tupanaku (upper Pequeé).
        The headwaters lie within ~40 km of the Zoopan-Tucareño border (0-B).
Approximate coordinates: 36°08'S, 66°15'W (fictional; internally consistent)
Length: 480 km (established in 0-B)
Flow direction: Southeast, from the Cordillera Pequeé through Pequeé Norte, then
                east through San Bartolomé province to its confluence with the Marán.
Joins Río Marán: At Confluencia, Provincia de San Bartolomé, ~510 km from the
                 Atlantic (established in 0-B).
```

### The Disputed Stretch — War of the Pequeé

```
Disputed section: Upper 180 km of the river (from headwaters to the settlement
                  then called Paso del Pequeé, now Cerro Verde) — established in 0-B.

What made it strategically and economically valuable:

1. Mineral rights: The Cordillera Pequeé's eastern foothills contained significant
   silver and copper deposits known to both the Spanish colonial authorities and
   to the indigenous Zopikí and Tucareño peoples. Both nations' mining codes
   claimed sovereignty over the deposits, which the river valley gave access to.
   The silver mines near what is now Laguna Sal were already producing in the
   1860s and their ownership was the primary economic grievance.

2. Ford crossings: Three natural ford sites on the upper Pequeé (at Vado Blanco,
   Vado Tupanaku, and Vado de las Piedras — now submerged under the Represa
   Pequeé Alta reservoir) allowed crossing without bridges and were essential for
   military movement and animal trade. Whoever held the fords controlled
   movement through the valley.

3. The towns: The settlement of Paso del Pequeé (renamed Cerro Verde in 1873;
   pop. ~440,000 today) was the largest population center in the disputed valley.
   Cantonments of both Zoopan and Tucareño settlers made it a contested community;
   the 1870 Treaty of Pequeé City's border settlement displaced an estimated
   3,000–4,000 Tucareño settlers who chose to leave rather than live under
   Zoopan jurisdiction.

4. Water rights: The upper Pequeé was (and remains) the primary water source for
   the entire northern Sierra del Oeste. Controlling the headwaters meant
   controlling irrigation for valley farming below.

5. Strategic depth: The river valley provided a natural invasion corridor from
   Tucaré into Zoop's heartland; the 1865–1870 war began in part when Tucareño
   forces moved down the valley; Zoop's counter-push seized the entire upper valley.
```

### Ecological Character

```
The Pequeé basin is ecologically distinct from the main Marán basin in several ways:

1. Higher-altitude catchment: The Pequeé drains the Cordillera Pequeé's eastern
   rain shadow — a drier, more mineralized landscape than the humid Cordillera
   Tupanaku where the Marán rises. The upper Pequeé is correspondingly cleaner
   and harder (higher dissolved mineral content) than the Marán.

2. Smaller gallery forest: The narrow valley of the upper Pequeé, carved through
   arid highland, supports a more limited riparian tree community — dominated by
   queñoa (highland polylepis relatives), tola shrubs, and in lower reaches, 
   carob (algarrobo zoopano) woodland — rather than the broadleaf gallery forest
   of the lower Marán.

3. Mining legacy: The upper Pequeé is heavily affected by legacy silver and copper
   mining; the river carries elevated heavy-metal loads (lead, cadmium, arsenic)
   in its upper 220 km. The Río Pequeé at Cerro Verde fails Zoop's Class III
   (marginal use) water quality standard in 7 of the past 10 monitoring years.

4. The Pequeé Norte wetland: In its lower reaches, before joining the Marán near
   Confluencia, the Pequeé broadens into a seasonal floodplain wetland
   (Bañados del Pequeé, approximately 1,100 km²) that provides important habitat
   for migratory waterbirds; this zone is partially protected as a provincial
   nature reserve.

5. The Pequeé fishery: Before heavy metal contamination from mining, the Pequeé
   supported a distinct native fish fauna. The Pequeé pejerrey (Atherinopsis
   pequeensis) was once commercially fished; populations have declined by an
   estimated 85% since 1960.
```

## SECTION 3 — Atlantic Coastline (Detailed)

### Named Capes

```
1. Cabo de San Roque
   Location: Northernmost point of Zoop's coastline; Provincia de Candelaria
   (established in 0-B)
   Elevation: The cape rises 48 m above sea level on a basalt headland; the
              lighthouse (established 1847; automated 1978) is visible 22 nautical
              miles offshore.
   Character: Tropical-edge character; warm subtropical waters; the sea temperature
              here is 2–4°C warmer than at Cabo Marán. Rocky shoreline to the north
              (dramatic cliffs), sandy beaches to the south. The cape marks the
              northern boundary of the main commercial shipping lane into Puerto Marán.
   Wildlife: Nesting ground for the Candelaria sea turtle (tortuga de Candelaria,
             Chelonia zoopana); a small but recovering population that nests on the
             sandy beaches south of the cape headland each November–January.
   Tourism: Playa Candelaria Norte, the beach 8 km south of the cape, is Candelaria
            province's primary resort area; modest hotels and eco-lodges; popular
            with domestic tourists in January–February.

2. Cabo Marán
   Location: Southern headland of Bahía de Maraná, near Puerto Marán (established
             in 0-B); the Port Authority lighthouse dates to 1883.
   Elevation: 31 m above sea level; granite and sedimentary rock formation.
   Character: Urban-adjacent; the most photographed coastal feature in Zoop (per
              0-B). The cape separates the working port of Puerto Marán from the
              resort beaches of Playa del Cabo to the south. The lighthouse is a
              national historic monument; the associated keeper's house is now
              a museum and popular walking destination.
   Wildlife: A small colony (~240 individuals) of South American sea lions (lobo
             marino del Marán, Otaria flavescens zoopana) occupies a rock outcrop
             1.2 km south of the lighthouse; the colony is protected and has become
             a significant tourist attraction.
   Note: The cape gives its name to the Banco Marán fishing grounds ~80 km offshore.

3. Cabo de las Tormentas
   Location: Southern coast, Provincia de Quilmes del Sur; Zoop's southernmost
             navigable cape (established in 0-B)
   Elevation: 72 m above sea level; dramatic shale and sandstone cliffs.
   Character: Wild, windswept; named for the violent austral winter storms that
              funnel up from subantarctic waters (established in 0-B). The cape
              is avoided by coastal shipping October through June. Wind speeds
              exceed 90 km/h on average 18 days per year. The cape scenery is
              stark and celebrated — poet Valentina Oriundo Bracho described it
              as "where the sky ends and the sea begins its argument."
   Wildlife: The largest Magellanic penguin (pingüino zoopano, Spheniscus
             magellanicus zoopanus) colony in Zoop nests at the cape; ~4,800
             breeding pairs arrive in September–October; a growing eco-tourism
             circuit (restricted access) operates October–March.
   Lighthouse: Established 1872; one of the most isolated postings in the Zoopan
               coast guard; the lighthouse keeper's position has been continuous
               since 1873, making it the longest-continuously-staffed lighthouse
               in the country.
```

### Named Bays and Coastal Waters

```
1. Bahía de Maraná
   Location: Puerto Marán, Provincia de Maraná (established in 0-B)
   Width at mouth: approximately 18 km (established in 0-B)
   Character: Broad, deep estuary; Puerto Marán's commercial harbor occupies its
              northern shore; the southern shore (Playa del Cabo district) is
              partly residential and partly tourist beach.
   Ecology: The estuary is a critical nursery habitat for juvenile hake and corvina;
            the Río Marán's freshwater plume creates a productive mixing zone that
            extends 15–20 km offshore; however, industrial and municipal pollution
            from Puerto Marán has significantly degraded intertidal habitat.
   Annual dredging: 12-m-channel maintenance required; ~800,000 m³ dredged/year
                    (established in 0-B).

2. Bahía Larga
   Location: Provincia de Bahía Larga; capital Villa Inés (established in 0-B)
   Dimensions: Approximately 85 km long, 6–14 km wide; shallow (maximum depth
               4 m in the main channel); separated from the open sea by a
               discontinuous barrier-beach system.
   RAMSAR status: Designated 1993 (established in 0-B); designation based on the
                  lagoon system's role as:
                  (a) Primary wintering ground for the Zoopan flamingo (flamenco
                      zoopano, a near-endemic subspecies; established in 0-B)
                  (b) Staging area for ~40 migratory shorebird species on the
                      Atlantic flyway, including regional populations of the
                      lesser knot and Hudsonian godwit
                  (c) Nursery habitat for commercial fish and shellfish species
                  (d) Carbon-sequestering saltmarsh and seagrass habitats
   Ecology: The barrier beach system filters wave energy; the lagoon interior
            supports extensive seagrass beds (Zostera zoopana) and saltmarsh
            (dominated by Spartina maranensis); total extent of protected
            wetland is 94,000 ha (940 km²).
   Threats: Agricultural drainage from dairy farms inland is increasing nutrient
            loads; a 2023 study found seagrass extent had declined 18% since
            the 1993 RAMSAR baseline.

3. Ensenada de Santa Lucía
   Location: Santa Lucía, Provincia de Santa Lucía del Mar (established in 0-B)
   Dimensions: Approximately 22 km across; 8 km deep; maximum depth 12 m.
   Character: Smaller, curved bay; adequate depth for naval vessels and medium
              cargo ships; site of the Zoopan Navy's southern Atlantic base
              (established in 0-B). The beach district (Playa Santa Lucía) is
              a popular domestic resort — known for its 9-km continuous sandy
              beach, slightly cooler than the Candelaria beaches and calmer than
              the exposed Quilmes del Sur coast.
   Note: Santa Lucía is Zoop's most politically watched city (established in 0-B);
         the beach resort economy adds a tourist dimension to its political
         significance — national journalists half-joke that polling the beach
         vendors predicts presidential elections.
```

### Primary Fishing Grounds

```
Banco Marán:
   Location: Continental shelf, centered ~80 km east of Puerto Marán (established
             in 0-B); extends approximately 180 km north-south and 90 km east-west;
             depth 60–220 m.
   Primary species: merluza (hake, Merluccius zoopanicus), corvina (Micropogonias
                    maranensis), calamar (squid, Illex zoopanus) — established in 0-B
   Annual catch (Zoopan registered fleet): approximately 340,000 tonnes/year (2023);
              breakdown: hake ~190,000 t, squid ~95,000 t, corvina ~55,000 t
   Fleet size: 214 registered commercial fishing vessels (2024); 89 are large
               industrial trawlers, 125 are mid-sized artisanal/semi-industrial.
               Additionally, 1,200+ small-boat fishing operations work inshore
               grounds.
   Foreign fleet: Chinese, Spanish, and South Korean fleets operate in adjacent
                  waters and periodically violate Zoop's 200-nautical-mile EEZ;
                  8–12 foreign vessel seizures per year on average; a serious
                  diplomatic irritant (especially with China, given lithium
                  investment sensitivities).
   Squid season: March–August (peak); the squid fishery has grown dramatically
                 since the 1990s and now accounts for roughly 28% of total catch
                 value; Chinese demand drives the market.

Northern inshore grounds (Candelaria coast):
   Species: Yellowfin tuna (dorado), mahi-mahi, artisanal fish (snapper, sea bass)
   Character: Warmer subtropical waters; smaller-scale artisanal operations; Puerto
              Canela is the main landing port.

Southern inshore grounds (Quilmes del Sur coast):
   Species: Patagonian toothfish (occasional), king crab, southern hake
   Character: Colder waters; requires licensed operations; smaller fleet.
```

### Beach Tourism Areas

```
The primary beach resort zone lies along the Provincia de Santa Lucía del Mar
and the southern Bahía Larga coast — the "Costa Central" circuit:

  - Playa Santa Lucía: 9-km beach at the Ensenada de Santa Lucía; the most
    visited beach in Zoop; 34 hotels and resorts; peak season January–March.
  - Playa de las Dunas: 12 km south of Villa Inés; backed by active dune
    system; popular with naturalists and kite-surfers.
  - Playa Bahía Sur: A resort strip at the southern end of Bahía Larga;
    3 resort hotels (2–4 star); outside the RAMSAR protected zone; water
    sports; less crowded than Santa Lucía.

Secondary resort area: Provincia de Candelaria (north)
  - Playa Candelaria Norte (near Cabo de San Roque): Subtropical warmth;
    longest swimming season (October–April); eco-lodge oriented; smaller
    than the Costa Central circuit.

Note: Puerto Marán's beaches (Playa del Cabo, Playa Puerto Viejo) are
      visited by locals but suffer from pollution in Bahía de Maraná;
      not considered a significant tourist destination for domestic
      or international beach vacationers.
```

## SECTION 4 — Sierra del Oeste (Detailed)

### Sub-Ranges

```
1. Cordillera Tupanaku — Main Range (south-central Sierra del Oeste)
   Location: Runs north-south through Provincias de Río Hondo and Las Cumbres
             (established in 0-B). Contains Zoop's highest peaks.
   Character: Glaciated, rugged; source of the Río Marán (established in 0-B).
              Maximum elevation: 4,812 m (Pico Aranduy). Permanent snow above
              ~4,200 m; retreating glaciers documented on 7 of the 12 named
              glacial masses. Dense cloud forest on eastern windward slopes
              (1,800–2,800 m); puna grassland above.
   Geology: Precambrian metamorphic core with Mesozoic intrusive granite;
            significant silver and copper mineralization associated with the
            granite batholiths; legacy colonial mines throughout Las Cumbres
            and Río Hondo provinces.
   Ecological significance: The Cordillera Tupanaku cloud forest zone is one
              of the most biodiverse habitats in Zoop; approximately 38% of
              endemic plant species are found here.

2. Cordillera Pequeé — Northern Range (border with Tucaré)
   Location: North-south through Provincia de Pequeé Norte; forms the disputed
             border with República de Tucaré to the west (established in 0-B).
   Character: Lower than Tupanaku; maximum elevation approximately 4,200 m.
              Drier than the Tupanaku; the eastern side lies in a rain shadow
              (~300–400 mm precipitation/year in the high basins) — the condition
              that creates the evaporative salares.
   Geology: The Cordillera Pequeé sits atop a Miocene-Pliocene volcanic sequence
            overlying a basement of lithium-bearing brine aquifers. The volcanic
            rock's low permeability keeps the brines concentrated below the salares.
            Silver and copper: legacy mining districts at Mina Plata Norte (silver,
            operated 1882–1953; now dormant), Mina Cóndor (copper, still active
            but declining; Pequeé Norte province).
   Lithium: See Lithium Deposits sub-section below.
   Strategic significance: Contains Paso de la Viuda (established in 0-B; see
            below) and Paso Internacional Colinas Verdes (main trade crossing).

3. Sierras del Río Blanco — Eastern Foothills (wine country)
   Location: Eastern foothills; transition from Cordillera Tupanaku to Pampa
             Central; Provincias de Las Cumbres and Valles del Oeste
             (established in 0-B).
   Character: Dissected by river valleys; lower, eroded range (maximum elevation
              ~2,100 m); more accessible than the higher ranges (established in
              0-B). The Val del Oeste wine appellation occupies these foothills
              (established in 0-B); soils are calcareous-sandstone-derived, with
              good drainage and mineral complexity. The diurnal temperature range
              of 15–20°C (established in 0-B) is ideal for aromatic grape varieties.
   Ecology: Transitional woodland of algarrobo (Prosopis zoopanus), quebracho
            blanco (Aspidosperma zoopanum), and native pastures; extensively
            modified by viticulture but with intact native patches on steeper
            slopes. Significant bird diversity (raptors; a nesting population
            of the Zoopan hawk-eagle, Spizaetus zoopanus, in the upper slopes).
```

### Pico Aranduy (4,812 m)

```
Location: Cordillera Tupanaku, Provincia de Las Cumbres (established in 0-B)
Character: Named for General Mariano Pelayo Aranduy, first president (established in 0-B)

First ascent:
  Date: 14 September 1881
  Party: A five-person team from the Comisión Geográfica Militar (Military Geographic
         Commission), led by Capitán Eduardo Farías Molina (no relation to the later
         dictator General Faría). The ascent was undertaken as a boundary and
         topographic survey, not as sport; the party reached the summit after a
         three-day approach from San Ignacio de las Cumbres.
  Significance: Capitán Farías Molina's survey report, published in the Boletín
                Geográfico Zoopano in 1882, contained the first detailed topographic
                description of the Cordillera Tupanaku and established Pico Aranduy
                as Zoop's highest point (previously disputed with a peak in
                Pequeé Norte that later measurement confirmed is 4,617 m).

Current status:
  - Recreational climbing: The Ruta Normal (standard route) is a non-technical
    high-altitude climb graded PD (peu difficile); typically completed in 3–4 days
    from San Ignacio de las Cumbres. The Refugio Farías (at 3,950 m; established
    1956; rebuilt 2009) provides overnight accommodation. Approximately 800–1,000
    climbers attempt the summit each year; 60–70% success rate.
  - Protected: Pico Aranduy lies within the Parque Nacional Tupanaku (see
    National Parks section); commercial guiding is regulated; access requires
    park entry permit and rescue-fee payment.
  - Condor colony: One of the five primary Andean condor nesting sites in Zoop
    is located on the eastern cliffs of Pico Aranduy (see Fauna section).
  - Cultural significance: Reaching the summit is colloquially described as
    "tocar el techo" (touching the roof); school groups and military units
    complete ascents as character-building exercises.
```

### Paso de la Viuda (3,340 m)

```
Location: Cordillera Pequeé, Provincia de Las Cumbres, near the border with
          República de Tucaré (established in 0-B)
Elevation: 3,340 m (established in 0-B)
Character of the pass:
  - The pass is a broad saddle approximately 1.4 km wide between two peaks
    (Cerro de la Viuda, 3,890 m, and Cerro Oscuro, 3,720 m); not a narrow
    knife-edge but a wind-scoured plateau of rock and sparse puna grass.
  - From the Zoopan side: the approach is via the Quebrada Larga valley
    (established in 0-B), a 28-km access route from the town of Río Blanco;
    the gradient is manageable by vehicle to ~3,000 m (4WD required); the
    final 340 m gain is on foot or by mule.
  - From the Tucareño side: the pass drops steeply into the Río Oscuro valley
    (established in 0-B); this side is more exposed and receives less maintenance.
  - Climate: Snow-blocked June–September typically; viento blanco (white-wind
    blizzard; established in 0-B) can close the pass without warning year-round.

Strategic importance in the 1979 Cordillera War:
  - The Paso de la Viuda overlooks the Zoopan Quebrada Larga valley to the east;
    artillery or observation posts on the pass would have commanding fields of fire
    over 18 km of the valley below, including the supply road from Río Blanco.
  - Tucareño forces seized the pass on Day 1 of the war (14 August 1979), giving
    them this commanding position; the Zoopan counterattack over three weeks
    pushed Tucareño forces off the western rim but did not retake the saddle.
  - The ceasefire of 30 September 1979 left the joint patrol line running across
    the middle of the saddle (a 200-m "neutral zone"); Zoopan checkpoints are on
    the east side, Tucareño checkpoints on the west.
  - Name legend: The pass was named well before 1979 — from a legend that widows
    of the War of the Pequeé (1865–1870) came to this pass to scan the western
    horizon for husbands who never returned (established in 0-B). The name's
    resonance was not lost on the 312 families bereaved in 1979.

Current status:
  - Under joint patrol protocol per the Acuerdo de Cese de Fuego de Río Blanco
    (1980; established in 0-B); Tucaré has never fully ratified the border
    demarcation (established in 0-B).
  - Joint patrol: A six-person Zoopan Gendarmería team and a corresponding
    Tucareño border force unit maintain liaison posts at the pass; they rotate
    on 90-day cycles; the arrangement has operated continuously since 1981
    despite periodic diplomatic tensions.
  - Annual ceremony: On 30 September each year (ceasefire anniversary), both
    sides hold separate but simultaneous commemorations at their respective
    patrol posts; a symbolic exchange of mountain flowers has been a tradition
    since 1995.
  - Tourism: The pass is NOT a tourist destination; access from the Zoopan side
    requires Gendarmería authorization; organized trekking groups have been
    refused since a 2018 incident when Spanish trekkers inadvertently entered
    the neutral zone.
```

### Lithium Deposits

```
Location: The Salar de Tupanaku (established in 0-B, Pequeé Norte province)
          and adjacent smaller salares in the eastern rain shadow of the
          Cordillera Pequeé.

Salar de Tupanaku:
  Area: approximately 2,100 km² (the largest salar in Zoop)
  Elevation: ~3,650 m asl
  Character: A dry salt flat underlain by lithium-rich brine aquifer; the brine
             sits in a porous sediment layer (halite, silica, gypite) at depths
             of 15–120 m below the salar surface. Lithium concentrations in
             the brine average 890 mg/L (high grade by South American standards;
             comparable to the upper tier of the Chilean/Bolivian deposits).
  Adjacent salares: Salar Pequeé Chico (~380 km²), Salar del Volcán (~140 km²);
                    together these form the "Tupanaku Lithium Triangle."
  Lithium carbonate equivalent (LCE) reserve estimate: 6.8 million tonnes
                    (USGS-equivalent estimate by Zoopan Geological Survey, 2021);
                    potential for 12–18 million tonnes with deeper drilling.
  Extraction method: Solar evaporation of pumped brine in evaporation ponds
                    (the same methodology as Atacama and Salar de Uyuni operations);
                    evaporation pond infrastructure visible by satellite; the
                    ponds cover approximately 340 km² as of 2024.
  Operator: Consorcio Litio Tupanaku (CLT) — a joint venture between the Zoopan
            state mining company MINZOOP (45%), China's Yanhe Minerals Corporation
            (35%), and a U.S. private fund (20%). The CLT concession was granted
            in 2013; full-scale production began 2018; currently produces ~48,000
            tonnes LCE/year; target 120,000 tonnes/year by 2030.
  Water use controversy: Brine pumping draws from the same aquifer system that
            indigenous Zopikí communities use for drinking water and high-altitude
            llama pasture irrigation in the altiplano around the salar. The CLT's
            own 2019 hydrogeological study acknowledged aquifer drawdown of 1.2 m
            since operations began; independent monitoring by Universidad Nacional
            de Pequeé Norte found 2.8 m drawdown and three traditional water
            sources (ojos de agua) that have gone dry since 2019.
  Indigenous claim: Zopikí communities of the Alto Tupanaku — the Comunidad
            Tupanaku and the Comunidad Volcán — filed a formal objection in 2014
            under the 1994 constitutional provisions on indigenous land rights;
            litigation was pending before the Constitutional Court as of 2025.

Copper and silver (historical mining; see also Sub-Ranges section):
  Silver: Historically mined in the Cordillera Tupanaku (Río Hondo and Las Cumbres
          provinces) from the colonial era; the mines around San Ignacio de las
          Cumbres were active from approximately 1580 to the early 1800s; silver
          production declined after 1700 (as the seed document notes). Legacy
          mine workings (Minas de San Ignacio colonial district) are now a
          cultural heritage site; some artisanal silver mining continues.
  Copper: Active copper mining at Mina Cóndor (Pequeé Norte) and legacy operations
          in the Cordillera Pequeé foothills. The Mina Cóndor complex is the
          second-largest copper producer in Zoop after the larger Tucaré-border
          mines; tailings management has been a persistent regulatory issue.
```

## SECTION 5 — Pampa Central

### Area and Physical Character

```
Area: Approximately 78,000 km² (roughly 41% of Zoop's national territory of 189,000 km²)
Provinces encompassed: Aranduy, Llanuras del Centro, San Bartolomé, Quilmes del Sur,
                       Los Esteros, Solórzano; plus the pampa-zone interior of
                       Santa Lucía del Mar and Bahía Larga provinces.
Elevation: Generally 50–350 m asl; rising gently from the coast to the Sierra
           del Oeste foothills; the "flatness" is not absolute — there are gentle
           undulations (cuchillas) of 5–15 m that are culturally invisible but
           hydrologically significant.
```

### Soils

```
Soil type: Deep Mollisols (equivalent to the Argentine pampas pampic soil series);
           locally called "tierra negra pampeana" (black pampa earth).
Character: The soils formed over 8,000–12,000 years from loessial deposits (wind-
           blown silt from glacial outwash to the south and west) that accumulated
           over a pre-existing calcrete hardpan. Organic matter content: 4–7%
           (exceptional by global standards; comparable to Ukraine's chernozems).
Depth: A horizon (humus-rich topsoil) averages 0.8–1.4 m deep before reaching
       the B horizon; below 2.5 m is the calcrete (tosca) hardpan.
Productivity: The Llanuras del Centro and Aranduy provinces produce soy yields of
              3.2–4.0 tonnes/ha (above the South American average); wheat yields
              of 3.5–4.5 t/ha. The soil's fertility has historically required
              relatively modest external inputs, though continuous soy monoculture
              since the 1990s has begun to deplete organic matter.
```

### The Pampa Aquifer

```
Name: Acuífero Pampero (the "Pampa Aquifer")
Character: A large, unconfined aquifer in the loessial sediment layer, recharged
           by Río Marán flood events and direct precipitation infiltration.
Depth to water table: Typically 8–25 m below surface in the center; shallower
           (3–8 m) near river courses; deeper (25–40 m) in the drier western
           sub-zone near the Sierra del Oeste foothills.
Extent: Underlies roughly 60,000 km² of the Pampa Central; the thickest
        saturated zone (up to 80 m) is in central Llanuras del Centro.
Agricultural use: ~42,000 registered wells extract irrigation water (2022);
           total extraction estimated 3.2 billion m³/year; aquifer recharge
           estimated at 2.4–2.7 billion m³/year — a deficit of 0.5–0.8 billion
           m³/year in recent dry cycles.
Depletion concerns: Water table has dropped an average of 0.6 m/decade in central
           Llanuras del Centro since 1985 monitoring began; in the drier western
           sub-zone, decline is 1.1 m/decade. Agricultural lobby has resisted
           metering requirements; the Secretaría de Medio Ambiente has proposed
           mandatory aquifer monitoring since 2017 without legislative resolution.
Contamination: Nitrate contamination from fertilizers exceeds WHO drinking water
               standards in 8% of tested wells (2023 survey); pesticide
               contamination (organochlorines) detected in 14% of wells.
```

### Seasonal Patterns

```
Wet season: October–April (austral spring/summer/autumn)
   - Main rainfall period; agricultural growing season
   - Flood risk in low-lying areas (cuencas) when >200 mm falls in a month
   - Temperature: 18–34°C; hot, humid; thunderstorms common December–February

Dry season: May–September (austral autumn/winter)
   - Reduced rainfall; cold pampero wind from southwest
   - Temperature: -2 to 18°C; frost common June–August
   - Agricultural machinery season: wheat harvest April–June; soy harvest
     March–May; land preparation for planting September–October
   - Drought risk: approximately 1 year in 4 is a significant drought year
     in the western Pampa sub-zone
```

### Cultural Significance of the Flat Horizon

```
The pampa horizon — the point where grassland and sky merge in an unbroken line —
is one of the most powerful images in Zoopan cultural life.

In literature: Nobel laureate Esteban Calderón Ríos (1989) repeatedly used the
   pampa horizon as a metaphor for both freedom and existential loneliness; his
   1974 novel "El Fin del Horizonte" (The End of the Horizon) opens with the
   line: "There is a country where the earth meets the sky and neither wins."
   The novel was banned by the Proceso dictatorship (1974–1985) and circulated
   clandestinely; its status as a symbol of resistance is partly owed to this.

In folklore: The pampero wind is personified in Zoopan folklore as "El Pampero,"
   a capricious spirit who travels the plain; folk songs frequently address the
   horizon as a character. The phrase "mirar el horizonte" (to look at the
   horizon) means to dream, to hope, or sometimes to avoid the present.

In architecture: The low, outward-looking design of traditional estancia farm-
   houses — windows wide, walls low — is designed to capture the horizon on
   all sides; the unobstructed view is considered psychologically essential
   in pampa construction culture.

In national identity: The pampa is both the economic heart of Zoop and a
   cultural archetype of openness and self-sufficiency. The tension between
   the pampa's vast horizontal freedom and the city's vertical ambition runs
   through Zoopan literature, music, and political rhetoric.
```

## SECTION 6 — Flora

### National Flower

```
Ceibo zoopano (Erythrina zoopana)
   The national flower; a large tree of river edges and pampa margins; related
   to the Argentine-Uruguayan ceibo (E. crista-galli) but distinct; produces
   spectacular crimson-red flowers October–January. The ceibo zoopano grows up
   to 8 m tall and tolerates seasonal flooding, making it the characteristic
   tree of the Río Marán gallery forest margins. The flowers are pollinated
   primarily by hummingbirds; the wood is soft (used for floats and carved crafts
   in Zopikí tradition).
```

### 4–5 Notable Plant Species

```
1. Ombú zoopano / Ombú del Marán
   Scientific name: Phytolacca maranensis
   Common name: "Ombú del Marán" (Zoopan ombú)
   Character: A massive arborescent herb (not technically a tree; like the
              Argentine ombú) that grows to 12–15 m height and spreads a
              canopy 20–25 m wide; the trunk base is deeply furrowed and
              fire-resistant. The dominant isolated plant of the Pampa Central;
              found singly or in small groves on open grassland, especially near
              watercourses. The ombú zoopano is the "sentinel of the pampa" —
              estancias are often identified in aerial photographs by the ombú
              groves near the main house. Fire-resistant, flood-tolerant,
              immune to most agricultural chemicals (its sap is mildly toxic
              to livestock); as a result it survives in otherwise fully
              cultivated areas as a remnant of the pre-agricultural landscape.
   Cultural status: Appears on the Zoopan 10-peso coin; the shade of an ombú
              grove is a stereotyped setting for gaucho storytelling in
              folklore zoopano music.

2. Queñoa del Oeste / Mountain polylepis
   Scientific name: Polylepis zoopanus
   Common name: "Queñoa del Oeste"
   Character: The primary native tree of the Andean zone between 2,800 and
              4,100 m elevation; grows in gnarled, densely-branched groves in
              sheltered mountain gullies and ravines. Trunk bark peels in
              red-orange sheets; wood extremely dense and long-burning.
              Critical habitat for numerous endemic bird species (see Fauna).
              The queñoa forest is highly threatened by historic cutting for
              firewood; less than 12% of the estimated pre-colonial queñoa
              cover remains in the Sierra del Oeste.
   Conservation status: Protected species since 1991; replanting programs
              under Secretaría de Medio Ambiente; Parque Nacional Tupanaku
              contains the largest intact queñoa forest in Zoop (~14,000 ha).

3. Totora del Marán / River reed
   Scientific name: Schoenoplectus maranensis
   Common name: "Totora del Marán"
   Character: The dominant large reed of the Río Marán's freshwater wetlands,
              lagoons, and slower river margins; grows to 3–4 m; dense stands
              provide essential nesting and roosting habitat for waterbirds.
              The Represa Las Totoras takes its name from the totora beds lost
              to its construction. Zopikí communities have used totora for
              mat-weaving, basket-making, and float construction for millennia;
              the craft tradition is still practiced in Los Esteros province.
   Status: Locally abundant in protected areas; greatly reduced along the
           engineered lower Marán and near Puerto Marán due to water quality
           and bank hardening; not threatened nationally.

4. Yerba del Marán / Zoopan mate plant
   Scientific name: Ilex maranensis
   Common name: "Yerba del Marán" or "Yerba Zoopana"
   Character: A close relative of Ilex paraguariensis (the standard South American
              mate plant), but native to the subtropical gallery forests of
              Candelaria province and the northern Río Marán basin. It produces
              leaves slightly smaller and more bitter than standard yerba mate,
              with a distinct earthy aroma attributed to specific soil chemistry.
              The Yerba del Marán was commercially cultivated in limited quantities
              in Candelaria province since the 1930s; small premium batches are
              marketed as "Mate Zoopano Puro" and command a price premium.
              Zopikí communities of the north have used this plant in ceremony
              for centuries, pre-dating European adoption of the mate custom.
   Status: Cultivated in ~4,200 ha of Candelaria province; not endangered.
           Wild populations in gallery forest remnants are declining due to
           deforestation.

5. Flor de la Viuda / Widow's flower (ENDANGERED)
   Scientific name: Calycophyllum viudense
   Common name: "Flor de la Viuda" (Widow's flower)
   Character: A small flowering shrub (0.4–0.8 m) native to the puna grasslands
              of the Cordillera Pequeé above 3,400 m; produces white five-petaled
              flowers with a violet center July–September (austral winter/early
              spring). The plant is associated with the Paso de la Viuda legend;
              Zopikí high-altitude communities use the dried flowers in a
              memorial ceremony for the dead. The species is a narrow endemic —
              found only within approximately 280 km² of the Cordillera Pequeé
              around the Paso de la Viuda and adjacent ridgelines.
   Threats: Climate change is reducing the puna grassland zone by upward creep
            of shrub vegetation; military disturbance at and near Paso de la
            Viuda; overgrazing by feral livestock; the species is being
            studied by researchers from Universidad Nacional de Pequeé Norte
            for possible inclusion in ex situ seed banking.
   Conservation status: CRITICALLY ENDANGERED on the Zoopan national endangered
                        species list (Lista Roja Zoopana, 2022); fewer than
                        14,000 wild individuals estimated.
```

## SECTION 7 — Fauna

### National Animal

```
Andean condor (cóndor de los Andes, Vultur gryphus)
   Nesting grounds in Zoop specifically:
   Five primary nesting colonies are identified in the Sierra del Oeste:
     1. Eastern cliffs of Pico Aranduy (Cordillera Tupanaku, Las Cumbres) — the
        most photographed colony; ~12 breeding pairs; accessible by telescope from
        the Refugio Farías.
     2. Quebrada Oscura (northern Río Hondo province) — ~9 breeding pairs; below
        the Lago Aranduy shoreline.
     3. Cerro de la Viuda (Cordillera Pequeé near Paso de la Viuda) — ~7 breeding
        pairs; monitoring complicated by restricted military zone.
     4. Cañón del Río Blanco (Sierras del Río Blanco, Las Cumbres) — ~11 breeding
        pairs; most accessible to researchers.
     5. Sierras del Volcán (Pequeé Norte) — ~6 breeding pairs; increasing as the
        lithium mining area expands (disturbance is pushing condors to higher
        elevations).
   Total estimated population in Zoop: 180–220 individuals (2023 census);
           stable to slightly increasing since the 1980s when the population
           was estimated at fewer than 100. The recovery is attributed to
           anti-poisoning campaigns (lead shot and strychnine bait elimination),
           protection of nesting sites under the 1989 Ley de Conservación
           de la Fauna Zoopana, and prey base recovery.
```

### Notable Species

```
1. Guanaco del Marán (Large mammal)
   Scientific name: Lama guanicoe maranensis
   Common name: "Guanaco del Marán" (Marán guanaco)
   Character: A subspecies of the guanaco — the wild South American camelid —
              adapted to the transitional zone between the Sierra del Oeste and
              the Pampa Central. Larger than the Patagonian form; pale fawn
              body with a darker face. Found in the foothills and upper pampa
              grasslands at 300–2,500 m elevation; grazes primarily on native
              grasses and shrubs.
   Population: Estimated 22,000–28,000 individuals in Zoop (2022 census);
               the species was hunted heavily during the colonial era and
               through the early 20th century; populations crashed to fewer
               than 5,000 by 1960. Protected since 1972; the recovery has
               been substantial.
   Cultural significance: The guanaco is a major figure in Zopikí traditional
               belief — its migration patterns across the foothills are used
               in traditional calendar systems; it appears in rock art
               throughout the Sierra del Oeste.
   Threat: Agricultural fencing fragments its range; conflicts with cattle
           ranchers in the Valles del Oeste transition zone.

2. Puma zoopano (Large mammal — apex predator)
   Scientific name: Puma concolor zoopanus
   Common name: "Puma zoopano"
   Character: The native large cat of the Sierra del Oeste and pampa-fringe
              areas; a regional form of the South American puma, somewhat
              smaller than the Andean form; coat is tawny without significant
              spot variation.
   Population: Estimated 600–800 individuals in Zoop; range restricted to the
               Sierra del Oeste and isolated populations in the Pampa Central
               gallery forests.
   Status: Listed as Vulnerable on the Lista Roja Zoopana; rancher conflicts
           are the primary driver of mortality; a compensation fund for
           livestock losses to puma predation (Fondo Gaucho-Puma) was
           established in 2021 by the Secretaría de Medio Ambiente to
           reduce retaliatory killing.

3. Lobo marino del Marán (Coastal species)
   Scientific name: Otaria flavescens zoopana
   Common name: South American sea lion (Zoopan coastal form)
   Key colony: The Cabo Marán colony (~240 individuals) is the most visited
               (see Atlantic Coastline section). The largest colony in Zoop is
               at Isla de los Lobos, a small islet 14 km off Playa de las Dunas
               (Bahía Larga province); this colony numbers ~1,800 individuals
               and is part of the Parque Marino Bahía Larga protected area.
   Other colonies: Small groups at the Ensenada de Santa Lucía breakwaters
               (~60 individuals) and off Puerto Canela (~30 individuals).
   Total Zoopan population: ~2,400 individuals (2023 estimate).
   Threats: Entanglement in fishing nets; disturbance at haul-out sites;
            reduced prey availability due to overfishing.

4. Delfín del Marán / Marán river dolphin (Coastal/estuarine species)
   Scientific name: Sotalia maranensis
   Common name: "Delfín del Marán"
   Character: A small coastal and estuarine bottlenose-type dolphin (not a
              true river dolphin) found in the Bahía de Maraná and lower 60 km
              of the Río Marán; pale grey with a pinkish ventral surface;
              body length 1.8–2.2 m. Found in groups of 4–15 individuals;
              feeds on corvina, mullet, and squid in the estuary.
   Population: Approximately 280–340 individuals in the Marán estuary
               population; the species is poorly studied. A 2020 genetic
               survey found the Marán population is genetically distinct
               from offshore coastal dolphins, suggesting it is partially
               isolated — possibly qualifying as a distinct population
               segment warranting additional protection.
   Threats: Vessel collision, net entanglement, and estuary pollution are
            the primary threats.

5. Bandurria del Oeste / Zoopan ibis (Endemic bird)
   Scientific name: Theristicus zoopanus
   Common name: "Bandurria del Oeste" (Western ibis)
   Character: A large (75–85 cm) ibis of the Sierra del Oeste foothills and
              upper pampa margins; bronze-brown back, white chest, long
              decurved bill; distinctive honking call audible 1 km away.
              The Bandurria del Oeste is a Zoopan near-endemic — closely
              related to the Black-faced ibis of Chile and Argentina but
              with differences in plumage and habitat use that taxonomists
              recognize as species-level separation. Found primarily between
              1,000 and 3,000 m in the foothills; nests in loose colonies in
              queñoa woodland.
   Population: Estimated 14,000–18,000 individuals; stable; not threatened.
   Cultural note: The Bandurria is the unofficial symbol of the Provincia de
                  Valles del Oeste; its call is said to predict weather
                  changes (a piece of Zopikí ecological knowledge now studied
                  by meteorologists as an early indicator of approaching fronts).

6. Flamenco zoopano / Zoopan flamingo (Near-endemic subspecies)
   Scientific name: Phoenicopterus chilensis zoopanus
   Common name: "Flamenco zoopano" (established in 0-B, Bahía Larga section)
   Character: A subspecies of the Chilean flamingo with slightly deeper pink
              coloring and a more restricted range; the primary wintering
              population uses Bahía Larga (established in 0-B); breeding
              occurs on remote high-altitude salares in Pequeé Norte province
              near the Salar de Tupanaku.
   Population: Approximately 9,000–12,000 individuals winter in Bahía Larga;
               the breeding population on the salares is estimated at 6,000–
               8,000 pairs but is poorly counted (access difficulties at
               high-altitude breeding sites).
   Threats: The Salar de Tupanaku lithium mining operation's water use is a
            documented concern for flamingo breeding; brine pumping has altered
            the hydrology of two flamingo breeding lagoons adjacent to the
            salar, reducing water depth below the threshold for successful nest
            construction in 2021 and 2022. Conservation groups have filed
            objections with the Secretaría de Medio Ambiente.

Conservation Success Story:
   The Andean condor recovery (described above) is Zoop's most cited conservation
   success. A more recent and celebrated success is the guanaco del Marán
   recolonization of the Parque Nacional Llanuras del Sur: the park's 2009
   reintroduction program (32 individuals translocated from a wild population
   in Las Cumbres province) had grown to an established herd of ~480 guanacos
   within the park by 2024 — the first self-sustaining guanaco population in the
   Pampa Central in over 80 years. The program won the Premios Aranduy de
   Conservación (Zoop's national conservation award) in 2023.
```

### Río Marán Water Quality (by industry/province)

```
Province: Río Hondo
  Pollution source: Copper tailings from Mina Cóndor and legacy operations;

  Contaminants: Copper, arsenic, cadmium; elevated in Lago Aranduy Alta sediments.

  Contaminants: Nitrates (exceeds standard in dry-flow conditions), E. coli,
```

### Lithium Mining Water Use

```
The Salar de Tupanaku operation's brine pumping draws from the same deep aquifer
system that indigenous communities and highland ecosystems depend on:

- Aquifer drawdown confirmed: The CLT's own 2019 study acknowledged 1.2 m drawdown
  since operations began; independent monitoring by UNPN found 2.8 m.
- Ojos de agua (natural springs) that have gone dry since 2019: at least 3 confirmed
  cases in the Alta Tupanaku area used by the Comunidad Tupanaku.
- Flamingo breeding impact: Two flamingo breeding lagoons adjacent to the salar have
  had inadequate water depth for successful nesting in 2021 and 2022.
- Groundwater model: The Secretaría de Medio Ambiente's 2022 groundwater model
  projects that at the 2030 production target (120,000 tonnes LCE/year), aquifer
  drawdown could reach 6–9 m by 2035, potentially permanently altering the
  high-altitude wetland hydrology.
- CLT's response: The company has implemented a partial brine reinjection program
  (injecting processed, delithiated brine back into the aquifer) since 2023;
  early data suggests this reduces net drawdown by ~30% but does not eliminate it.
- Government position: President Carballo's government (2023–present) has stated
  it will not halt lithium operations but has required CLT to fund an independent
  10-year hydrogeological monitoring program; environmental groups and the Zopikí
  communities consider this insufficient.
```

### Agricultural Deforestation

```
Gallery forest loss along river corridors:
  - The Río Marán, Río Pequeé, and their tributaries once supported a continuous
    gallery forest (bosque en galería) of ceibo zoopano, willow (sauce criollo),
    and algarrobo zoopano along their banks.
  - By 2020, an estimated 78% of the original gallery forest in the Pampa Central
    river corridors had been cleared for agriculture and cattle.
  - The clearance began in the late 19th century but accelerated sharply with the
    soy expansion of the 1990s–2000s; land owners cleared gallery strips that
    were previously considered marginal agricultural land.
  - Consequences: Accelerated bank erosion, increased sediment loads in rivers,
    loss of shade cooling that maintained water temperature for fish, loss of
    nesting habitat for riparian birds.
  - Restoration: The NGO Bosques del Marán (founded 2004) has replanted 8,200 ha
    of gallery forest (2025); the Programa de Saneamiento del Marán provides
    limited funding for landowner incentives to maintain 20-m buffer strips.
  - Deforestation rate: Currently slowing (from ~12,000 ha/year cleared in 2005–2010
    to ~4,000 ha/year in 2020–2024) but the remaining native vegetation is largely
    in fragmented patches.
```

### Coastal Erosion and Fishery Depletion

```
Coastal erosion:
  - The Bahía de Maraná delta has been retreating landward at an average rate of
    2.3 m/year since the construction of Represa Las Totoras (1993); the reduced
    sediment load reaching the coast no longer replenishes the barrier beaches and
    delta mudflats at the rate they erode.
  - Beach erosion at Playa del Cabo (Puerto Marán) has required three artificial
    nourishment (sand dumping) projects since 2005; total cost ~ZPS 340 million.
  - Sea level rise (current rate ~3.5 mm/year measured at Puerto Marán tide gauge
    since 1973) compounds the erosion problem.

Fishery depletion:
  - Hake (merluza) stock on the Banco Marán: The 2023 stock assessment found the
    spawning biomass at 41% of the Maximum Sustainable Yield reference level — the
    lowest recorded. A voluntary catch reduction of 20% was agreed in 2024 between
    the Zoopan fishing industry association (AFIZ) and the Secretaría de Pesca.
  - Squid: Populations appear stable but are subject to large inter-annual
    variability; a poor 2022 squid season (catch -38% of average) triggered
    significant economic hardship in Puerto Viejo fishing communities.
  - Foreign fleet incursions: Chinese, Spanish, and South Korean fleets operating
    near Zoop's EEZ boundary exacerbate pressure on hake and squid stocks.
```

### Climate Change Vulnerabilities

```
Most vulnerable regions:

1. Pampa Central (drought risk):
   - IPCC-consistent regional climate projections for Zoop (Zoopan Meteorological
     Service, 2022) project a 15–25% reduction in summer rainfall in the western
     pampa sub-zone by 2050.
   - Drought frequency in Llanuras del Centro: current ~1 year in 4 is projected
     to become ~1 year in 2 by 2060.
   - Agricultural adaptation: shift toward drought-tolerant crop varieties; the
     seed lobby has lobbied for GMO soy approval (controversial); water-metering
     of the Acuífero Pampero is increasingly urgent.

2. Sierra del Oeste (glacier loss, water supply):
   - The Glaciar Helado (source of the Río Marán) is retreating; projections
     suggest it may disappear by 2060–2070 at current rates.
   - Reduced glacial melt will decrease dry-season base flows in the upper Marán;
     the Represa Mariano Aranduy reservoir could face critical low-level events
     more frequently.

3. Atlantic coast (sea level rise, storm intensity):
   - Bahía de Maraná delta and Bahía Larga lagoon system are most vulnerable.
   - A 1 m sea level rise scenario (possible by 2100) would inundate approximately
     28% of the Bahía Larga lagoon system's barrier beach complex.
   - Puerto Marán's lowest-lying industrial districts (including the petroleum
     refinery) are at risk of increased tidal flooding; a climate adaptation
     infrastructure plan was commissioned in 2022 but not yet funded.

4. Los Esteros wetlands (rainfall pattern shifts):
   - The Los Esteros wetland system (northeastern Pampa Central) depends on
     predictable wet-season flooding; changing rainfall distribution threatens
     the flood-pulse ecology of the wetlands.
```

### Major Droughts

```
Drought of 1977–1978 ("La Seca Grande"):
  Affected provinces: Llanuras del Centro, Aranduy, San Bartolomé, Quilmes del Sur
  Duration: 14 months of consecutive below-normal rainfall (May 1977–July 1978)
  Agricultural impact: Wheat yield fell 52% in Llanuras del Centro; cattle
                       mortality reached an estimated 340,000 head nationally;
                       total economic loss estimated at ZPS 1.8 billion (1978 values).
  Human impact: Rural unemployment in the pampa provinces drove internal migration
                to Puerto Marán and Zoopaná; the La Crisis of the military government's
                economic credibility began in part with this drought.
  Government response: Inadequate under the military government; emergency grain
                       imports from Argentina; the drought accelerated discussions
                       about the Represa Mariano Aranduy's importance for irrigation
                       (the dam had just been completed in 1974).

Drought of 2008–2009 ("El Año Seco"):
  Affected provinces: Primarily Llanuras del Centro, Los Esteros, and eastern San
                      Bartolomé; milder in other pampa provinces.
  Duration: El Niño-linked; 8 consecutive months below normal (December 2008–
            July 2009).
  Agricultural impact: Soy production -41%; wheat -29%; total sector loss
                       estimated USD 1.2 billion at 2009 values.
  Water supply: The Acuífero Pampero water table dropped to record lows in three
                monitoring stations in Llanuras del Centro; 14 rural towns
                required emergency water trucking.
  Legacy: The 2008–2009 drought is the primary reference point used in all
          subsequent Pampa Central water-security policy discussions.
```

### Seismic Activity

```
The Sierra del Oeste lies on the eastern edge of the Andean subduction zone;
Zoop is moderately seismically active, primarily in the western provinces.

Major recorded earthquakes:
1. Terremoto de Río Hondo (1944):
   Magnitude: 6.8 Mw
   Epicenter: Approximately 40 km northeast of Hondo (Río Hondo province),
              at ~12 km depth.
   Impact: Significant structural damage in Hondo (then a much smaller mining
           town); 43 deaths; several mine tunnels collapsed in the Río Hondo
           gorge; landslides temporarily blocked the Arroyo Cóndor.
   Significance: This event prompted the first geological hazard assessment
                 of the Cordillera Tupanaku; the assessment was later cited
                 in the environmental approval process for the Represa Mariano
                 Aranduy (which was specifically designed with seismic safety
                 margins for an M7.0 event).

2. Terremoto de Cerro Verde (1998):
   Magnitude: 5.9 Mw
   Epicenter: 18 km west of Cerro Verde (Pequeé Norte province); shallow
              focal depth (~8 km).
   Impact: Moderate structural damage in Cerro Verde; 7 deaths; minor landslides
           on the approach to Paso Internacional Colinas Verdes (briefly closed
           for road clearing); some damage to the Mina Cóndor surface infrastructure.
   Significance: Prompted upgrades to Cerro Verde's building code; the event
                 accelerated the geological survey of the Cordillera Pequeé
                 that later identified the lithium-bearing brine formations
                 under the Salar de Tupanaku.

Seismic risk assessment (current):
  - The Comisión Geológica Nacional (CGN) rates the Sierra del Oeste provinces
    (Pequeé Norte, Las Cumbres, Río Hondo) as Zone III (moderate-high seismic
    hazard) out of four zones.
  - The Represa Mariano Aranduy has been assessed for M7.5 event resilience
    as part of its 2022–2027 upgrade; engineers confirmed structural adequacy.
  - No M7.0+ event has been recorded in Zoop since records began in 1880.
```
