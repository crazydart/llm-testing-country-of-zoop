## SECTION 1 — City Overview

### Official Name and Name History

The city was founded on **12 November 1551** as **Villa de San Martín de Zoopaná**
by Don Rodrigo de Espinosa y Valdés, the first Gobernador de la Provincia del
Río Marán. The name honored both the patron saint of the founding date (Feast of
Saint Martin of Tours) and the indigenous Zupakk toponym already used by the
Zopikí Confederation for the confluence zone.

Through the colonial and early independence periods the city was commonly
shortened to "Zoopaná" in colloquial usage and most commercial correspondence.
The formal constitutional shortening occurred by **Decreto Presidencial No. 14 of
7 March 1862**, issued by President Eugenio Marcelino Dávila Herrero immediately
following the adoption of the **Constitution of 1862**. The decree declared that
"in all official instruments, the capital shall henceforth be styled simply
*Zoopaná*, the name by which it is universally known among the people of the
Republic." The 1875 Constitution codified this in its text, making "Zoopaná" the
sole official form.

### Location

Zoopaná sits on the **left (north) bank of the Río Marán** at approximately
**kilometer 610 from the river's source** — that is, the city center lies roughly
930 km from the Atlantic mouth, well above the navigable limit for oceangoing
vessels (which reaches only to Corrientes del Marán, ~390 km from the coast).
The Río Marán at Zoopaná runs northeast-to-southwest; the old colonial center is
built on the highest ground above the river's flood terrace.

The city is situated at the **convergence of the Río Marán and the Arroyo de los
Ceibos**, a short tributary that enters from the north. This secondary confluence
echoes the Zopikí tradition of settlement at "where rivers run together" and is
the geographical reason the Zopikí community of Zupakka established its center
here centuries earlier.

**Coordinates (approximate):** 33°10'S, 60°35'W (no real-world location intended;
these place Zoopaná in interior pampa-to-foothills transitional zone, consistent
with the seed document's geography).

### Elevation

**Elevation:** 182 m above sea level (city center, at Plaza de la Independencia).
The city proper rises to approximately 240 m at its western residential heights
(Barrio Altamira) and drops to approximately 140 m at the riverfront (Costanera
del Marán). This modest elevation sits just above the flat pampa, which is why
Zoopaná's founders chose the site — the terrace provided flood protection that
the surrounding lowlands lacked.

### Population

```
City proper (Zoopaná, within official district boundaries): 3,400,000
Metropolitan area (Distrito Autónomo + commuter municipalities of
  Arroyo Ceibos, San Isidro del Marán, Colonia Aranduy, Villa
  Nueva del Río, Parque Industrial Norte): 3,550,000
Greater metro statistical region (including exurban zones): ~3.7 million
```

The city grew from approximately 5,000 at founding (1551) to:
- ~15,000 by the 1810 independence junta
- ~60,000 at the 1875 Constitution
- ~250,000 by 1900 (Liberal Era growth)
- ~900,000 by 1950
- ~2,200,000 by 1980
- ~3,400,000 today (2024 estimate)

### Climate

Zoopaná falls within **Clima Costero** (Coastal Climate, established in Session 0-B),
though its interior location means it experiences more continental extremes than
the coastline proper.

```
Annual mean temperature: 17.4°C
Summer (December–February): mean 24°C; highs frequently 32–36°C; humid
Autumn (March–May): pleasantly mild 16–21°C; most rainfall
Winter (June–August): mean 10°C; overnight lows to 2–4°C; frost 2–5 nights/yr;
                       the garúa (coastal fog) reaches Zoopaná in winter months,
                       making the city grey and damp for weeks at a time
Spring (September–November): warming rapidly; blooming season for ceibo zoopano
Annual rainfall: 1,020 mm; evenly distributed with slight autumn peak
Prevailing wind: pampero (cold southwesterly) in winter; norteño (warm northerly)
                  in summer; locals distinguish roughly 9 named local wind variants
Signature weather: grey winter fog and spring thunderstorms of unusual intensity;
                   Zoopaná's fogs are referenced in literature going back to the 1840s
```

## SECTION 2 — Neighborhoods (13 named)

---

### 1. Centro Histórico
*(The Historic Center)*

```
Character: Colonial heart; tourist core; administrative and religious center
Historical origin: The original urban grid laid out by Don Rodrigo de Espinosa y
  Valdés in 1551; the Plaza Mayor (now Plaza de la Independencia) was its nucleus;
  the grid used the standard Spanish colonial plan of a central plaza with streets
  running perpendicular to it; the original four blocks around the plaza were built
  in colonial masonry or stone
Key streets: Calle del Fundador (east side of plaza), Calle San Martín (north),
             Calle de la Catedral (south), Avenida de la Independencia (running
             west from the plaza)
Notable for: Colonial architecture including the Cathedral, Casa de Gobierno,
             and the Convento de San Francisco; densely touristed by day; popular
             restaurants and galleries occupy 18th-century buildings
Approximate resident population: 28,000 (residential population is small; most
  buildings are commercial or institutional; true residents are concentrated in
  upper floors of refurbished colonial-era buildings)
Challenges: Gentrification pressure, flooding risk at lower edges near river,
            traffic congestion on narrow colonial-era streets
```

### 2. Barrio de la Misión
*(The Mission Quarter)*

```
Character: Historic; bohemian; mixed-income; arts district
Historical origin: The site of the Misión de Nuestra Señora de la Paz de Zupakka
  (the largest Jesuit mission in the region, active until the Jesuit expulsion of
  1767). After Jesuit expulsion the mission buildings were converted to various uses;
  Zopikí families displaced from the mission system settled around the old mission
  walls. The neighborhood retains the irregular street pattern of the mission-era
  layout, distinct from the surrounding colonial grid.
Key streets: Calle de la Misión (follows the old mission perimeter wall),
             Pasaje Zupakka (narrow pedestrian alley; most photographed street in city)
Notable for: The best surviving mission-era wall section (restored 1988;
             national monument); a thriving gallery and ceramics market on weekends;
             the Museo de la Comunidad Zopikí (a small neighborhood museum run
             by the Zopikí cultural association; not to be confused with Centro
             Cultural Zopikí); Zoopaná's most famous food market (Mercado de la
             Misión; established 1843)
Approximate resident population: 42,000
Challenges: Tension between artists/tourists and long-established Zopikí-heritage
            families; the Zopikí Cultural Association has repeatedly contested
            development proposals
```

### 3. Barrio de Palacio
*(The Palace Quarter / Government & Diplomatic Quarter)*

```
Character: Government and diplomatic; formal; well-maintained
Historical origin: The area immediately surrounding the Casa de Gobierno
  (Governor's Palace, 1553) developed as the administrative and military
  quarter of the colonial city. After independence it became the site of
  the new republic's institutions; throughout the 19th century the area
  was gradually cleared and formalized to create the grand institutional
  streetscape seen today.
Key streets: Paseo de los Presidentes (the ceremonial boulevard running
             from the Plaza de la Independencia to the Palacio Aranduy
             main entrance), Calle de los Ministerios (lined with ministry
             buildings in the 1890s–1910s Neoclassical style)
Notable for: The Palacio Aranduy (executive palace), the Ministerio de
             Relaciones Exteriores (Foreign Ministry; 1898 building), the
             Ministerio de Economía (1912), numerous embassy residences;
             low residential population; heavy security presence;
             ceremonial use on national holidays
Approximate resident population: 8,000 (mostly embassy staff and
  security personnel)
Challenges: Frozen in institutional amber; very little social life beyond
            office hours; debated as underused urban land
```

### 4. Barrio Rivadavia
*(Italian Immigrant Quarter)*

```
Character: Historic Italian immigrant neighborhood; now middle-class residential
           with excellent restaurants
Historical origin: The Liberal Era (1870–1916) brought an estimated 45,000
  Italian immigrants to Zoopaná (primarily from Liguria, Calabria, and Veneto).
  They settled in the blocks northwest of the Centro Histórico, building tenement
  housing (*conventillos*) and establishing the workshops, bakeries, and small
  factories that made this the city's working artisan quarter. Named after Rivadavia
  Street, which was renamed from Calle de la Fábrica in 1872 after the great
  Argentine statesman as a gesture of Pan-American liberal solidarity.
Key streets: Calle Rivadavia (the main commercial spine), Vía Genovesa (a
             pedestrian street lined with restaurants; named for Genoa),
             Calle dei Tessituri ("Weavers' Street" in Italian; preserved as
             a heritage name)
Notable for: The Iglesia Madonna del Marán (small Italian immigrant church,
             1891; unofficial name; officially Santa María del Marán); the
             best pasta restaurants in the city (particularly celebrated:
             Trattoria da Concetti and Osteria Zanollo, both founded in the
             1920s); an annual Sagra della Pasta festival in February; the
             Circolo Italiano (Italian community club, 1883; still active);
             a gentrifying but still-authentic character
Approximate resident population: 67,000
Challenges: Third-generation Italian families moving to the suburbs; replaced
            by young professionals and students seeking proximity to the Centro;
            rising rents
```

### 5. Barrio Altamira
*(Old Money Residential)*

```
Character: Affluent; established; quiet residential
Historical origin: From the 1880s onward, Zoopaná's creole elite and successful
  Liberal Era merchants moved away from the increasingly crowded Centro Histórico
  to a genteel residential suburb on the city's western heights. Altamira
  (named for a real estate developer, Don Ernesto Altamira y Cossio, who
  subdivided the first lots in 1884) developed as a district of large family
  houses with gardens — the Zoopan equivalent of Buenos Aires's Palermo or
  Recoleta, though quieter.
Key streets: Avenida Altamira (broad, tree-lined boulevard; many embassies
             and private clinics), Calle del Bosquecillo (leads to the
             Parque Mirador del Marán), Paseo de las Rosas (famous for the
             private gardens that back onto it)
Notable for: The largest concentration of late 19th-century and early 20th-century
             residential architecture in the city; the Parque Mirador del Marán
             (a hilltop park with the best view of the river valley below;
             established 1889); Club Altamira (private sports and social club,
             1891; an old-money institution); Colegio San Martín de Tours
             (the city's most prestigious private secondary school, founded 1876,
             located on Avenida Altamira)
Approximate resident population: 54,000
Challenges: Aging population; large houses subdivided into flats or converted
            to consulate residences; parking gridlock around the private schools
            during school hours
```

### 6. Barrio Universidad
*(University Quarter)*

```
Character: Young; vibrant; politically engaged; cafés and bookshops
Historical origin: The Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná (UNZ), founded 1830,
  was originally housed in repurposed colonial buildings near the Convento
  de San Francisco. As it expanded through the Liberal Era, the university
  acquired land to the south of the Centro Histórico, and the surrounding
  neighborhood developed as a student and intellectual district. By the 1920s
  it was the center of Zoopaná's political and literary life.
Key streets: Avenida Universidad (the main campus boulevard), Calle de los
             Estudiantes (pedestrianized; bookshops and cafés for 6 blocks),
             Calle Palma Torrontegui (named for the UNZ's first rector)
Notable for: The UNZ main campus (monumental 1890s buildings; original colonial
             building survives as the Aula Magna); the Biblioteca Nacional de
             Zoop (national library; 1908 building, Art Nouveau facade); café
             culture — Bar Confluencia has been the intellectual watering hole
             since 1934; the student-run newspaper *El Mirador* (founded 1919;
             suppressed by every military government; revived 1985); murals
             covering many walls (added post-1985 as democratic expression);
             Instituto Universitario de Arte Zoopano (IUAZ); second university
             campus (Universidad Católica de Zoopaná)
Approximate resident population: 61,000 (heavily student in proportion)
Challenges: Noise and nightlife tensions with adjacent residential areas;
            housing costs forcing students to the periphery; the murals
            are periodically defaced and repainted (a cultural flashpoint)
```

### 7. Barrio San Lorenzo
*(Working-Class District; Now Gentrifying)*

```
Character: Former industrial/working-class; currently in active gentrification;
           contested
Historical origin: San Lorenzo developed in the early 20th century as workers
  from the city's tanneries, textile factories, and railway workshops settled south
  of the Centro Histórico. Named for the barrio's main church (Parroquia San
  Lorenzo Mártir, 1907). At its mid-century peak, San Lorenzo had a dense network
  of mutual-aid societies, labor unions, and community organizations. The military
  dictatorship's "Years of Lead" (1970–1979) devastated this community — San
  Lorenzo had the highest rate of disappearances of any Zoopaná neighborhood,
  a fact commemorated annually.
Key streets: Avenida de los Obreros ("Workers' Avenue"; named 1924 by the socialist
             city council; retained even under military rule as a concession to
             labor), Calle San Lorenzo, Pasaje de las Industrias (now converted
             to galleries and studios)
Notable for: The Parque de la Memoria Obrera (established 2003; names the 847
             residents of San Lorenzo who disappeared under the dictatorship);
             a thriving street art scene; the Mercado San Lorenzo (converted
             1990s tannery building; now artisan market and food hall); several
             craft breweries; rising property values and conflict between
             long-term residents and new arrivals
Approximate resident population: 88,000
Challenges: Active gentrification displacing working-class families; the
            neighborhood's left-political character clashes with incoming
            professional class; ongoing debate in city council over rent controls
```

### 8. Barrio Financiero
*(Financial / Business District)*

```
Character: Modern; corporate; daytime-intensive; deserted at night
Historical origin: The financial district emerged in the 1950s–1970s as the
  government's developmentalist policies drove construction of office towers along
  the former factory belt east of the Centro Histórico. Significant high-rise
  construction occurred during the military era (1966–1985), giving the district
  an ambivalent architectural heritage. Several towers bear the names of banks
  that no longer exist.
Key streets: Avenida Marán (the main financial boulevard; officially Avenida
             General Mariano Aranduy but universally called "Avenida Marán");
             Calle de los Bancos; Plaza del Comercio (a modern plaza with a
             controversial abstract sculpture)
Notable for: The Torre Zoopana (tallest building in Zoopaná; 42 floors; built 1998;
             glass curtain wall; headquarters of Banco Nacional de Zoop's
             modern successor, the Banco de la República); the Bolsa de Valores
             de Zoopaná (Zoopaná Stock Exchange; founded 1892 in a handsome
             beaux-arts building; current trading floor is modern; old building
             preserved as facade); Plaza del Comercio (meeting point for noon
             protests and street performers on weekdays)
Approximate resident population: 11,000 (nearly all in newer residential
  towers on the district's eastern edge)
Challenges: Becoming a monoculture of finance; proposals to create a "mixed-use"
            planning zone rejected repeatedly by property developers
```

### 9. Barrio Costanera
*(Riverfront District)*

```
Character: Transitional; scenic; contested development zone
Historical origin: The Costanera del Marán (the Marán riverfront) was long an
  industrial and port area — tanneries, river docks, and warehouses occupied
  the waterfront until the decline of river commerce in the mid-20th century.
  Post-dictatorship urban reform projects have progressively converted sections
  of the waterfront to public parks and promenades, but large derelict industrial
  sites remain. A Costanera Norte and Costanera Sur section are distinguished.
Key streets: Paseo de la Costanera (the main riverside promenade; 3.2 km of
             paved walkway and cycle path, completed 2009), Calle del Puerto
             Viejo (retains the name of the old river port, now the entrance
             to the riverfront bar and restaurant strip)
Notable for: The Costanera is Zoopaná's main outdoor recreation space; on
             weekends it fills with cyclists, joggers, and families; the
             Embarcadero del Marán (small river boat terminal; tourist cruises
             up and down the Marán; the one remaining working dock); the
             Puente de la Unidad (the city's main bridge over the Río Marán;
             built 1958; functionalist concrete; a symbol of the city despite
             its ungainliness; the 1975 Puente Aranduy to its west is the
             modern traffic bridge)
Approximate resident population: 32,000 (growing rapidly as new apartment
  developments replace old warehouses; this is Zoopaná's most contested
  development zone)
Challenges: Flooding risk (the Marán regularly overtops the lower sections in
            heavy rainfall; the 1983 La Crecida Negra flood devastated the
            neighborhood); developer pressure; the unresolved question of the
            old Puerto Industrial site (57 hectares of former industrial land)
```

### 10. Barrio Norte
*(Upper-Middle Residential; Café Society)*

```
Character: Comfortable; cosmopolitan; "the neighborhood everyone moves to
           when they've made enough money"
Historical origin: Developed from the 1920s–1950s as a middle-class residential
  expansion to the north of the Centro Histórico; several post-war European
  immigrant communities (Spanish Republicans, some German and Eastern European
  arrivals) settled here.
Key streets: Avenida del Norte (the main commercial boulevard), Calle de
             las Acacias (known for its spring flower display), Paseo
             Primavera (the main outdoor café and restaurant strip)
Notable for: The Mercado del Norte (covered market; 1947 Art Deco building;
             finest produce market in the city); the highest density of
             independent bookshops in Zoopaná; the city's most active
             café life; several concert venues and jazz clubs; the Parroquia
             Nuestra Señora del Pilar (1934; modernist church with an
             excellent Zopikí-inspired mosaic interior, completed 1952)
Approximate resident population: 94,000
Challenges: Property prices now rival Barrio Altamira; described as
            having lost its character to boutique hotels and international
            restaurants; the bookshops are celebrated but precarious
```

### 11. Barrio Villa Nueva
*(Peripheral Internal Migrant Neighborhood)*

```
Character: Working-class; densely populated; internally migrant; informal economy
Historical origin: Villa Nueva emerged from the 1960s onward as rural-to-urban
  migration accelerated. Families from Los Esteros, the Sierra del Oeste, and
  the northern provinces (especially Candelaria) settled in the flatlands 8–12 km
  north of the historic center. The neighborhood has never been fully incorporated
  into the city's formal planning structure; large sections grew as informal
  settlements (*villas de emergencia*) without utilities until the 1990s. The
  Carballo government (2023–present) has a formalization program underway.
Key streets: Avenida de la Esperanza ("Avenue of Hope"; the main commercial
             spine; informal market stalls crowd the sidewalk), Calle del
             Ceibo (where the barrio's community center and health clinic sit)
Notable for: The highest proportion of Zopikí-speaking residents in any Zoopaná
             neighborhood (~18% Zopikí first language); the Feria de Villa
             Nueva (a Sunday market famous for food from Los Esteros and the
             Sierra); the community-built Escuela Popular del Norte (1982;
             built by residents during the dictatorship as an alternative
             education space); the neighborhood's collective social organization
             (the Unión de Vecinos de Villa Nueva has been a political force
             since the 1980s democratic transition)
Approximate resident population: 182,000
Challenges: Infrastructure deficits in older informal sections; lack of
            formal land title for many residents; limited metro connectivity
            (the Line 3 extension to Villa Nueva was announced in 2019 and
            remains unfinished); high informal employment
```

### 12. Barrio Ceibo
*(Middle-Class Residential; Tech and Media Hub)*

```
Character: Modern; professional; Zoopaná's emerging "innovation district"
Historical origin: Ceibo was developed as planned middle-class residential in the
  1970s (during the military era), with standardized apartment blocks replacing
  earlier low-density housing. Post-democratization it attracted media companies
  (several national television and radio broadcasters based here), then from the
  2010s the software services sector. Named for the national flower (ceibo zoopano).
Key streets: Avenida Ceibo (the main avenue; media company headquarters line it),
             Calle de las Antenas (the informal name for the block where the
             national broadcasters' antenna towers cluster)
Notable for: The Zoopan public television and radio broadcaster HQ (RTZP —
             Radio Televisión de la República Zoopana; founded 1958); the
             Parque Tecnológico Zoopaná (tech park; est. 2012; ~85 software
             firms); the best public secondary schools in the city; relatively
             new construction with decent infrastructure
Approximate resident population: 79,000
Challenges: Traffic congestion during morning and evening commutes to/from
            the Centro; few cultural amenities of note compared to older barrios
```

### 13. Barrio de los Olivos
*(Quiet Southern Residential; Upper-Middle; University Staff)*

```
Character: Leafy; quiet; family-oriented; high proportion of university faculty
           and civil servants
Historical origin: Developed from the 1940s–1960s as academics at the UNZ and
  civil servants from the expanding national bureaucracy sought residential
  areas close to work but quieter than the Centro. Named for the olive trees
  planted along its main street in the 1950s.
Key streets: Paseo de los Olivos (the olive-tree boulevard; the calming visual
             heart of the neighborhood), Calle Palma Torrontegui (shared name
             with the street in Barrio Universidad; this stretch is the
             older, quieter section)
Notable for: The highest concentration of secondary schools in the city; a
             well-established farmer's market on Saturdays; the Biblioteca
             Barrial de los Olivos (neighborhood library; one of 14 branch
             libraries in the city; holds a significant Zoopan literature
             collection); the Parque de los Olivos (formal gardens; band
             concerts in summer)
Approximate resident population: 56,000
Challenges: Aging infrastructure; seen as "boring" by younger residents;
            nonetheless beloved by its inhabitants for its predictable calm
```

## SECTION 3 — Major Landmarks

### Plaza de la Independencia
*(Formerly Plaza Mayor)*

```
Dimensions: ~120 m × 80 m (established at founding 1551; dimensions locked
            from Session 1-A)
Orientation: The long axis runs east-west; the cathedral occupies the south
             side; the Palacio Aranduy (Casa de Gobierno) the west side;
             the Congreso Nacional building the north side; the Cabildo
             (Museo de la Fundación) the east side.
Surface: Paved in cream-colored limestone slabs (repaved 1989; the 1875
         paving was recorded in photographs); large ceibo zoopano trees in
         four quadrants; central fountain (the Fuente de los Héroes; cast
         iron; installed 1878; depicts the four founding figures of the
         republic symbolically)
Central monument: Equestrian statue of General Mariano Pelayo Aranduy;
  bronze; 4.8 m on a 3 m granite plinth; unveiled 1 October 1876 (Liberal
  Era centennial project); sculptor: Don Pedro Carrucci (Italian-born;
  the first major public commission by an immigrant sculptor in Zoopaná);
  Aranduy faces west, toward the hills from which the royalists came
Secondary monument: The Mausoleo de los Fundadores occupies the
  southeastern corner; a neoclassical marble structure (1872); holds the
  remains of Aranduy and three other founding figures; flanked by an
  eternal flame (added 1960; maintained by the Presidential Guard)
Secondary plaque: A bronze plaque on the north face of the Cabildo building
  marks the exact window of the Salón de Actas where the 1810 junta
  declaration was read aloud to the people gathered in the plaza below
Use today: The most important civic space in the republic; National Day
  (23 August) ceremonies are centered here; the annual Te Deum at the
  Cathedral is followed by a march across the plaza; used for concerts,
  markets, and political demonstrations; permanently guarded by two
  sentinels at the Mausoleo
```

### Palacio Aranduy (Casa de Gobierno)
*(The Presidential Palace)*

```
Official name: Palacio de Gobierno de la República de Zoop
Common name: "Palacio Aranduy" (informal but universally used; the name
             became so entrenched after 1876 that it appears in official
             documents as "commonly known as the Palacio Aranduy")
Historical origin: Founded 1553 as the Governor's Palace (Casa de Gobierno)
  by Don Rodrigo de Espinosa y Valdés; continuous use as executive seat
  to the present (longest continuously operating executive building in
  South America, a claim made in Zoopan tourism materials)
40% original masonry: The western wall of the palace and parts of the
  north wing retain the original colonial masonry (irregular limestone
  block; visible distinction from later stucco-covered sections)
Major building phases:
  - 1553–1580: Original single-storey colonial structure; mudejar
    roof elements; thick-walled against heat
  - 1640–1660: Two-storey expansion; new reception hall built
  - 1791–1798: Major Bourbon-era renovation; neoclassical elements
    added; the current main courtyard (Patio de los Laureles) dates
    to this period; 14 laurel trees, one per province (updated in
    1862 and 1895 as new provinces were created)
  - 1898–1910: Liberal Era grand facade; current public face of the
    palace; cream limestone with Ionic pilasters; portico with 6 columns
    (added 1904; named the "Pórtico de la República"); the golden sun
    emblem inset above the main entrance (Zopikí Inti-Zupaka motif;
    added 1907; the first major official use of the sun symbol in state
    architecture)
  - 1962–1965: Modernization of interior; air conditioning; the
    executive residential wing reconstructed
  - 1985–1988: Post-dictatorship restoration; original 1553 masonry
    sections given heritage protection status
Size: Approximately 11,000 m² of built space; occupies half a city block
      on the west side of Plaza de la Independencia
Public access: The Plaza de la Independencia facade and Pórtico de la
  República are open to the public; the Patio de los Laureles is open
  for 2 hours each morning (Monday–Saturday) as part of the "Palacio
  Abierto" program instituted by President Peñaloza Villafuertes in 2005;
  the west wing including the state reception rooms is accessible on
  guided tours by appointment; the residential and working floors are
  not accessible
Changing of the Guard: Daily ceremony at 09:00 and 12:00 at the Pórtico
  de la República; the Presidential Guard (*Guardia Presidencial*) wears
  the historic 1832-era uniform (dark blue with gold braid; plumed shako)
  for the ceremony; the guard was disbanded 1966–1985 (military era) and
  revived on 28 February 1985 (the first day of the Marqués government)
Famous events in the palace:
  - Aranduy died here, 4 March 1833
  - The 1858 Second Civil War armistice was signed in the Sala de los
    Fundadores
  - The military coup of 15 June 1966 ended with General Robaina's
    forces entering through the Pórtico de la República
  - President Cossio Peralta announced the 2002 peso devaluation from
    the balcony overlooking the plaza (3 February 2002; a night of
    riots in the plaza followed)
  - President Marqués signed the 1985 Reconciliation Decree in the
    Patio de los Laureles on 15 March 1985
```

### Catedral de San Martín de Tours

```
Full name: Catedral Metropolitana de San Martín de Tours de Zoopaná
Foundation: 12 November 1551 (the founding date of the city itself; the
  first mass was held in a temporary wooden structure on this day)
Stone church: completed 1641 (replaced the earlier adobe structure)
Baroque limestone facade: 1712 (the current face visible from the plaza)
UNESCO World Heritage Candidate: nominated 2018; decision pending
Architectural notes:
  - Three naves; barrel-vaulted ceilings; five side chapels
  - The 1641 nave walls and columns are the oldest surviving stone
    construction in the country
  - The facade (1712): High Baroque limestone, with carved figures of
    San Martín and the beggar (the iconic episode from the saint's life);
    two bell towers added 1744–1749; the right tower 4 m shorter than
    the left (construction funds ran short; never completed to original
    plan; a beloved city eccentricity)
  - The 1724 altarpiece: Gold-gilded carved wood; Central panel depicts
    San Martín de Tours sharing his cloak; flanking panels have a
    syncretic Zopikí sun motif — the golden sun of Inti-Zupaka — worked
    into the decorative border; the blending of Catholic iconography with
    Zopikí solar symbolism was commissioned by Bishop Cristóbal Navarro
    de Ibarra and is considered the finest example of colonial religious
    syncretism in the country
Tombs: Originally Aranduy was interred here 1833–1872 (remains removed
  to the Mausoleo); the tomb of Bishop Navarro de Ibarra (died 1731)
  remains; the 14 original junta signatories are commemorated by a
  bronze tablet near the south entrance (installed 1910)
Organs: The main pipe organ dates to 1889 (German-built; Walcker firm);
  a smaller baroque organ (1743; the oldest musical instrument in Zoop)
  is in the north chapel; both remain in playing condition
Role today: Seat of the Archbishop of Zoopaná; active parish; major
  state occasions (Te Deum on 23 August, presidential inaugurations
  begin with a mass here); open to visitors daily; guided tours
  available in Spanish and Zopikí
```

### Congreso Nacional (Building)

```
Official name of building: Palacio Legislativo de la Nación
Common reference: "El Congreso" or "El Legislativo"
Location: North side of Plaza de la Independencia (facing the cathedral)
Historical notes: The colonial Cabildo building originally occupied the north
  side of the plaza; it was partially demolished in 1875 when the current
  legislature building was planned. The new building was constructed
  1876–1892 (16 years; several architectural plans revised). The eastern
  section of the original Cabildo was preserved and is now the Museo de
  la Fundación de la República.
Architecture: Eclectic neoclassical; principal influence is the French Second
  Empire style; a large central dome (the green copper dome visible from
  most of the city center; 52 m from ground to lantern tip); the two
  legislative chambers occupy the dome's north and south wings respectively
Facade: White Italian marble imported 1885–1890; eight columns of the
  Corinthian order; the frieze above the colonnade depicts "Los Pueblos
  del Río Marán" — a sculptural allegory of Zoop's provinces paying
  tribute to the nation; designed by sculptor Arnoldo Ferretti (1888;
  Italian-born; trained in Rome)
Interior notable features:
  - The Salón de Honor (the grand ceremonial hall under the dome):
    decorated with murals by Edmundo Quiroga Saraví (1904–1909; the
    most significant painted interior in the country); the murals depict
    the four great eras of Zoopan history — pre-Columbian, colonial,
    independence, and Liberal Era
  - The Senate chamber (south wing): original mahogany benches from 1892;
    42 seats; the VP's chair is placed on a raised dais of quebracho wood
    (national hardwood)
  - The Chamber of Deputies (north wing): rebuilt 1962 to accommodate 180
    members; modernist intervention criticized by architectural historians
    but popular with deputies for its acoustics
Opening: First session of Congress in the new building: 1 March 1892;
  President Acuña Pittini (#11) presided
Public access: Gallery access to both chambers when in session; Salón de
  Honor open for guided tours on non-session days; the dome is accessible
  to the public on the first Sunday of each month (the "Domingo del
  Congreso" program)
Famous moments: General Robaina's coup of 15 June 1966 saw tanks
  positioned in the plaza with cannon trained on the Palacio Legislativo;
  the restoration of democracy on 28 February 1985 was announced by
  General Valdés Merino from the steps of this building
```

### Teatro Manzano
*(The Opera House)*

```
Full official name: Teatro Nacional Ruperto Manzano
Common name: "El Manzano" or simply "el teatro" (in Zoopaná)
Named for: President Ruperto Hilario Manzano Goicoechea (#12; 1898–1906),
  who championed the building's construction as a centerpiece of the Liberal
  Era cultural program; he did not live to see its completion; the naming
  was a posthumous honor by the subsequent administration
Location: Avenida de la Independencia, 4 blocks west of Plaza de la
  Independencia; Barrio Universidad / Centro Histórico border
Construction: Groundbreaking 1902; opened 14 September 1912 (National Day
  of the Constitution; a deliberate symbolic choice)
Architect: Federico Lozano Parodi (1858–1941; Zoopan-born; trained in Paris
  at the École des Beaux-Arts 1879–1885; also designed the Banco Nacional
  de Zoop headquarters building and the Estación Central railway station)
Architecture: Belle Époque / Beaux-Arts; cream stone facade with Italian
  marble detailing; a horseshoe-plan auditorium with four tiers of boxes
  and a total seating of 1,680; the ceiling fresco ("Alegoría de las Artes
  Zoopanas") was painted by Ramón Fuentes-Neira (1912–1914; the last major
  work of the Liberal Era); the notorious "golden curtain" (a hand-embroidered
  velvet curtain with gold thread, made by nuns of the Convento de Santa
  Clara, 1911; now preserved in the Museo de Bellas Artes as too fragile
  for regular use; replaced by a crimson velvet replica)
Opening production: Aída (Verdi); conducted by Maestro Lorenzo Castiglione
  (Italian guest conductor); the soprano was Carmelita Alvarado de Vega
  (Zoopaná-born; trained in Milan; the first internationally recognized
  Zoopan opera singer; she later performed at La Scala)
Troubled history:
  - Closed 1930–1934 (Faría military government cut cultural funding)
  - Used as military barracks 1966–1970 (Robaina coup; the damage
    to the interior required a major restoration in 1984–1985)
  - Nearly sold to a hotel developer in 1982 (the Zoopan architectural
    community's successful campaign against this sale is credited with
    launching Zoopaná's heritage preservation movement)
Major renovation: 1984–1987; restored to 1912 specifications with modern
  stage machinery; 2019 second renovation (acoustics, accessibility,
  air handling); current capacity 1,680 (main house) + 280 (Sala Velázquez
  Sarabia, the small studio theatre named for the national anthem composer)
Current programming: The Teatro Manzano is home to:
  - Orquesta Sinfónica de Zoopaná (OSZ; founded 1897; 98 musicians;
    principal season September–July)
  - Ballet Nacional de Zoop (BNZ; founded 1954; 48 dancers; shares the
    main stage)
  - Opera season: 6 full productions per year, September–April; mix of
    European repertoire and Zoopan commissioned works; the annual
    "Ciclo Americano" in January features Latin American operas
    exclusively (a program begun 2001)
  - Chamber music series in the Sala Velázquez Sarabia: weekly, year-round
Reputation: Considered the finest acoustic venue in Zoop; competes with
  Puerto Marán's Teatro del Puerto for prestige; internationally, it is
  moderately well-known through touring productions and recordings by the OSZ
Annual gala: The Gala de San Martín (12 November; the city's founding
  anniversary) is always held at the Manzano; black-tie; tickets sell out
  in advance; broadcast nationally on RTZP
```

### Estación Central Zoopaná
*(The Main Railway Station)*

```
Name: Estación Central de Zoopaná
Common reference: "La Central"
Location: Barrio San Lorenzo / Barrio Financiero border; 6 blocks east
  of the Plaza de la Independencia
Architect: Federico Lozano Parodi (same architect as the Teatro Manzano;
  the two buildings are considered his masterworks; Estación Central
  built 1908–1915)
Architecture: Beaux-Arts; the main headhouse features a 30 m glass-and-iron
  barrel-vaulted train shed (the "Gran Cúpula"); facade of honey-colored
  limestone with a central clock tower (the clock, installed 1916, is the
  most photographed in the city)
Historical role: The hub of Zoop's national rail network at its 1920s–1940s
  peak; 22 platforms at maximum operation; served coastal, pampa, and
  Andean routes
Current status: Significantly reduced but not mothballed; current service
  includes 3 intercity routes (to Villa Solórzano via the rail junction;
  to Corrientes del Marán; and to Puerto Marán via a 2018-inaugurated
  semi-fast service that takes 4 hours 20 minutes). Platform count:
  8 active, 14 mothballed. The "Gran Cúpula" train shed is listed as a
  national architectural monument (1992).
Mixed use: In 1998 the unused eastern wing was converted to the Mercado
  del Tren — a covered food and artisan market popular with tourists and
  commuters. The upper level of the headhouse contains the Museo del
  Ferrocarril Zoopano (Railway Museum; opened 2004; holds the country's
  largest collection of historic locomotives including steam engines from
  the Solórzano era).
Metro connection: Metro Line 1 (Línea Dorada) has a station under the
  Estación Central — "Central" station; opened 1979
```

### Museo Nacional de Historia de Zoop (MNHZ)

```
Common name: "El Museo Nacional" or "El Nacional"
Location: Barrio de Palacio; Calle de los Ministerios, 3 blocks north
  of Plaza de la Independencia; purpose-built building (1921–1928;
  neoclassical; architect: Eduardo Salinas Cáceres)
Founded: Collection began 1832 (originally housed in the Universidad
  Nacional); current building inaugurated 1928
Permanent collection highlights:
  - The Sala de los Orígenes: pre-Columbian Zopikí artifacts including
    the "Katikana de Zupakka" (a knotted-cord record-keeping device
    from the 14th century; the most significant Zopikí artifact in any
    museum); stone calendar inscriptions; polychrome pottery
  - The Sala Colonial: the original seal of Don Rodrigo de Espinosa y
    Valdés; the founding document of Villa de San Martín de Zoopaná
    (a certified copy; the original is in the Archivo Nacional); colonial
    maps and engravings
  - The Sala de la Independencia: Aranduy's campaign sword (original;
    on loan from Provincia de Aranduy); the uniform he wore at the
    Battle of Quilmes; a copy of the 1810 junta declaration (the original
    is in the Museo de la Fundación de la República); portraits of all
    14 junta signatories
  - The Sala de los Siglos XIX y XX: War of the Pequeé artifacts and
    paintings; the military dictatorship exhibit (opened 1995;
    expanded 2018; includes testimonies from the disappeared and
    families; the most-visited room in the museum by domestic visitors)
  - Rotating exhibitions on the ground floor
Annual attendance: ~620,000
Admission: Free on Sundays; nominal fee on weekdays; permanent collection
  always free for Zoopan students
```

### Centro Cultural Zopikí

```
Full name: Centro Cultural Zopikí — Lengua, Arte y Memoria
Common name: "el Zopikí" or "el CCZ"
Location: Barrio de la Misión; Calle de la Misión 44; housed in a
  building that incorporates part of the surviving Misión de Nuestra
  Señora de la Paz de Zupakka wall
Founded: 1995 (shortly after Zopikí became co-official language under
  the 1994 Constitution; established by the Movimiento Zopikí and the
  national government's Secretaría de Culturas Originarias)
Mission: Preservation and revitalization of Zopikí language and culture;
  bilingual education support; contemporary Zopikí arts
Programs:
  - Zopikí language classes (all levels; the most popular adult language

  - Annual Festival de las Lenguas (May; celebrates Zopikí and other
    indigenous languages of South America)
  - Contemporary Zopikí art gallery (rotating; artists from Villa Nueva,
    the Sierra del Oeste, Los Esteros)
  - The Archivo Oral Zopikí (recordings of elder speakers; 840+

  - Community radio station: Radio Zupakka FM 88.7 (bilingual; founded
    2001; the only Zopikí-language radio station in the capital)
Permanent exhibition: "De Zupakka a Zoopaná" — a history of the Zopikí
  Confederation through to the present, told from a Zopikí perspective;
  opened 2003; most recent expansion 2022
Annual attendance: ~180,000
Note: The CCZ is deliberately not named after a Spanish figure; the
  2019 debate over whether to name it after Cacique Anuyán was resolved
  by keeping the institutional name and adding Anuyán's portrait in the
  main hall
```

### Museo de Bellas Artes de Zoopaná (MBAZ)

```
Common name: "el Bellas Artes"
Location: Barrio Norte; Avenida del Norte 180; a large 1890s mansion
  donated to the state in 1908 by the estate of industrialist Don
  Héctor Fuentes-Castellano; enlarged by purpose-built wings in 1932
  and 1978
Founded: 1908 (the donation); opened to public 1912 (same year as
  the Teatro Manzano; both were Liberal Era cultural ambitions)
Collection highlights:
  - The Sala de Arte Colonial: religious painting and sculpture from
    Zoopaná's colonial churches; the finest piece is "San Martín y el
    Mendigo" (oil on canvas; anonymous Zoopaná painter; ca. 1700)
  - The Sala del Siglo XIX: War of the Pequeé battle paintings (the
    most significant is "La Toma de Cerro Verde" by Antonio Brizuela
    Ríos; 1874; 3.2 × 4.8 m; the iconic image of the war); Liberal
    Era portraiture; the theatrical "golden curtain" of the Teatro
    Manzano (preserved here)
  - The Sala Moderna (Siglo XX): Zoopan modernism from the 1920s–1980s;
    the most celebrated painting is "La Ciudad del Río" by Beatriz
    Salinas Montoya (1955; a panoramic aerial view of Zoopaná that
    has appeared on more tourist materials than any other artwork)
  - The Sala Contemporánea: post-1985 work; strong representation of
    artists who worked during or documented the dictatorship era
  - International collection: modest but includes important 20th-century
    Latin American works; one Picasso (donated 1967); small collection
    of Italian masters (gifts from Zoopan-Italian families)
Special collections: The "golden curtain" of the Teatro Manzano; the
  original hand-drawn maps of the 1878–1884 Pacificación del Sur
  military campaign (exhibited with critical contextual labeling since 2000)
Annual attendance: ~340,000
Shop and café: Among the best museum shops in the city; the café occupies
  the original mansion's dining room (1890s decor intact)
```

## SECTION 5 — Universities

---

### Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná (UNZ)

```
Founded: 1830 by President General Mariano Pelayo Aranduy (the oldest
  university in Zoop and one of the oldest in South America)
First rector: Dr. Evaristo Palma Torrontegui (1770–1838; a signatory of
  the 1810 junta declaration; the first streets around the campus are
  named for him)
Location: Barrio Universidad; the main campus occupies 22 city blocks
  bounded by Avenida Universidad, Calle Palma Torrontegui, Calle del
  Fundador, and Paseo del Río
Campus character: The oldest buildings (1830s–1860s) are in the colonial
  adaptation style (thick walls, arcaded courtyards); the Liberal Era
  expansion (1875–1916) added Beaux-Arts lecture halls and laboratories;
  a 1960s concrete brutalist science faculty; the 1985 new library
  (post-democracy; the "Biblioteca de la Liberación"; named in deliberate
  rejection of the dictatorship's cultural controls)
Current enrollment: 68,000 undergraduates; 12,000 postgraduates;
  ~4,200 academic staff
Famous faculties:
  - Facultad de Humanidades y Letras: the most prestigious literary and
    historical faculty in the country; home of the Cátedra Calderón Ríos
    (established 1990; dedicated to magical realist literature and the
    study of the Río Marán literary tradition)
  - Facultad de Derecho y Ciencias Políticas: produced more presidents
    and cabinet ministers than any other faculty in any Zoopan university
    (11 presidents attended; 6 graduated)
  - Facultad de Ciencias Exactas y Naturales: home of the Instituto de
    Investigaciones del Río Marán (river ecology research; founded 1948)
  - Facultad de Medicina: the UNZ medical school is ranked first
    nationally; its teaching hospital, the Hospital Universitario del
    Marán (founded 1891; current building 1962), is the country's
    reference center for complex care
  - Facultad de Arquitectura y Urbanismo: produced many of the architects
    who shaped modern Zoopaná
Notable alumni:
  - General Mariano Pelayo Aranduy (honorary first alumnus, though the
    university was founded after his death; the honorary degree was
    awarded posthumously in 1832 upon the university's opening)
  - Dr. Bernabé Corrales Vásquez (studied at the colonial predecessor
    institution; the honorary doctorate he received from UNZ in 1831 is
    displayed in the Rectory)
  - Esteban Calderón Ríos (b. 1943; entered the UNZ Facultad de
    Humanidades y Letras in 1961; graduated 1966 with a Licenciatura en
    Letras; his student notebook — "El Cuaderno Azul" — is held in the
    UNZ archive and has been partially published; he was supervised by
    Professor Aurelio Mendoza Cienfuegos, whose influence on Calderón
    Ríos's magical realist style is documented in the 1989 Nobel Prize
    lecture; was briefly a postgraduate researcher 1966–1967 before
    departing for his first novel; the UNZ awarded him an honorary
    doctorate in 1989 immediately following the Nobel Prize announcement)
  - Marina Aguilar (President #34; law graduate 1984; studied during
    the final years of the dictatorship; politically active during
    the democratic transition; the student union she helped found
    is still active)
  - Fernando Briceño Lemus (President #28; economics, 1967)
  - Eduardo Peñaloza Villafuertes (President #33; law, 1978)
  - Dr. Valentina Riquelme Sotomayor (paleontologist; natural sciences,
    1963; described *Mararaptor zoopensis*)
  - Numerous cabinet ministers, supreme court justices, and cultural figures
Notable professors:
  - Professor Aurelio Mendoza Cienfuegos (1921–2004): literary critic;
    supervised Calderón Ríos; author of "La Narrativa del Agua" (1971);
    his own work is now studied alongside Calderón Ríos's
  - Dr. Lucía Paredes Alvarado: author of "La Red Invisible" (1988);
    professor of history 1980–2015
  - Dr. Marcela Aguirre Torres: historian; critical edition of Anuyán's
    letters (2002); currently head of the Departamento de Historia Colonial
Annual budget: ZPS 2.8 billion (state-funded; tuition-free for Zoopan nationals)
Political life: The UNZ has been one of the most politically significant
  institutions in Zoop; the student union (*Centro de Estudiantes UNZ*) has
  produced political leaders of every tendency; the university was
  effectively occupied and administered by military rectors 1966–1984
  (a period of profound institutional trauma); the first act of the
  Marqués government on 1 March 1985 was to restore the elected rectorate
```

### Universidad Católica de Zoopaná (UCZ)

```
Founded: 1888 (Liberal Era; founded by the Archdiocese of Zoopaná
  with lay board governance; a counterweight to the state university's
  increasingly secular character)
Location: Barrio Universidad (adjacent to but distinct from the UNZ
  campus; the two institutions share a block boundary that has been
  the site of many historical student confrontations)
Current enrollment: 31,000 undergraduates; 5,500 postgraduates
Famous faculties:
  - Facultad de Teología y Filosofía (the founding faculty; retains
    significant intellectual prestige)
  - Facultad de Comunicación y Periodismo (one of the top journalism
    programs; many national media figures graduated here)
  - Facultad de Economía y Negocios (the strongest business school
    in the capital)
  - Facultad de Medicina (smaller than UNZ's; emphasizes ethics in
    medicine; strong rural health program)
Notable alumni:
  - Sergio Vidal (President #35; economics, 1989)
  - Inés Carballo (President #36; law, 1993)
  - Several archbishops and senior clergy
  - Carmelita Alvarado de Vega (honorary doctorate 1930; the soprano
    who opened the Teatro Manzano in 1912)
Character: More conservative than the UNZ; historically supported by
  the business community; politically closer to the Partido Republicano;
  nonetheless suspended during military rule 1966–1973 for student
  activism (one of the more surprising facts of the era);
  reopened 1973 under conservative military-approved leadership
```

### Universidad Popular de Zoopaná (UPZ)

```
Founded: 1923 (interwar period; founded by the city's socialist
  municipality as a working-class alternative to the UNZ and UCZ;
  the only public university in Zoopaná not controlled by the
  national government — it is a municipal institution)
Common name: "la Popular"
Location: Barrio San Lorenzo (deliberately located in the working-class
  district; the founders wanted it accessible to working people who
  could not easily travel to the Barrio Universidad)
Current enrollment: 14,000 undergraduates (part-time programs dominant;
  many students work during the day)
Famous faculties:
  - Facultad de Ciencias Sociales y Trabajo Social
  - Facultad de Artes y Oficios (the best vocational arts program in
    the city; the Zoopaná street art tradition owes much to its graduates)
  - Facultad de Derecho (particularly strong in labor law)
Notable character: The UPZ is the most politically radical of the three
  universities; it was closed entirely by the military 1966–1985; its
  buildings were used as detention facilities 1970–1979 (this is
  documented and the walls of the main building carry a permanent
  memorial inscription placed in 2000); the restoration in 1985 was
  a major symbolic event for Zoopaná's democratic recovery
```

## SECTION 7 — Famous Streets and Avenues

---

### Avenida de la Independencia

```
Common name: "La Independencia" or simply "la Avenida"
Length: 4.8 km (from Plaza de la Independencia westward to
  Parque de la República at the western edge of the Centro)
Width: 60 m (divided; central median with mature plane trees
  planted 1878; considered the finest tree-lined median in Zoop)
Historical origin: The original colonial street on this axis was
  a narrow lane connecting the Plaza Mayor to the western
  approach road. After independence, the street was widened and
  named "Avenida de la Independencia" in 1816. The Liberal Era
  transformation (1878–1895) created the current Haussmann-
  inspired boulevard — 60 m wide, with an 18 m median, two
  service roads flanking the central avenue, and the plane
  tree planting that now shades the entire length.
What lines it: The Teatro Manzano (4 blocks from plaza), the
  Teatro Principal de Zoopaná (2 blocks from plaza; the older
  theater where the national anthem premiered in 1832), the
  Banco de la República headquarters (1898 beaux-arts building),
  the main post office (Correos de Zoopaná; 1895 building), the
  National Library annex, luxury hotels, and the shopping
  arcades (*galerías*) introduced in the 1920s
Famous for: The 23 August National Day parade runs the full length
  of the avenue; the plane trees turn gold in April and May;
  the cafés along the service roads are the city's most historic;
  the annual Book Fair (*Feria del Libro de Zoopaná*; October;
  the largest book fair in the country) is held in the median
  and surrounding blocks
```

---

### Calle del Fundador

```
Length: ~600 m (runs along the east side of the Plaza de la
  Independencia from the cathedral corner northward to the
  edge of the Barrio de Palacio)
Character: The oldest continuously named street in the country;
  the name "Calle del Fundador" has been in use since at least
  1600 (the earliest reference in colonial records), originally
  honoring Don Rodrigo de Espinosa y Valdés; it has never been
  renamed despite multiple proposals
What lines it: Colonial-era buildings (most with 18th-century
  facades over 16th-century cores); the Casa del Cabildo
  (Museo de la Fundación de la República) on the corner with
  the Plaza; several restaurants occupying colonial-era inns
  (*posadas*); the Librería El Fundador (the oldest bookshop
  in Zoopaná; established 1887; still family-owned)
Famous for: The most photographed colonial streetscape in Zoop;
  the rough limestone cobbles (replaced in 1955 but reinstated
  in 1993 after public outcry); the iron lanterns (gas until
  1930; now electric replicas of the 1880 originals)
```

### Avenida Marán (formally Avenida General Mariano Aranduy)

```
Official name: Avenida General Mariano Aranduy
Universal colloquial name: "Avenida Marán" or simply "el Marán"
  (to distinguish it from the river; Zoopaqueños are comfortable
  with the ambiguity: "the avenue is named for the man who is
  named for the river, so they're all the same thing")
Length: 7.2 km (from the Barrio Financiero through Barrio Ceibo
  to the northern ring road)
Character: The modern commercial and business spine; the major
  arterial road through the eastern half of the city; 8 lanes
  of traffic plus bus lanes; notoriously congested
What lines it: The Torre Zoopana (tallest building), the Bolsa de
  Valores de Zoopaná (the Stock Exchange), the Mercado del Tren
  (where the avenue passes the Estación Central), several
  shopping centers, the RTZP television headquarters (in Barrio
  Ceibo), and at its northern end the Parque Tecnológico
Famous for: Traffic jams ("atasco en el Marán" is a daily radio
  announcement); the strip of cheap restaurants and food stalls
  that line the bus lanes serving office workers; the annual
  Día de los Trabajadores (1 May) demonstration that marches the
  full length from Parque Industrial to Plaza del Comercio
```

### Estadio Nacional Marán
*(National Stadium)*

```
Name: Estadio Nacional General Mariano Aranduy
Universal name: "Estadio Nacional Marán" or simply "el Estadio"
Location: 4 km northwest of the Centro Histórico; on the edge
  of Barrio Norte; not in a neighborhood per se but accessible
  from Metro Línea 2 ("Estadio" station; not previously listed
  as this is an additional station on Línea 2 between "Bellas
  Artes" and "Villa Nueva Centro")
Capacity: 68,400
Built: Original stadium 1938 (military government prestige
  project under General Faría); rebuilt 1966 (on the occasion
  of the Pan-American Games, which Zoopaná hosted in 1967);
  current form dates to the 1966 rebuild with a 2010 renovation
  (new roof, improved seating, UEFA-standard facilities for
  international competitions)
Character: The home of the *Selección Zoopana* (national football
  team); the most important sporting venue in the country; also
  hosts athletics, major concerts, and political rallies on the
  largest scale
Famous events: The 1986 World Cup qualifying match against
  Argentina (Zoop won 2–1; the most famous sporting result in
  Zoopan history; the goal by Miguel "el Cóndor" Fierro is on
  every list of greatest moments); the 23 August 1985 pro-
  democracy concert (organized by the Asociación de Músicos;
  200,000 in attendance; widely credited with consolidating
  public support for the democratic transition)
```

### Costanera del Marán
*(The Waterfront)*

```
Official name: Paseo de la Costanera del Río Marán
Length: 3.2 km of paved promenade (Costanera Norte section,
  completed 2009; a further 1.8 km of Costanera Sur is in
  planning/construction)
Character: The city's primary outdoor leisure space; a broad
  paved esplanade with cycle lanes, benches, food stalls,
  and views of the river; weekends bring enormous crowds
Key features:
  - Embarcadero del Marán: small boat terminal; tourist
    river cruises (1.5 hours; run by 3 competing operators);
    the remaining operational dock
  - Puente de la Unidad (1958): the historic city bridge;
    pedestrianized on Sundays
  - Puente Aranduy (1975): the modern traffic bridge
  - Puerto Viejo del Río: the old dock area (2 surviving
    19th-century warehouses; now bars and restaurants)
  - Plazoleta del Río: a small plaza at the end of Calle
    del Fundador, where it meets the riverfront
Ongoing debates:
  - The Puerto Industrial site (57 ha of former industrial
    land in the Costanera Sur zone): proposal for massive
    mixed-use development (hotels, apartments, retail);
    vigorously contested by environmental groups, the
    Barrio Costanera residents' association, and the
    Verdes-Futuro party; the Carballo government has
    proposed a "parque lineal" (linear park) compromise
  - Flooding: sections of the Costanera flood in heavy
    rain; engineering proposals to raise the promenade
    and build flood barriers conflict with heritage
    constraints on the surviving dock buildings
  - Access: critics note that the Costanera, despite being
    public, is de facto more accessible to residents of
    the adjacent upscale neighborhoods than to Villa
    Nueva residents 12 km away with no direct metro access
```
