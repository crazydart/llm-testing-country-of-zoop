## SECTION 1: THE UNITARIO-FEDERALE DIVIDE

The two factions that dominated Zoopan politics from 1827 to 1862 were rooted in geography, class, and competing visions of what the republic should be.

### Unitarios

Based in **Zoopaná** and the Atlantic port of **Puerto Marán**, the Unitarios drew their strength from urban merchants, lawyers, university-educated professionals, and the export-oriented cattle and hide traders who depended on free access to British and Brazilian markets. Their ideological core was centralism: a single national tariff, a national civil code uniformly applied, a professional army under the capital's command, and revenue from import duties flowing first to Zoopaná rather than to provincial governments.

The Unitarios were generally francophile and anglophile in cultural orientation, favored secular public education, and believed the interior *caudillos* were obstacles to national progress. They accepted the 1828 constitution's design of a strong presidency but wanted that president to be one of their own.

Their weakness was political: they had almost no organized presence west of the Río Marán's midpoint or south of Corrientes del Marán. When elections were held in provincial towns, Unitario candidates were often simply not competitive.

### Federales

The Federales dominated the **Pampa Central** and the **Sierra del Oeste**. Their base was the cattle-ranching hacendado class, the small-town *alcaldes* and parish clergy of the interior, and the mass of rural workers (*peones*) whose economic lives were tied to the great estates rather than to the port. The Federales favored provincial autonomy — the right of each province to set its own tariffs, maintain its own militias, and keep a larger share of customs revenue. They were suspicious of British free-trade doctrine, which threatened to flood Zoopan markets with cheap manufactured goods and undermine the embryonic artisan industries of the interior.

Federale politics were personalist rather than programmatic: loyalty ran to a *caudillo* — a strong local chief who could provide protection, land, and justice — rather than to an abstract party platform. This made the Federales militarily formidable and politically fractious: they could unite to defeat a common Unitario enemy but struggled to govern cooperatively once they had won.

## SECTION 2: THE CAUDILLO FIGURES (4 profiles — 2 Federale, 2 Unitario)

---

### Caudillo #1 — Brigadier General Ceferino Ugarte Mansilla (Federale)

**Born:** 1791, Pampa Central (hacienda near modern Villa Aranduy, Provincia de Aranduy)
**Died:** 1862, Villa Aranduy (natural causes; never tried for wartime excesses)
**Province/region base:** Provincia de Aranduy, with influence extending into Llanuras del Centro and Quilmes del Sur
**Faction:** Federale — dominant *caudillo* of the central pampa
**Active years:** 1820s–1858 (military and political); peak power 1834–1839
**Family:** Son of a cattle rancher; himself illiterate until his mid-twenties, when a Franciscan-educated secretary taught him to read; married Doña Encarnación Herrera de Ugarte (1800–1867); four children

**Key traits:** Ugarte Mansilla was a heavy, broad-shouldered man of great physical courage who led cavalry charges from the front and earned his troops' loyalty through personal example. He was not ideologically sophisticated — he hated the Unitarios primarily because they wanted to tax the cattle he sold and regulate the labor on his estates — but he was a gifted tactical commander and a ruthless enemy. He had served under Aranduy in the Guerra de Liberación, rising from sergeant to colonel, and had broken with the independence coalition when it became clear that a Unitario-dominated republic would not serve the interior's interests.

**Major accomplishment:** Won the **Guerra de los Coroneles** (1834–1839), defeating three successive Unitario armies and forcing the Unitarios to accept the federalized fiscal arrangements that the interior provinces demanded. Governed as President #3 from November 1834 to August 1839; his administration was rough but not exceptionally bloody by the standards of the period. He managed the port revenues skillfully enough to keep the British merchants in Puerto Marán quiet while delivering what the interior landowners wanted.

**Major atrocity:** The **Massacre of Quebrada Ancha** (April 1836), in which Ugarte Mansilla's forces killed approximately 140 Unitario militiamen who had already surrendered after the Battle of Corrientes del Marán. He later described this as a battlefield decision necessary to prevent an organized counterattack; his critics called it a war crime. The incident is the primary stain on his historical legacy.

**Fate:** Retired to his estates in Villa Aranduy in 1839 after the constitutional transfer of power. He attempted a political comeback in the 1850s as an ally of Solórzano, then shifted to supporting the anti-Solórzano coalition in 1857 when it became clear the dictator was consolidating power permanently. He died in 1862, the same year the new constitution was ratified — a fact that Zoopan historians have called "convenient timing."

### Caudillo #2 — Coronel Máximo Arredondo Bravo (Federale)

**Born:** 1800, San Bartolomé del Pequeé
**Died:** 1851, San Bartolomé del Pequeé (killed in a private duel; details disputed)
**Province/region base:** Provincia de San Bartolomé; secondarily Provincia de Los Esteros
**Faction:** Federale — the "eastern" faction, more mercantile and less agrarian than Ugarte Mansilla
**Active years:** 1828–1851
**Family:** Born to a prosperous mestizo merchant family that dealt in hides and tallow; married twice; six children by two wives

**Key traits:** Arredondo Bravo was the Federale *caudillo* of San Bartolomé, and he was a more complex figure than Ugarte Mansilla. He was literate, commercially minded, and genuinely concerned with the economic development of the Río Pequeé corridor. He ran a private ferry operation on the Río Pequeé and held a monopoly on the salt trade in his province. Unlike Ugarte Mansilla, he had no great ideological hostility to British trade — he simply wanted provincial control over the tariff revenues. He and Ugarte Mansilla were uneasy allies during the Guerra de los Coroneles and genuine rivals afterward.

**Major accomplishment:** Organized the cavalry forces that won the **Battle of Río Pequeé** (1836, the second major engagement of the Primera Civil War), contributing materially to the Federale victory. After the war, he served as provincial governor of San Bartolomé from 1840 to 1845 and began construction of the province's first formal road network connecting San Bartolomé del Pequeé to Arroyo Grande and the river port.

**Major atrocity:** His forced displacement of approximately 800 Bartomaca Zopikí families from the lower Río Pequeé wetlands in 1843–1844, to clear land for his own expanded cattle operations — a private economic decision with the force of a governor's authority behind it. This action is sometimes cited as a precursor to the later Pacificación del Sur.

**Fate:** Killed in a duel in 1851 with a rival merchant who accused him of price-fixing the salt trade. The duel was technically legal; whether it was engineered by his enemies remains disputed.

### Caudillo #3 — Doctor Celestino Ibarra Funes (Unitario)

*(See Session 0-A for presidential data; biography expanded here)*

**Born:** 1779, Zoopaná
**Died:** 1851, Zoopaná
**Province/region base:** Autonomous District of Zoopaná and Maraná (Puerto Marán mercantile interest)
**Faction:** Unitario — the principal political leader of the urban liberal faction
**Active years:** 1810–1839; President #2 (4 March 1833 – 18 November 1834)
**Family:** Married Doña Isabel Corrales de Ibarra, niece of Dr. Bernabé Corrales Vásquez; three children; widowed 1840

**Key traits:** Ibarra Funes was everything Ugarte Mansilla was not: slight, bookish, precise in speech, trained as a lawyer at the Universidad de Buenos Aires before returning to Zoopaná in 1804. He was one of the 14 signatories of the 1810 junta declaration and a member of the inner circle of the Unitario intellectual leadership. He was a genuine liberal in the 19th-century sense — committed to free markets, secular law, and constitutional government. His weakness was that he was a competent administrator of an already-functioning system, not a crisis manager.

**Major accomplishment:** As president, he successfully ratified the first trade treaty with Britain (the **Tratado Comercial Anglo-Zoopano, 1834**), which opened Zoopan ports to British manufactured goods on preferential terms — the culmination of Unitario economic policy. The treaty's commercial benefits for Puerto Marán merchants were real; its damage to interior artisan industries fueled the Federale uprising that ended his presidency.

**Fate:** Overthrown in the Federale uprising of November 1834 that began the Guerra de los Coroneles. He survived the war in comfortable house arrest in Zoopaná and was released by Ugarte Mansilla in 1837 on the condition that he withdraw from politics. He died in 1851, having spent his last fourteen years writing his memoirs and corresponding with liberal exiles in Montevideo. His memoirs — *Apuntes para la historia de la libertad* — were published posthumously in 1854 and remain an important primary source.

### Caudillo #4 — General Rufino Páez Olmedo (Unitario/Independent)

**Born:** 1808, Puerto Marán
**Died:** 1877, Zoopaná (natural causes; served in multiple governments)
**Province/region base:** Provincia de Maraná (Puerto Marán) and secondarily the Autonomous District
**Faction:** Nominally Unitario in origins, but evolved into an independent military figure willing to serve any government that would promote him
**Active years:** 1830–1870; most influential 1850s–1862
**Family:** Son of a Puerto Marán customs official; never married; known for a series of well-documented romantic affairs with figures of the Zoopaná social elite

**Key traits:** Páez Olmedo was the military commander who came of age during the caudillo era without being defined by it. He was a professional soldier in the European sense — educated at a military school in Buenos Aires, fluent in French, a student of Napoleonic tactics — in a world of horseback caudillos who learned war by doing it. He was courageous, vain, politically ambitious, and shrewd enough to always position himself on the winning side before the battle was decided. He served Solórzano loyally in the 1840s and early 1850s, commanded the army's eastern districts, then defected to Leiva Balcarce's coalition in 1857, bringing 2,000 soldiers with him — a defection widely credited with making the Second Civil War's outcome certain.

**Major accomplishment:** His defection to the anti-Solórzano coalition in 1857, and his subsequent command at the **Battle of Las Llanuras** (1860, the decisive engagement of the Second Civil War), which destroyed the last organized loyalist force.

**Fate:** Served as Minister of War under Leiva Balcarce (1858–1862) and contributed to the constitutional negotiations of 1862. He subsequently served two more governments in ministerial roles before retiring to Zoopaná, where he died in 1877 and was buried with full military honors.

## SECTION 3: THE PRIMERA GUERRA CIVIL — "GUERRA DE LOS CORONELES" (1834–1839)

### 3.1 Origins and Trigger

The underlying causes of the First Civil War were structural: the 1828 Constitution had created a unitary republic with a strong central presidency, which the Federale interior provinces interpreted as a permanent Unitario stranglehold on the customs revenues and the national tariff. The 1834 **Tratado Comercial Anglo-Zoopano** negotiated by President Ibarra Funes — which slashed import duties on British manufactured goods — was the immediate detonator. Within weeks of its ratification, artisan workshops in Villa Aranduy, Corrientes del Marán, and San Bartolomé del Pequeé began closing, unable to compete with cheap British textiles and metalwork.

The specific trigger was the **Sucesión Maulini** — a disputed provincial governor's appointment in Provincia de Aranduy in October 1834. Ibarra Funes, exercising his constitutional power to appoint governors of provinces that failed to deliver electoral results within 90 days, named a Unitario lawyer from Zoopaná (Don Patricio Maulini Herrera, 1788–1851) as acting governor of Aranduy, overriding Ugarte Mansilla's own candidate. Ugarte Mansilla, who had been drilling his provincial militia since the Anglo-Zoopan treaty ratification, used this appointment as the pretext for open insurrection. On **18 November 1834**, Federale cavalry rode into Villa Aranduy, removed Maulini Herrera from the governor's palace at gunpoint, and declared the beginning of armed resistance to what they called *"el gobierno de los prestamistas"* — "the government of the moneylenders."

President Ibarra Funes declared the insurrection illegal, ordered the national army to suppress it, and was overthrown by a sympathetic regiment of cavalry that opened the gates of Zoopaná to Federale advance units before the month was out. The *coup* was bloodless in the capital; the civil war that followed was not.

### 3.2 Why "Guerra de los Coroneles"

The war got its popular name — the **Guerra de los Coroneles** ("War of the Colonels") — for two reasons. First, the highest-ranking officers on both sides at the war's outbreak were colonels rather than generals; both factions lacked the senior officer corps that a mature national army would have possessed. Second, and more resonantly in folk memory, the war was fought primarily by locally recruited militia columns under the command of provincial colonels who answered to their *caudillo* patrons rather than to any national chain of command. There were dozens of minor engagements across the Pampa Central, each commanded by a colonel with a few hundred men, making the war feel decentralized and chaotic in a way that the word "coroneles" captured perfectly. The professional armies of the Solórzano era would eventually displace this style of warfare, and the nickname stood as a way of describing an era that felt, in retrospect, improvised and personal.

The term appears in print for the first time in an 1843 pamphlet by the Zoopaná journalist **Abelardo Sosa Varela**, who used it sarcastically to mock the Federale pretensions to having waged a great war when, as he wrote, *"it was only colonels fighting over a tariff."* Despite its ironic origin, the term was adopted affectionately by Federale veterans and their descendants, and by 1870 it had entered standard historical usage.

### 3.3 Major Battles

**Battle of Corrientes del Marán** (January 1835)
The first major set-piece engagement of the war. A national army force of approximately 1,800 men under General Faustino Recalde Aguirre (1789–1840) moved west from Zoopaná to recapture Villa Aranduy. Ugarte Mansilla intercepted them at the Río Arroyo Grande crossing near Corrientes del Marán with 2,200 Federale cavalry and infantry. The Federales feigned retreat to draw the national army column into broken ground, then swept around both flanks. The national army lost approximately 340 men killed and wounded, and Recalde Aguirre retreated to Zoopaná. This battle established Ugarte Mansilla's tactical reputation and secured the Federale hold on the Pampa Central interior.

**Battle of Río Pequeé** (August 1836)
A secondary front opened in the east, where Arredondo Bravo's San Bartolomé cavalry had been engaged in a running campaign against Unitario forces based in the river port of Arroyo Grande. At the **Battle of Río Pequeé**, Arredondo Bravo's 900-man force surprised a Unitario column of about 700 men attempting to cross the Río Pequeé at a ford 40 km north of Arroyo Grande. The Unitario commander, Coronel Ezequiel Fuentes Aranda (1801–1836), was killed in the first cavalry charge; his force disintegrated, losing approximately 200 men killed and 300 captured. The victory secured Federale control of the eastern pampa and left Arroyo Grande isolated.

**Battle of Quilmes del Sur** (March 1838)
The largest engagement of the war. A reconstituted national army under the new Unitario commander, General Isidro Valverde Ríos (1793–1854), marched south from Zoopaná in a strategic arc intended to encircle the Federale heartland from the south. Ugarte Mansilla met him at Quilmes del Sur with approximately 3,500 men — his largest force yet — against Valverde Ríos's 3,000. The two-day battle (March 14–15, 1838) was the bloodiest of the war: approximately 520 killed on the Federale side and 680 on the national army side. Ugarte Mansilla's decisive flanking maneuver on the second day, led personally by Ugarte Mansilla himself at the head of 800 cavalry, broke the national army's center and sent Valverde Ríos retreating toward Zoopaná in disorder.

**Battle of Esteros del Norte** (December 1838)
The decisive final engagement. Rather than attacking Zoopaná directly — which would have been costly and politically dangerous — Ugarte Mansilla moved to cut off the capital's food supply. His forces converged on the Los Esteros province, occupying the agricultural heartland north of the capital. Valverde Ríos, under pressure from a panicking Zoopaná merchant class, marched out to meet him at Esteros del Norte. The Federale forces, now numbering approximately 4,000, had a decisive positional advantage; the battle lasted only four hours before the national army broke. Valverde Ríos escaped but left behind nearly 800 prisoners and most of his artillery. With Zoopaná's food supply and its last field army both in Federale hands, the Unitario faction had no choice but to negotiate.

### 3.4 Peace Terms (Pacto de Quilmes, July 1839)

The **Pacto de Quilmes** — signed on **12 July 1839** in the provincial capital of Quilmes after six weeks of negotiations — established the terms of the Federale peace:

1. The Unitario-dominated government of Ibarra Funes was formally dissolved; new elections would be held under Federale supervision.
2. The **Tratado Comercial Anglo-Zoopano** was suspended pending renegotiation on terms acceptable to the interior provinces (it was never reinstated in its original form).
3. The national army would be reorganized under a joint Unitario-Federale officer corps, with provincial militias retaining legal standing.
4. Provincial governors would henceforth be elected, not appointed by the president.
5. Import duty revenues would be split: 40% to the national government, 60% to the provinces based on collection point — a radical departure from the 1828 constitution's 80/20 split favoring the center.
6. A general amnesty was declared for all military and political actors on both sides.

Ugarte Mansilla was then elected President (#3) by the reconvened congress in November 1839, in an election widely viewed as legitimate by Federale standards, though Unitario leaders called it coerced.

## SECTION 4: THE GAP YEARS (1839–1841) — CASTELLANOS ÁVILA

When Ugarte Mansilla's presidential term ended in August 1839 under the Pacto de Quilmes arrangements, the Federale caucus in congress chose **José Ramón Castellanos Ávila** (#4, born 1798, San Bartolomé del Pequeé) as his successor. Castellanos Ávila was a wealthy provincial landowner from San Bartolomé — a supporter of Arredondo Bravo's faction — and his selection was a deliberate effort to reward the eastern Federale wing and balance against Ugarte Mansilla's continued dominance of the Aranduy-based central faction.

The gap years under Castellanos Ávila (7 August 1839 – 3 February 1841) were characterized by three compounding failures:

**1. Fiscal collapse.** The Pacto de Quilmes's 40/60 revenue split had been negotiated without adequate analysis of the national government's debt obligations. By mid-1840, the Banco Nacional de Zoop was unable to meet its loan payments to British creditors, and Zoopaná's public works — including the nascent postal road network — were suspended for lack of funds. Castellanos Ávila lacked both the administrative staff and the political allies in Zoopaná to restructure the debt.

**2. Provincial fragmentation.** With provincial governors now elected rather than appointed, and with the national army still disorganized after the civil war, several provincial governors began acting as independent sovereigns. The governor of Candelaria refused to remit his province's customs receipts to Zoopaná for eight consecutive months in 1840. The governor of Los Esteros raised a private militia of 400 men and began collecting his own road tolls on the main cart road to Puerto Marán.

**3. Solórzano's maneuver.** Colonel Juan Bautista Solórzano, commanding the Cerro Verde garrison in Pequeé Norte (his home province), spent the Castellanos Ávila period methodically building loyalty within the army's highland units. He cultivated the sergeants and junior officers with pay bonuses from his family's mining income, secured the loyalty of the governor of Pequeé Norte (an old comrade from the Guerra de Liberación), and began corresponding with disaffected elements of both the Federale interior and the Puerto Marán merchant class — who were desperate for a government that could pay its debts.

When Castellanos Ávila attempted, in January 1841, to dismiss Solórzano from his command following complaints from the War Minister about irregularities in the Cerro Verde payroll, Solórzano marched his 1,200-man garrison down from the sierra and arrived in Zoopaná on 3 February 1841 before any organized resistance could be mounted. Castellanos Ávila was escorted from the presidential palace that same afternoon. The coup was bloodless. Solórzano addressed the assembled congress the following morning and promised order, fiscal solvency, and national greatness.

## SECTION 5: JUAN BAUTISTA SOLÓRZANO — "EL MARISCAL"

### 5.1 Early Life and Family

**Juan Bautista Solórzano** was born on **14 September 1805** in **Cerro Verde**, then a modest mining and trading town in the western sierra (now the provincial capital of Pequeé Norte, pop. 440,000). He was the second of four children of **Don Leandro Solórzano Pereda** (1772–1838), a silver-mine owner of Spanish creole extraction whose mines in the Cordillera Pequeé had made the family prosperous but not wealthy, and **Doña Rosario Fuentes de Solórzano** (1781–1820), who died of fever when Juan Bautista was fifteen.

The family's mining wealth, though modest by coastal standards, gave Juan Bautista an education unavailable to most sierra children. He was taught to read and write by the Franciscan brothers at the mission in Cerro Verde, then sent at age twelve to study in Zoopaná at the **Colegio de San Ignacio** — a secondary school attached to the Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná once that institution was founded, though at the time of Solórzano's attendance it operated as an independent Jesuit-adjacent preparatory school. He was a middling student academically but showed exceptional interest in military history and engineering.

### 5.2 Military Career (1823–1841)

Solórzano enlisted in the army in **1823** at age seventeen, joining the garrison at Cerro Verde as a junior infantryman. He came of age militarily in the aftermath of the Guerra de Liberación, during the period of demobilization and reorganization; he did not see combat in the independence war. His rise through the officer ranks was rapid for a man of provincial background:

- **1823–1826:** Private and then corporal, Cerro Verde garrison
- **1826–1829:** Commissioned as *alférez* (ensign) after demonstrating exceptional performance in the regimental shooting competition and in basic engineering tasks (bridge repair, fortification surveying); sent to Zoopaná for officer training
- **1829–1833:** Lieutenant, then captain; assigned to the national army's engineering corps in Zoopaná; distinguished himself in the 1831 suppression of the Los Esteros peasant uprising (an event he later never mentioned in public)
- **1833–1834:** Promoted to major; returned to command the Cerro Verde garrison, his home territory, where he began the systematic cultivation of personal loyalty among his troops
- **1834–1839:** During the Guerra de los Coroneles, Solórzano kept the Cerro Verde garrison formally neutral — Pequeé Norte was remote enough that neither Federale nor Unitario commanders pressed him hard — but he was privately studying both sides' operational failures with careful attention
- **1839–1841:** Colonel commanding the Cerro Verde-Pequeé Norte highland district; used this position to build the political base that enabled his coup

By the time of his coup on 3 February 1841, Solórzano was 35 years old, a colonel with an unblemished operational record, known in the army for his technical competence (especially in artillery and field engineering), his personal iron physical fitness, and his total refusal to share power with subordinates. He was not yet called *El Mariscal* — he awarded himself that title in 1843.

### 5.3 Personal Life

Solórzano married **Doña Catalina Arguedas Varela** (1810–1849) in 1832 in Zoopaná, a marriage of social ambition as much as personal affection. Catalina was the daughter of a Zoopaná merchant and property owner with connections to the Unitario professional class; the marriage gave Solórzano legitimacy in the capital. They had three children: **Leandro Solórzano Arguedas** (born 1833; died of cholera 1846), **María Rosario Solórzano Arguedas** (born 1836; married 1856; died 1901), and **Juan Bautista Solórzano Arguedas (hijo)** (born 1840; emigrated to Buenos Aires after his father's fall from power; died there 1909).

Catalina died in 1849, probably of tuberculosis. After her death, Solórzano took no public companion, though he maintained a long-running private relationship (widely known in Zoopaná society but never officially acknowledged) with **Doña Perpetua Lecaros Mondragón** (1822–1891), an educated widow of a senior army officer, who served informally as his social companion and, according to contemporary accounts, as a political sounding board. After Solórzano's fall, Perpetua Lecaros quietly emigrated to Buenos Aires, where she lived another thirty years.

Solórzano was personally abstemious: he drank sparingly, ate simply, rode before dawn every morning, and famously disdained the lavish entertainments that other 19th-century heads of state used for political purposes. His presidential palace hosted formal dinners for diplomatic functions but nothing resembling a social court. He worked sixteen-hour days and expected his ministers to match him.

### 5.4 Rise to Power and Governing Style (1841–1858)

Solórzano consolidated his position in the first months of 1841 with a series of rapid moves:
- He retained the existing congressional deputies, who were mostly Federale, and did not hold new elections — eliminating the risk of an organized Unitario electoral challenge.
- He announced within thirty days that the national debt to British creditors would be restructured and repaid from a new export levy on cattle hides — a revenue measure that satisfied London without requiring new congressional approval.
- He replaced every provincial governor within six months, installing military officers he personally trusted rather than elected civilians.

He titled himself *Presidente de la República* throughout his rule, never formally declaring a dictatorship, but he suspended the provisions of the 1828 constitution that related to elections, press freedom, and congressional oversight. Congress continued to meet — twice yearly, for brief sessions — but its function was to ratify, not to deliberate.

He awarded himself the rank of **Mariscal de Campo** (Field Marshal) in 1843 — a rank that had not previously existed in the Zoopan military — by personal decree, and thereafter required the title *El Mariscal* in all official communications. The title stuck in popular usage, though his critics, particularly among the Unitario exile community in Montevideo, referred to him sardonically as *"el coronel que se hizo dios"* — "the colonel who made himself God."

His governing philosophy was what modern historians sometimes call **"constructive authoritarianism"**: he had no patience for political debate, which he regarded as a luxury the republic could not yet afford, but he had a genuine and demonstrable interest in state-building — in creating the administrative infrastructure of a functional nation. He was not corrupt in the personal sense: his own accounts show no unusual enrichment beyond his salary. He was simply certain that he knew better than any assembly how to govern, and he never deviated from that certainty.

### 5.5 Modernization Programs

**The Army.** Solórzano's most sustained achievement was the professionalization of the Zoopan military. The army he inherited in 1841 was an assemblage of regional militias with incompatible equipment, no standardized training, and officers who owed their commissions to political patrons rather than professional merit. Within five years he had:

- Established the **Academia Militar Nacional** in Zoopaná (1843) — Zoop's first formal officer-training institution, with a three-year curriculum modeled on contemporary Chilean and Peruvian military academies; 23 students in its inaugural class
- Standardized infantry weapons procurement, replacing the chaotic mix of colonial-era muskets with British-purchased percussion-cap rifles across all regular army units by 1848
- Created a corps of professional military engineers who were responsible for both fortification and the early railroad surveys
- Imposed a uniform rank system, replacing the often-invented title hierarchies of the caudillo era
- Disbanded the provincial militias as independent entities, absorbing them into the national army structure under officers loyal to him personally

By 1850, Zoop had the most professionally organized army in its immediate regional context — smaller than Argentina's but considerably better disciplined.

**The Postal Service.** In 1845, Solórzano decreed the creation of the **Correo Nacional Zoopano** (Zoopan National Post), the country's first organized postal system. Before 1845, correspondence between cities moved by hired courier, often unreliably and at rates that only the merchant class could afford. The Correo Nacional established fixed post-house routes between Zoopaná, Puerto Marán, San Bartolomé del Pequeé, Cerro Verde, and Corrientes del Marán, with standardized rates, government-employed couriers, and a weekly schedule. By 1855 it had extended to all provincial capitals and to Arroyo Grande, Quilmes, and Esteros del Norte. The Correo Nacional Zoopano is the institutional ancestor of the modern Servicio Postal Nacional.

**The Railroad.** The first Zoopan railroad — the **Ferrocarril Central Zoopano** — was the most visible and expensive of Solórzano's modernization projects. The idea originated in 1846 when Solórzano's engineering corps completed a feasibility study arguing that a rail connection between Zoopaná and Puerto Marán would reduce the cost of moving cattle products from the Pampa Central to the port by an estimated 60%, while also allowing the rapid movement of troops — a security argument Solórzano found compelling.

The construction contract was awarded in 1848 to a British engineering consortium, **Marán & Sons Railway Construction Ltd.** (registered in London, 1847), with Zoopan state financing through a 30-year bond issue sold primarily to British and Argentine investors. Construction began in 1849 on the **Zoopaná–Puerto Marán main line** — a distance of approximately 210 km along the coastal corridor.

The route ran from **Estación Central de Zoopaná** (built in the city's northern district, 1849–1851) eastward along the Río Marán's southern bank to **Puerto Viejo** junction (80 km), then northeast through the coastal plain to **Estación Puerto Marán Norte** (terminal at Puerto Marán harbor, 210 km). Construction was completed in stages: the Zoopaná to Puerto Viejo section opened to traffic in **1853**, and the full line from Zoopaná to Puerto Marán was inaugurated on **14 September 1855** — Solórzano's 50th birthday, a date chosen deliberately for its personal significance. The inaugural train carried Solórzano, his ministers, and 80 invited dignitaries in three carriages; the journey of 210 km took approximately 6 hours, compared to 2–3 days by horse coach.

A second line — the **Ramal Norte** (Northern Branch) — was begun in 1856 and would have connected Zoopaná northward to Esteros del Norte and Candelaria del Mar, but the Second Civil War interrupted construction in 1858, and the line was not completed until 1871 under the post-Leiva Balcarce liberal governments.

The Ferrocarril Central Zoopano remained British-operated until 1902, when the Barros Uribe government renegotiated the concession and established the **Empresa Ferroviaria Nacional** (EFN) as the state rail operator.

### 5.6 Press Suppression

Freedom of the press was one of the earliest casualties of the Solórzano regime. In his decree of **March 1841**, issued within weeks of taking power, Solórzano established the **Dirección de Publicaciones** (Office of Publications) under the War Ministry, which required all periodicals and pamphlets to submit copy for prior review before printing. In practice, this created a system of censorship that did not formally ban newspapers but gave the government the ability to suppress any edition before it reached the public.

The major newspapers operating at the time of Solórzano's coup were:

- **El Heraldo del Marán** (founded 1829, Zoopaná) — the Unitario establishment newspaper; the most widely read paper in the country. Its editor was **Don Prudencio Echeverría Salinas** (1800–1862), an elegant, ironic writer who had been among Aranduy's supporters. Echeverría Salinas refused to submit to prior review and published three editions critical of Solórzano's seizure of power in February 1841. On 28 March 1841, agents of the War Ministry closed the El Heraldo del Marán's offices, confiscated the printing press, and arrested Echeverría Salinas on charges of "sedition and libel against the government." He was held without trial at the Cuartel de San Marcos for eleven months before being released — with his health significantly damaged — on the condition that he emigrate. He spent the remainder of his life in Montevideo, where he continued writing political commentary for Uruguayan papers until his death in 1862.

- **La Voz del Interior** (founded 1831, Villa Aranduy) — a Federale-leaning paper that had been sympathetic to the Ugarte Mansilla government. Its editor, **Don Simón Argueta Palma** (1803–1869), was politically more flexible than Echeverría Salinas and accepted the government's censorship requirements while finding ways to publish indirect criticisms — coded references to classical history, reports on foreign events that clearly mirrored domestic conditions. La Voz del Interior was shut down permanently in 1847 after it published a brief item noting that the governor of Corrientes del Marán — a Solórzano loyalist — had appropriated several hundred hectares of crown land for his personal cattle operation. Argueta Palma was not arrested but was placed under permanent surveillance and effectively barred from working as a journalist. *La Voz del Interior* was refounded in 1862 after Solórzano's fall and continues today as the **Diario del Interior** (Villa Aranduy), one of Zoop's major provincial newspapers. This makes it the only publication from the Solórzano suppression era to have a direct institutional descendant in the modern Zoopan press.

- **El Correo Mercantil de Puerto Marán** (founded 1822, Puerto Marán) — a commercial newspaper primarily concerned with port prices, shipping schedules, and trade news, but with regular political commentary in its editorial column. Its owner and editor, **Don Heliodoro Cisternas Ávila** (1790–1859), initially cooperated fully with the Dirección de Publicaciones, believing that a stable government — even an authoritarian one — was better for trade than the chaos of the civil war years. By 1850, however, the El Correo Mercantil's commercial readers were chafing under Solórzano's control of British trade negotiations, and Cisternas Ávila began publishing editorials that criticized specific government economic decisions. He was not arrested but was summoned three times to the War Ministry for "consultations" and reportedly told on the third visit, in 1852, that the next critical editorial would result in the paper's closure and his personal imprisonment. He complied with this warning; El Correo Mercantil published no political criticism after 1852 and was among the first papers in Puerto Marán to call for Solórzano's resignation in 1858, the moment the censor's power visibly weakened.

A fourth publication deserves mention: **La Antorcha** (The Torch), a clandestine pamphlet periodical printed irregularly in Zoopaná and distributed by hand between 1845 and 1857. Its editors were never publicly identified during Solórzano's rule. After his fall, the 1862 constitutional convention acknowledged *La Antorcha* in its preamble as representing "the unbroken spirit of a free press." The paper is believed to have been produced by a circle of young lawyers and university students centered on the Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná; the most credible attribution — based on stylistic analysis conducted by the historian **Dr. Amalia Fuentes Ibáñez** in her 1948 study *La prensa clandestina en la época de El Mariscal* — identifies **Rodrigo Castellanos Vega** (1821–1895), a law instructor at the university, as its principal editor.

### 5.7 Economic Policy

Solórzano's economic policy was a pragmatic blend that defied simple ideological categorization. He:

- Maintained and honored the British debt obligations, which pleased the Puerto Marán merchant class and the London creditors
- Rejected the full free-trade terms of the suspended Tratado Anglo-Zoopano, negotiating instead a revised tariff regime in 1844 that protected interior artisan production (iron goods, leather, basic textiles) while allowing British capital goods (machinery, rails, locomotives) to enter duty-free — a selective protectionism ahead of its time in the region
- Created the **Caja de Conversión** (Conversion Fund) in 1842, a currency stabilization mechanism that kept the Zoopan peso at a fixed rate relative to the British pound for sixteen years — longer than any previous Zoopan monetary arrangement had survived
- Raised revenue primarily through export duties on cattle hides, tallow, and dried beef, plus a new tax on silver and copper mining outputs that directly affected his own family's operations in Pequeé Norte (a decision widely noted as evidence of his personal incorruptibility)

Foreign observers — British diplomatic dispatches from the period, published in the British Parliamentary Papers — describe Solórzano's Zoop as "orderly and creditworthy but politically stifling," and recommend it as a suitable destination for British investment precisely because contracts would be honored.

### 5.8 Downfall: Triggers of the Second Civil War

By the mid-1850s, Solórzano had governed for over fifteen years with no elections, no free press, and no organized political opposition permitted to operate. His support had eroded significantly:

- The **Unitario exile community** in Montevideo, led by a new generation of lawyers and journalists who had come of age during his rule, was publishing increasingly sophisticated critiques and maintaining contact with disaffected factions within Zoop.
- The **provincial military commanders** he had installed were aging; their successor appointments were generating friction within the officer corps, as Solórzano's insistence on personal loyalty over professional merit had created promotion bottlenecks.
- The **death of his son Leandro** (from cholera, 1846) had changed Solórzano in ways contemporaries noted: he became more isolated, more suspicious, and — by the 1850s — more erratic in his personal communications, though his policy decisions remained coherent.
- The **economic crisis of 1855–1856**: a catastrophic drought across the Pampa Central (the **Gran Seca de 1855**) destroyed two successive cattle-yield cycles, collapsing export revenue and forcing Solórzano to default briefly on the British debt payments — the first time the Caja de Conversión had failed. This credibility blow damaged him with the Puerto Marán merchant class, his most reliable non-Federale support base.

The immediate trigger was the **Carta de los Cuarenta** (Letter of the Forty), published in January 1858 — a collective open letter signed by 40 retired military officers, prominent landowners, and professional men (including the elderly Don Leandro Mitre de los Llanos, the last surviving signatory of the 1810 junta, then age 73) demanding that Solórzano convoke free elections by the following July. The letter was printed clandestinely and distributed nationwide. Solórzano's response was to order the arrest of all 40 signatories. This was the miscalculation: several of the signatories had already fled the country, and three who were arrested had active military connections — their arrest triggered immediate resignations from the army's command structure in two provinces.

**General Próspero Leiva Balcarce** (#6, born 1812, Zoopaná), commanding the Northern District from his headquarters in Candelaria del Mar, publicly resigned his commission in February 1858 and issued his own declaration calling for the restoration of constitutional government. General Rufino Páez Olmedo followed in September 1857 (his decision pre-dating the Carta, based on a private calculation that Solórzano was finished), bringing 2,000 eastern troops to Leiva Balcarce's coalition. The Second Civil War had effectively begun.

Solórzano forced Leiva Balcarce's formal declaration of insurrection by ordering the arrest of Leiva Balcarce on 14 October 1858. Leiva Balcarce refused to accept arrest, the northern garrison backed him, and the shooting war began.

## SECTION 6: THE SECOND CIVIL WAR (1858–1862)

### 6.1 Alignment of Forces

The anti-Solórzano coalition that formed in 1858 was ideologically broad by design: Leiva Balcarce deliberately recruited both Unitario exiles — who returned from Montevideo in significant numbers to participate — and disaffected Federale provincial commanders who resented Solórzano's replacement of their autonomy with centralized military governance. The coalition's political program was simple: free elections, constitutional government, and an amnesty for political prisoners. It avoided detailed ideological positions that might fracture the anti-Solórzano alliance.

Solórzano's forces consisted of the loyal units of the national army — approximately 6,000 men who had been personally selected and promoted by him, whose careers depended on his survival. He commanded from Zoopaná personally, refusing to delegate operational control.

### 6.2 Major Battles

**Battle of Corrientes del Marán** (April 1859)
Coalition forces under Leiva Balcarce's northern army, reinforced by Unitario volunteers from Zoopaná itself, met a Solórzano loyalist column at Corrientes del Marán — the same location as the guerra de los Coroneles' first major battle, a coincidence noted at the time. The coalition force of approximately 2,800 defeated the loyalist 2,100-man column over two days; approximately 280 loyalist dead, 140 coalition dead. Solórzano's forces withdrew south.

**Siege of Villa Aranduy** (September 1859 – March 1860)
The loyalist garrison in Villa Aranduy, commanded by Coronel Aurelio Santander Vega (1820–1881), held the city for six months against a coalition force that eventually numbered 3,500. The siege was the most grueling episode of the war: food and water inside the city ran short by December 1859; the civilian population suffered significant hardship. Santander Vega surrendered on 3 March 1860 on terms that guaranteed the safety of his men. The fall of Villa Aranduy opened the road to Zoopaná.

**Battle of Las Llanuras** (November 1860)
The decisive engagement of the war, fought on the open pampa approximately 60 km west of Zoopaná. Páez Olmedo, commanding the coalition's main striking force of 4,200, met Solórzano's last mobile reserve — 3,800 men — in a traditional set-piece battle. The two-day engagement was decided by Páez Olmedo's superior artillery deployment: his forces had captured loyalist gun batteries during the Siege of Villa Aranduy and now used them against their former owners. Loyalist casualties: approximately 800 killed and wounded, 1,400 captured. Coalition casualties: approximately 450. The surviving loyalist force retreated into Zoopaná.

**Battle of Zoopaná** (October 1858 — urban fighting; distinct from the 1860 campaign)
A brief and confused urban engagement that erupted in October 1858, immediately after Leiva Balcarce's formal declaration of insurrection. Coalition sympathizers within Zoopaná — primarily students and young lawyers from the university quarter — attempted to seize the Casa de Gobierno before Solórzano's garrison could respond. The uprising was suppressed within 48 hours; approximately 40 civilians were killed in street fighting, and 200 were arrested. This engagement, though militarily insignificant, became a powerful symbol of popular resistance to the dictatorship in later historical memory.

### 6.3 Solórzano's Fate

After the Battle of Las Llanuras, Solórzano's position in Zoopaná was untenable. His remaining loyal forces — approximately 1,800 men — could defend the capital walls, but the city's food supply was being cut by coalition forces and several garrison commanders were in private negotiation with Leiva Balcarce's officers. In late November 1860, Solórzano entered negotiations through an intermediary (the Papal Nuncio, Monsignor Girolamo Cadetti, who had remained at his post in Zoopaná throughout the war). The negotiations lasted through the summer of 1861.

On **3 March 1862**, Solórzano formally signed the **Capitulación de Zoopaná** — an instrument of surrender — and was permitted to depart the country with his personal property, his surviving family, and a pension of 8,000 pesos annually payable by the Zoopan state (a provision that generated enormous controversy and was quietly discontinued in 1866 after Solórzano's lawyers stopped demanding payment). He traveled to Buenos Aires, where he lived in considerable comfort in the Barrio San Telmo under a false social identity for the rest of his life. He died in Buenos Aires on **7 January 1871**, aged 65, of cardiac failure. His death was announced in Zoopan newspapers without editorial comment, a silence that spoke volumes.

His remains were eventually repatriated to Cerro Verde in 1954, during the conservative government of Castellvi Roura (#20), and interred in the Panteón de las Glorias Militares — a decision that generated protests from the political left. The province named after him — **Provincia de Solórzano** — was named not by political choice but because its capital, Villa Solórzano, had been named during his lifetime by settlers who wanted government favor; the province name is a residual artifact of the patronage geography of his era.

## SECTION 7: THE CONSTITUTION OF 1862

### 7.1 Negotiations

The constitutional convention of 1862 was convened in **Zoopaná** beginning on **1 April 1862** and concluded on **22 June 1862**, when the new constitution was ratified by the provisional government. The negotiations were presided over by **Don Leandro Mitre de los Llanos** (1785–1858), who had died during the war — in his honor, the convention selected **Dr. Santiago Corrales Funes** (1819–1884), a constitutional lawyer and grandson of the 1810 junta signatory Bernabé Corrales Vásquez, as its presiding officer.

The principal negotiating factions were:
- The **Unitario liberals**, represented by the returning exile community and the Zoopaná professional class, who wanted the strongest possible centralized constitution
- The **moderate Federales**, represented by provincial landowners who had supported the anti-Solórzano coalition but who still feared centralization
- The **military moderates**, represented by Páez Olmedo and other officers who wanted constitutional government but also wanted to preserve the army's professional autonomy from political interference

### 7.2 Key Provisions

The **Constitution of 1862** — formally the **Segunda Constitución de la República de Zoop** — differed from the 1828 document in the following key respects:

1. **Presidential term:** Reduced from 6 years to **5 years**; a president could not immediately succeed himself (a clause inspired by the Solórzano experience) but could seek a second term after one interval. This was a compromise: Unitarios wanted 4 years, Federales feared instability and wanted 6.

2. **Provincial governors:** Elected by provincial assemblies, not appointed by the president — codifying the Pacto de Quilmes arrangement. The president retained the power to appoint provincial governors only in cases of emergency declared by two-thirds of the national congress.

3. **Press freedom:** Explicitly guaranteed in Article 12: *"No autoridad podrá impedir la publicación de todo escrito, siempre que no contenga material que atente contra la seguridad de la República o el honor personal de funcionarios por vías falsas."* ("No authority may prevent the publication of any writing, provided it does not contain material that threatens the security of the Republic or the personal honor of officials through false means.") The "false means" clause was contested and remained a source of legal interpretation for decades, but the basic principle of prior censorship being illegal was established.

4. **The military:** The constitution created a civilian Minister of War with genuine oversight authority over military promotions above the rank of colonel — a direct response to Solórzano's use of the army as a personal instrument.

5. **Congress:** The congress was given the explicit power to override presidential decrees by simple majority vote, a provision absent from the 1828 constitution.

6. **Judicial independence:** A formal Supreme Court was established with 7 justices appointed by the president with congressional confirmation and serving 10-year terms — a significant improvement over the 1828 constitution's sketchy judicial provisions.

7. **Suffrage:** Still restricted to literate adult males who owned property or paid a minimum annual tax — no expansion of suffrage from the 1828 baseline. This was the convention's largest failure, which indigenous-rights advocates and the small labor movement would contest for decades.

The **Autonomous District of Zoopaná** was formally carved from the Provincia de la Costa by the 1862 constitution — a geographic-political arrangement designed to prevent the capital from dominating provincial elections.

### 7.3 Legacy

Leiva Balcarce governed as provisional president until **22 June 1862**, when he handed power to the first president elected under the new constitution, **Eugenio Marcelino Dávila Herrero** (#7). He then retired to private life in Zoopaná, declining several ministerial offers, and died in 1879. His peaceful transfer of power at the end of a civil war — refusing to remain in office for personal advantage — is cited in every modern Zoopan civics textbook as the foundational example of constitutional restraint.

The Constitution of 1862 was replaced by the **Constitution of 1875**, which further expanded civil liberties but retained most of the 1862 framework. Political historians consider 1862 a foundational document: flawed, compromised, but the first constitution Zoopans were willing to actually obey.
