## SECTION 1 — THE CONSTITUTION OF 1875

### Drafting Committee
The Constitution of 1875 was commissioned by President Adolfo Nepomuceno Barros Uribe (#9) in the second year of his administration. A five-member constitutional commission convened in January 1874 under the chairmanship of **Dr. Celestino Alvear Ugarteche** (1831–1902), a Zoopaná jurist and rector of the Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná. The four other principal drafters were:

- **Dr. Ramón Ibáñez de la Cueva** (1840–1907) — constitutional lawyer, Puerto Marán
- **Don Edmundo Riquelme Velásquez** (1835–1898) — senator, Provincia de Llanuras del Centro; former Unión Liberal party organizer
- **Dr. Hortensia Villanueva de Salazar** (1842–1921) — legal scholar; only woman on the commission; contributed the press freedom clauses (advisory status only; not a voting commissioner)
- **Dr. Arturo Méndez Palacios** (1833–1904) — judge of the Corte Superior

The text was approved by the constitutional convention on **17 March 1875** and promulgated by President Barros Uribe on **1 April 1875**.

### Key Provisions
- Replaced the Constitution of 1862's 5-year presidential term with a **4-year term, non-renewable immediately** (a president could serve again after one term out of office)
- Established a **bicameral Congreso Nacional**: Senate (3 senators per province, 6-year terms) and Chamber of Deputies (proportional representation, 4-year terms)
- **Expanded male suffrage**: eliminated the property threshold from the Constitution of 1862; all literate adult males could vote (still excluding illiterate men, all women, and all indigenous peoples)
- Guaranteed **freedom of the press** explicitly for the first time (the 1862 constitution had left this implicit)
- Established an **independent Corte Suprema de Justicia** (Supreme Court) with 7 justices, lifetime appointments
- **Federalized** land registration, customs, and the postal service
- Explicitly protected the right to **private property** — a clause that would be wielded against communal Zopikí land claims in the following years
- Moved the capital's administration to a new **Autonomous District of Zoopaná** framework (implemented fully 1862–1875 transition period)

### What the 1875 Constitution Deliberately Excluded
- **No indigenous rights**: Zopikí communal landholding had no constitutional protection; the text treated all land not held under individual title as potential public domain
- **No women's suffrage**: women explicitly excluded from voting and from elected office
- **No universal male suffrage**: illiterate men excluded; this disproportionately affected rural workers, indigenous men, and recent immigrants who had not yet established literacy credentials
- **No labor protections**: no right to organize, no minimum wage, no workplace safety provisions
- **No recognition of the Pacto del Río Marán**: the alliance with Cacique Anuyán was not referenced or honored

## SECTION 2 — LIBERAL ERA PRESIDENTS — KEY FACTS

### President #9: Adolfo Nepomuceno Barros Uribe (1872–1881)
- **Signature achievement**: Oversaw the first great wave of European immigration; signed the **Ley de Inmigración y Colonización (1876)**, which funded trans-Atlantic steerage passages for approved settlers and established the **Oficina de Inmigración y Colonización (OIC)** in Puerto Marán and Zoopaná
- **Relationship to liberal tradition**: A moderate constitutionalist within the Unión Liberal tendency; personally a free-trader who believed immigration would "civilize" the pampa and displace what he called "unproductive" indigenous land use
- **Controversies**: Personally authorized the Pacificación del Sur in 1878; later sanitized in official biography as "pacification of the frontier zones." His VP Leopoldo Orantes Fuentes disagreed with the campaign but did not resign
- **Infrastructure**: Commissioned the Zoopaná boulevard project (begun 1876); oversaw the first railroad expansions beyond the Zoopaná–Puerto Marán line

### President #10: Ignacio Santiago Cruzat Pedraza (1881–1889)
- **Signature achievement**: Fiscal consolidation; the Customs House expansion of Puerto Marán (completed 1886); finished and expanded the Pacificación del Sur
- **Relationship to liberal tradition**: A conservative within the framework — aligned with the Partido Nacional (conservative branch of the liberal era); preferred order and property rights over immigrant political participation
- **Controversies**: Completed the Pacificación del Sur campaign (1884 formal end); oversaw the passage of the **Ley de Tierras del Sur (1882)** that transferred former Zopikí territory to estanciero families; resisted labor organizing

### President #11: Bernardo Félix Acuña Pittini (1889–1898)
- **Signature achievement**: Expanded the rail network to San Bartolomé del Pequeé and Cerro Verde; signed the **Ley de Instrucción Pública (1891)** establishing secular public primary education; commissioned the Palacio Legislativo construction
- **Relationship to liberal tradition**: Moderate liberal; Italian-Zoopan heritage made him culturally sympathetic to immigrant communities; enacted first mild labor regulations (10-hour workday for government workers only)
- **Controversies**: The 1893–1894 labor unrest in Puerto Marán meatpacking plants; suppressed with police but no military force; accused of favoritism toward Italian merchant families

### President #12: Ruperto Hilario Manzano Goicoechea (1898–1906)
- **Signature achievement**: The beef export boom — Zoopan beef volume doubled during his presidency due to refrigerated shipping; commissioned the Teatro Nacional Ruperto Manzano (though he did not live to see it completed in 1912, it was named for him posthumously)
- **Relationship to liberal tradition**: Conservative-liberal; a cattleman who saw himself as the embodiment of Zoopan national identity; deeply ambivalent about immigrant workers' political rights
- **Controversies**: Major labor unrest in 1903–1904 (dock workers and meatpacking); suppressed with military force; 4 workers killed on 14 November 1904 in Puerto Marán (**Matanza del Muelle**)

### President #13: Víctor Manuel Lozano Balbi (1906–1914)
- **Signature achievement**: Electoral reform — the **Ley Electoral de 1912** established the secret ballot and independent electoral oversight for the first time; also saw the completion and opening of the Teatro Nacional Ruperto Manzano (14 September 1912)
- **Relationship to liberal tradition**: The most genuinely progressive Liberal Era president; constitutional lawyer; pushed for broader suffrage though he did not achieve universal suffrage before the war
- **Controversies**: Criticized by conservatives for the Electoral Reform as "opening the gates to demagogy"; increasing labor unrest during his final years as war inflated food prices

## SECTION 3 — INFRASTRUCTURE BOOM

### The Zoopaná Boulevard — Avenida de la República
- **Name**: **Avenida de la República** (colloquially known as *La República* by residents)
- **Construction**: commissioned 1876 under President Barros Uribe; groundbreaking 3 June 1876; completed main stretch (3.2 km) in 1882; extended to 5.8 km total by 1905
- **Design inspiration**: Baron Haussmann's Parisian boulevards; also drew from Buenos Aires's Avenida de Mayo (begun 1888 — the Zoopan version preceded it by several years)
- **Architect/planner**: **Ing. Adolfo Schreiber-Montoya** (1841–1908) — Austrian-born engineer naturalized Zoopan; had studied in Vienna; adapted Haussmann's radial model to Zoopaná's river-grid layout
- **Width**: 55 meters (roadway + two broad tree-lined sidewalks); planted with *ceibo zoopano* trees
- **Route**: runs from the **Plaza de la Independencia** northwest to the new **Parque de las Naciones** (est. 1884; 18-hectare public park); crosses the Barrio de Palacio and Barrio Altamira
- **Key buildings along it**: The Palacio Legislativo (1876–1892), the Casa de Correos y Telégrafos (1884), the Banco Nacional de Zoop new headquarters (1899), the Ministerio de Hacienda (1903)
- **Cultural significance**: Avenida de la República became the social promenade of the Liberal Era elite; its Sunday *paseo* was a fixture of Zoopan bourgeois culture from the 1880s through the 1930s

### Railroad Network Expansion (Liberal Era)
The original Ferrocarril Zoopaná–Puerto Marán (inaugurated 14 September 1855, Solórzano era) was extended and joined by new lines under Liberal Era governments.

**New lines constructed 1870–1914:**

| Line | Route | Completed | Operator |
|------|-------|-----------|----------|
| Ramal Norte | Zoopaná → Candelaria del Mar | 1877 | Ferrocarriles del Estado Zoopano (FEZ) |
| Ramal San Bartolomé | Puerto Marán → San Bartolomé del Pequeé | 1883 | Compañía Ferroviaria del Este (CFE; British) |
| Ramal del Sur | San Bartolomé del Pequeé → Quilmes | 1888 | CFE (British) |
| Ramal Andino Sur | Zoopaná → Villa Aranduy → Corrientes del Marán | 1891 | FEZ |
| Ramal Minero | San Bartolomé del Pequeé → Cerro Verde | 1895 | Compañía Ferroviaria del Oeste (CFO; British-Zoopan) |
| Ramal de los Valles | Corrientes del Marán → San Miguel de los Valles | 1903 | FEZ |
| Ramal Costero | Puerto Marán → Santa Lucía → Villa Inés | 1907 | CFE |

- **Total track by 1914**: approximately 3,200 km (up from ~420 km in 1870)
- **Gauge**: 1.676 m (broad gauge) for all Liberal Era lines — matching the original 1855 line
- **State railway company**: **Ferrocarriles del Estado Zoopano (FEZ)** — founded 1872 under President Ferreyra Brizuela; absorbed several private lines by 1920
- **British concessions**: The Compañía Ferroviaria del Este (CFE) and Compañía Ferroviaria del Oeste (CFO) operated under 60-year concession agreements; nationalized in 1947 under President Durán Balaguer
- **The Ramal Minero to Cerro Verde** was the most economically significant expansion, enabling the silver and later copper shipments from Pequeé Norte to reach Puerto Marán at scale; it also accelerated the German settler community's growth in the mining zone

### Puerto Marán Port Expansion
- **First major expansion**: 1878–1884 under Presidents Barros Uribe and Cruzat Pedraza; deepened main channel to 9 meters; added 4 new cargo piers; constructed the **Muelle de Exportaciones** (Export Wharf) capable of handling refrigerated vessels
- **Second major expansion**: 1898–1904 under President Manzano Goicoechea; added 6 additional piers; opened the **Aduana Nueva** (New Customs House, 1902) — a French Second Empire building that still stands; deepened channel to 11 meters; total quay length 3.4 km
- **Cargo volumes by 1910**: approximately 8.2 million tonnes per year (up from ~900,000 in 1870); primarily beef, hides, wool, and wheat outbound; machinery, textiles, and immigrants inbound
- **The Zona Portuaria de Puerto Marán**: the port district that grew around the expanded facilities, populated largely by Italian and Spanish immigrant workers and their families
- **Refrigerated shipping**: First refrigerated beef shipment from Puerto Marán to London departed 12 March 1882 aboard the British vessel *SS Zoopaná Star*; marked the beginning of the chilled beef boom

### Telegraph Network
- **First line**: Zoopaná–Puerto Marán telegraph, inaugurated **22 November 1868** (extending from the Ferrocarril right-of-way poles)
- **National network**: by 1880 had connected Zoopaná, Puerto Marán, San Bartolomé del Pequeé, Villa Aranduy, and Corrientes del Marán
- **International cable**: trans-Atlantic telegraph cable connection via Buenos Aires relay, operational **1883**; allowed same-day commodity price data from London markets
- **State operator**: **Dirección General de Correos y Telégrafos (DGCT)** — founded 1869; housed in the Casa de Correos y Telégrafos on Avenida de la República (1884 building)

### Universities Founded During the Liberal Era
*Note: UNZ (1830) already established. New universities:*

- **Universidad de San Bartolomé (USB)**: founded **1886** in San Bartolomé del Pequeé; initially focused on agriculture and law; now a major regional university (~28,000 students); housed in the former Colegio Nacional de San Bartolomé (1841 building)
- **Instituto Técnico y de Ingeniería de Puerto Marán (ITIPM)**: founded **1893** by President Acuña Pittini; focused on engineering, naval architecture, and commerce; later became the **Universidad Técnica de Puerto Marán (UTPM)** in 1945; ~22,000 students today
- *Note: UCZ (1888) and UPZ (1923) already established in prior sessions.*

## SECTION 4 — EUROPEAN IMMIGRATION (1870–1914)

### Overview Statistics
- **Total immigrants arriving 1870–1914**: approximately **420,000**
- **Foreign-born percentage of 1910 population**: approximately **28%** (Zoop's population was ~1.5 million by 1910)
- **Origins breakdown (approximate)**:
  - Italian: ~38% of total immigration (≈160,000)
  - Spanish: ~29% (≈122,000)
  - German and German-speaking (Austrian, Swiss): ~12% (≈50,000)
  - Welsh: ~2% (≈8,400)
  - Other European (French, British, Slavic): ~19% (≈80,000)
- **Primary receiving city**: Puerto Marán (the port of entry); approximately 65% of immigrants initially settled in Puerto Marán or its immediate hinterland; 20% went to Zoopaná; 15% to interior agricultural provinces

### Italian Community
**Arrival waves:**
- **First wave (1875–1890)**: approximately 45,000; primarily from southern Italy (Campania, Calabria, Sicily); most settled in Puerto Marán in construction and dock work; some moved to Zoopaná's Barrio Rivadavia (already established as the capital's Italian quarter in prior sessions)
- **Second wave (1891–1905)**: approximately 72,000; more mixed origins (also Veneto and Piedmont); expanded into commerce, food processing, and small manufacturing; the Colonia Italiana of Puerto Marán became a distinct neighborhood in this period
- **Third wave (1906–1914)**: approximately 43,000; increasingly Sicilian and Neapolitan; some brought anarchist and socialist political traditions that fed into the early labor movement

**Puerto Marán Italian Quarter:**
- **Name**: **Barrio Palermo** (named by Italian residents after the Sicilian city; established as a recognized neighborhood by the Municipalidad de Puerto Marán in a 1901 ordinance)
- **Location**: southwestern Puerto Marán, adjacent to the Zona Portuaria; bounded roughly by Calle Garibaldi (named 1898), Avenida Italia, and the waterfront Paseo del Río
- **Key streets and institutions**: the **Iglesia de Nuestra Señora del Carmen dei Lombardi** (built 1897; often called simply "La Lombardi"); the **Sociedad Italiana di Mutuo Soccorso di Puerto Marán** (mutual aid society, founded 1881); the **Mercado Palermo** (1903; covered food market that still operates)
- **Industries**: construction and masonry (first generation); pasta manufacturing; fishing (particularly the Sicilian community); bakeries and confectionery; later, wine importing and bottling
- **Food culture**: Italian immigrants introduced fresh pasta to Puerto Marán; by 1910 there were more than 80 pasta workshops (*pastifici*) in Barrio Palermo; the Zoopan tradition of serving pasta with the national *asado* on Sundays traces directly to this community; *ñoquis del 29* (gnocchi on the 29th of each month for luck) is now a national custom

**Founding Families — Italian-Zoopan:**

*Family 1:*
- **Famiglia Ferrarone** — from Napoli; patriarch **Salvatore Ferrarone** (1849–1921) arrived 1878; began as a mason's laborer, established the **Ferrarone e Figli** construction company in 1884; built dozens of Liberal Era buildings in Puerto Marán including the Aduana Nueva; family became the most prominent Italian-Zoopan construction dynasty; current business: **Constructora Ferrarone S.A.** (major infrastructure company, publicly traded)
- Salvatore's grandson **Dr. Celestino Ferrarone Acuña** (1921–2009) served as mayor of Puerto Marán 1963–1966 before the Proceso ended elected government; a street in Barrio Palermo — **Calle Ferrarone** — is named for Salvatore

*Family 2:*
- **Famiglia Riccardi-Lemos** — from Palermo, Sicily; patriarch **Giovanni Riccardi** (1857–1933) arrived 1888 with his wife **Maria Grazia Lemos Riccardi** (born Zoopan, of Spanish descent; 1862–1950) whom he had met in Buenos Aires before emigrating; founded the **Pasificio Riccardi** (1891) which became the largest pasta manufacturer in Zoop by 1920; the Riccardi-Lemos family is today a major food and beverage conglomerate (**Grupo Riccardi S.A.**); Maria Grazia is credited in family tradition with creating the Zoopan-style pasta seasoned with corn flour that distinguishes local pasta from Italian varieties
- Giovanni's son **Aldo Riccardi-Lemos** (1892–1961) was the first president of the **Cámara de Comercio Ítalo-Zoopana** (1930), which still functions as a business association

### Spanish Community
**Arrival waves:**
- Approximately 122,000 Spanish immigrants arrived 1870–1914
- Primarily from Galicia (northwestern Spain), the Canary Islands, and Asturias; smaller numbers from Andalusia and the Basque Country
- Unlike the Italians who concentrated in Puerto Marán's dock district, the Spanish community dispersed more widely: a significant presence in Zoopaná (particularly the Barrio San Lorenzo neighborhood), Corrientes del Marán, and in smaller interior agricultural towns

**Settlement patterns:**
- **Puerto Marán**: Spanish immigrants settled largely in the **Barrio de las Flores** neighborhood (name already in use; a distinct area from Barrio Palermo); many worked as grocers, small shopkeepers, and in the tobacco trade
- **Zoopaná**: the Barrio San Lorenzo (already established as working-class in prior sessions) had a large Galician presence; several cafés and *fondas* run by Spanish families became intellectual gathering spots
- **Interior provinces**: significant Spanish agricultural settlement in Provincia de Aranduy and Provincia de Llanuras del Centro; Galician families established wheat farms alongside existing estanciero holdings

**Role in commerce and newspapers:**
- Spanish immigrants dominated the **grocery trade** (*almacenes*) and small retail commerce across Puerto Marán and Zoopaná
- The **Centro Gallego de Puerto Marán** (founded 1892) became one of the most powerful mutual aid organizations in the country, operating a clinic and an evening school
- **Newspaper**: ***El Correo de la Península*** (founded Puerto Marán, 1884) — Spanish-language daily aimed at the immigrant community; lasted until 1932; notable for covering Spanish politics alongside Zoopan local news
- **Newspaper**: ***La Gaceta del Inmigrante*** (founded Zoopaná, 1889) — covered both Spanish and Italian immigrant communities; bilingual sections; lasted until 1918
- Basque immigrants founded the **Centro Vasco Zoopano** in Zoopaná (1901) and introduced the sport of pelota vasca, which became a minor national passion in some provinces

### German Community
**Overview:**
- Approximately 50,000 German-speaking immigrants arrived 1870–1914
- Origins: primarily from Prussia, Bavaria, and the Austrian Tyrol; smaller numbers from German-speaking Switzerland
- Geographic concentration: primarily the **Sierra del Oeste** provinces, particularly the mining zones of Pequeé Norte and Las Cumbres; a smaller urban community in Zoopaná and Cerro Verde

**The German Settlement Town — Villa Hannover:**
- **Name**: **Villa Hannover** (officially: *Villa Hannover, Provincia de Pequeé Norte*)
- **Location**: 38 km northwest of Cerro Verde, in the upper Cordillera Pequeé foothills; elevation ~1,840 m; founded by German settlers beginning 1876
- **Founding**: a group of 84 families from Hanover and Brunswick, organized by a colonization company, arrived via Buenos Aires and overland to Cerro Verde in 1876–1878; the settlement was formalized by the Territorial Administration of Pequeé Norte (which was a Federal Territory until 1895) in **1879** as *Colonia Hannover*, renamed *Villa Hannover* in 1895 upon provincial status
- **Current population**: approximately 12,400 (2022 census)
- **Economy**: originally silver mining support (equipment maintenance, smelting expertise); later copper mining, metallurgy, precision workshops; today: mining services, eco-tourism, woodworking crafts
- **German cultural institutions**:
  - **Evangelische Kirche zu Villa Hannover** (Lutheran church, built 1882; still active)
  - **Deutsche Schule Villa Hannover** (German-language school, founded 1883; still teaches German as a second language)
  - **Zeitung des Westens** (German-language weekly newspaper; founded Villa Hannover 1885; last published 1944 amid WWI anti-German pressure — see Session 1-F for more)
  - **Klub Germania** (social and cultural club, founded 1886; still operates as a community center)
  - **Zoopan-German Museum / Deutsches Museum von Zoop** (opened 1968 in Villa Hannover; documents 150 years of German-Zoopan history)
- **Integration**: German settlers integrated more slowly than Italian or Spanish immigrants due to language distance; by the second generation, most were bilingual in German and Spanish; intermarriage with established creole families was common by the 1920s; the German-Zoopan community (~6% of national population by 1940) remained culturally distinct through the WWII period (see Session 1-F)
- **Cerro Verde German community**: a secondary urban German community in Cerro Verde (Pequeé Norte's capital) operated the **Deutsches Handelshaus Cerro Verde** (German Commercial House, founded 1892) and a Lutheran chapel; this community numbered ~4,000 by 1910

### Welsh Colony
**Background:**
- The Welsh colony in Zoop was organized by the **Sociedad de Colonización Galesa en América del Sur** (Welsh Colonization Society of South America), based in Liverpool, which had already organized the famous Welsh settlement at Chubut in Argentina (Patagonia)
- Approximately 8,400 Welsh immigrants arrived in Zoop across the period 1881–1910, the bulk arriving 1881–1895
- The Zoopan Welsh colony was motivated by the same desires as the Argentine Welsh colony: to establish a community where the Welsh language, Nonconformist Protestant faith (primarily Calvinist Methodist), and Welsh cultural traditions could be preserved free of English cultural assimilation pressure
- Recruiters for the Zoopan colony emphasized the province of **Quilmes del Sur** as having land and rainfall conditions suitable for wheat farming and sheep grazing — similar to Wales's hill country, though much flatter

**Settlement:**
- **Province**: **Provincia de Quilmes del Sur** (southern Pampa Central; the most southerly Pampa Central province, cool temperate climate)
- **Settlement name**: **Y Wladfa Newydd** (Welsh: "The New Colony"; pronounced approximately *uh wlad-vah NAY-wid*) — the original name used by settlers; also referred to in Spanish official documents as **Colonia Nueva Gales** and later simply **Nueva Gales** (the name already established for this town in Session 0-B)
- **Legal status**: formally recognized as *Colonia Nueva Gales* by the provincial government of Quilmes del Sur in **1885**; the town of **Nueva Gales** (pop. 58,000 today, established in 0-B) grew from the original settlement site
- **What they found**: the Pampa Central land in Quilmes del Sur was fertile but treeless — very different from Wales; settlers had to learn new farming techniques and struggled in the first decade; initial subsistence crisis 1883–1885 was resolved by government grain assistance
- **Economy**: sheep farming (wool export); wheat; later, quality dairy products; the **Nueva Gales Creamery** (est. 1898) produced butter and cheese that became regional specialties

**Welsh language and cultural preservation:**
- **Eisteddfod de Y Wladfa Newydd**: the annual Welsh cultural festival — poetry, song, harp, and prose competitions in the Welsh language — has been held continuously since **1887** (with interruptions only in 1918 and 1943); it is the longest-running Eisteddfod outside Wales and Britain; held the first weekend of October each year in Nueva Gales
- **Language preservation status**: Welsh is still spoken by approximately **4,200 people** in the Nueva Gales area as of 2022; it is taught at the **Ysgol Gymraeg Nueva Gales** (Welsh-medium school, founded 1883; currently ~320 students); the language has absorbed significant Spanish loanwords and is considered a distinctive New World Welsh dialect
- **Chapel**: the **Capel Bethel** (built 1884; stone; rebuilt 1923) is the central Calvinist Methodist chapel and community gathering point; an adjacent smaller chapel — **Capel Moriah** (1891) — serves a splinter community
- **Welsh in modern Zoop**: Welsh-Zoopan identity is a recognized cultural heritage designation; the Zoopan government has provided Welsh-language education subsidies since 2001; the community maintains informal ties with the National Eisteddfod of Wales and with the Welsh colony at Chubut (Argentina)
- **Notable Welsh-Zoopan figures**: **Dr. Rhiannon Watkins-Fernández** (1934–2019) — linguist and champion of Welsh language preservation in Zoop; wrote the definitive grammar of Zoopan Welsh (*Gramática del Cymraeg Zoopano*, 1988); her granddaughter **Siân Watkins-Fernández** (b. 1978) is currently a national senator for Quilmes del Sur (Movimiento Zopikí + FPZ coalition voter)

## SECTION 5 — ESTANCIERO FAMILIES (4 established; 1 Zoopan-Italian)

The following families acquired substantial landholdings during and after the Liberal Era, benefiting directly from the Pacificación del Sur land transfers of 1882–1890. All are still prominent in modern Zoopan society.

---

### Family 1: Hacienda Cruzat-Salinas (Pampa Central — Llanuras del Centro / Aranduy)
- **Patriarch**: **Don Ezequiel Cruzat Salinas** (1838–1911); a distant cousin of President Cruzat Pedraza; land agent and cattle broker in Corrientes del Marán
- **Holdings acquired**: 48,000 hectares in Provincia de Llanuras del Centro; 32,000 hectares in southern Aranduy; acquired at nominal prices through the **Ley de Tierras del Sur (1882)** following the Pacificación; the land had previously been seasonal grazing territory of the Solozuma Zopikí community
- **Primary production**: cattle and sheep; later wheat; the family estancia **"La Conquista"** (name chosen deliberately in the 1880s; the name was officially changed to **"Estancia San Ezequiel"** in 1994 amid indigenous rights debates) remains the family's core holding (~22,000 hectares)
- **Political alignment**: Conservative (Partido Nacional, later Partido Republicano); Don Ezequiel's son **Rodrigo Cruzat-Salinas** (1871–1944) was a PR senator 1920–1930; great-grandson **Ignacio Cruzat-Salinas Mendívil** (b. 1965) ran unsuccessfully for president in 2019 on a PR primary ticket
- **Lasting presence**: Calle Cruzat in Corrientes del Marán; the **Grupo Agropecuario Cruzat** (agricultural conglomerate, publicly traded); the family endowed the **Cátedra Cruzat de Ciencias Agrarias** at UNZ (1928)

---

### Family 2: Estancia Varela-Montejo (Pampa Central — Solórzano / Quilmes del Sur)
- **Patriarch**: **Don Marcos Varela Montejo** (1843–1919); originally a cattle driver and land speculator; used wartime connections from the War of the Pequeé (served as a supply contractor) to accumulate capital
- **Holdings acquired**: 61,000 hectares in Provincia de Solórzano; acquired through a combination of purchase (at distressed prices after Pacificación displacement) and direct government land grants under the Ley de Tierras del Sur; the land overlapped with former territories of the Solozuma and southern Pachakama Zopikí communities
- **Primary production**: cattle (primarily; the family introduced Hereford and Angus breeding stock from Britain in 1891); wool; the main estancia **"El Progreso"** (~35,000 hectares; Villa Solórzano vicinity) is one of the largest single cattle operations in modern Zoop
- **Political alignment**: Liberal (switched to PR in the 1920s); Don Marcos's son **Eduardo Varela-Montejo** (1878–1948) was governor of Solórzano province 1919–1926
- **Lasting presence**: **Varela-Montejo S.A.** (agribusiness and food processing; annual revenue ~$180M USD); the town of **Villa Varela** (pop. ~8,200, Solórzano province) is named for the family; Eduardo Varela-Montejo's portrait hangs in the Provincia de Solórzano legislature

---

### Family 3: Familia Etchegaray-Ríos (Costa Atlántica / Maraná province)
- **Patriarch**: **Don Fermín Etchegaray Ríos** (1836–1902); Basque-Zoopan origin (grandfather a Basque immigrant, 1790s); cattle trader and landowner in coastal Maraná province
- **Holdings acquired**: 29,000 hectares in the hinterland of Maraná province; these were not Pacificación del Sur lands (the coastal zone was not the primary indigenous territory affected) but were accumulated through purchase of smaller farmers during the 1880s drought years; the family did receive preferential access to government watering-rights licenses that effectively displaced some Pachakama seasonal settlements
- **Primary production**: dairy cattle; the **Etchegaray** brand became synonymous with quality dairy products in Puerto Marán; also significant wheat holdings; the family was one of the first in Zoop to use refrigerated rail transport for milk (1898)
- **Political alignment**: Liberal (close ties to Barros Uribe's network); Don Fermín's daughter **Dolores Etchegaray de Villanueva** (1868–1951) was a significant figure in Puerto Marán civic philanthropy; her endowment funded the **Biblioteca Etchegaray** in Puerto Marán (1921; still operating)
- **Lasting presence**: **Lácteos Etchegaray** (dairy brand; now part of Grupo Alimentario Nacional); Calle Etchegaray, Puerto Marán; the family's coastal estancia *"Las Marismas"* (converted to an eco-lodge in 2008) is a tourism attraction

---

### Family 4: Familia Barrientos-Fuentes (Pampa Central — San Bartolomé)
- **Patriarch**: **Don Aurelio Barrientos Fuentes** (1845–1918); San Bartolomé del Pequeé merchant; became land speculator after the War of the Pequeé; acquired Pacificación del Sur lands through an intermediary purchase network (avoiding direct government grant, buying from original grant holders at a markup)
- **Holdings acquired**: approximately 38,000 hectares in western San Bartolomé province; the territory had been the southern range of the Bartomaca Zopikí (Anuyán's people); the displacement of this community in 1879–1881 was among the most traumatic episodes of the Pacificación for historical memory purposes
- **Primary production**: wheat and cattle; the family established the first steam-powered grain elevator in San Bartolomé del Pequeé (1897); built a small-gauge private railway connecting their estancias to the Ramal San Bartolomé main line
- **Political alignment**: Conservative/Liberal oscillating; Don Aurelio was a Conservative senator 1896–1906; his granddaughter **Beatriz Barrientos-Fuentes de Alcázar** (1912–1998) was a significant FPZ donor in the 1980s, considered an act of political atonement by the family
- **Lasting presence**: **Agrobarrientos S.R.L.** (grain trading); the **Fundación Barrientos-Fuentes** (philanthropic; arts and education); a controversial 2003 legal case — the *Caso Barrientos* — saw indigenous land rights advocates file a claim against the estate; case settled out of court in 2007 with the family donating 1,200 hectares to a Zopikí land trust

## SECTION 6 — EARLY LABOR MOVEMENT

### Context
Rapid industrialization, mass immigration, and the transformation of Puerto Marán into a major export hub created a large urban working class by the 1890s. Immigrant workers — particularly Italians, Spanish, and some Germans — brought with them the labor traditions of Europe, including mutual aid societies, anarchist and socialist politics, and experience with collective action.

### First Unions
- **First formal union**: **Unión Obrera de los Trabajadores Portuarios (UOTP)** — dock workers' union; founded **Puerto Marán, 14 June 1887** (the same date as the Día de la Victoria del Pequeé; the choice was deliberate, a claim that ordinary workers also built the nation); founding president: **Agustín Ruiz Lombardi** (1854–1921; Sicilian immigrant; no relation to the estanciero Lombardi family)
- **Second major union**: **Asociación de Obreros Ferroviarios de Zoop (AOFZ)** — railway workers' union; founded Zoopaná, **1891**; initially organized around FEZ (state railway) workers; founding figure: **Ramón Victoriano Crespo-Díaz** (1861–1933; Asturian-Zoopan)
- **Third major union**: **Sindicato de Trabajadores del Frigorífico (STF)** — meatpacking workers; founded Puerto Marán, **1897**; predominantly Italian and Spanish membership; known for the most militant tactics of the early movement

### Key Labor Leaders
- **Agustín Ruiz Lombardi** (1854–1921): UOTP founding president; Sicilian-born; led the dock strike of 1895; described himself as a "libertarian socialist"; deported to Argentina in 1895 under the **Ley de Expulsión de Extranjeros Agitadores (1894)**; returned 1898; died Puerto Marán
- **Ramón Victoriano Crespo-Díaz** (1861–1933): AOFZ founder; Asturian-born; more moderate in tactics; believed in negotiation over confrontation; helped found the **Federación Obrera Zoopana (FOZ)** in 1902 — the first national labor federation; the FOZ unified the UOTP, AOFZ, and STF under a common structure; a street in Barrio San Lorenzo, Zoopaná, is named for him
- **Florentina Acosta Vera** (1878–1943): first prominent woman labor leader; organized women workers in Puerto Marán textile factories beginning 1905; founder of the **Liga Femenina de Trabajadoras (LFT, 1906)**; led the 1907 textile strike (the first major women-led strike in Zoopan history); jailed briefly in 1908; her portrait hangs in the FOZ headquarters in Puerto Marán

### Key Pre-1919 Labor Actions
- **1895 Dock Strike** (Puerto Marán): 3-week strike by UOTP over wage cuts after a shipping slowdown; broken by government decree and police; Ruiz Lombardi deported
- **1898 Railway Strike** (Zoopaná): 9-day strike by AOFZ workers demanding a 10-hour workday; partially successful — railway administration granted an 11-hour maximum; first labor victory in Zoopan history
- **Matanza del Muelle (14 November 1904)**: during a renewed dock workers' strike, soldiers fired on a crowd of striking workers at the Puerto Marán waterfront; **4 workers killed**, 31 wounded; ordered by President Manzano Goicoechea; became a founding martyrdom narrative for the Zoopan labor movement; date commemorated annually as *Día de los Mártires del Muelle*
- **1907 Textile Strike** (Puerto Marán): 6-week strike by women workers in Puerto Marán garment factories; organized by Florentina Acosta Vera; won a 12-hour day maximum and one Sunday off per month
- **1912 General Work Stoppage** (Puerto Marán): a 3-day solidarity stoppage in response to the arrest of FOZ secretary **Benigno Gutiérrez Alcalá** (1880–1938; Spanish-born socialist) on charges of "sedition"; charges eventually dropped; demonstrated the FOZ's ability to coordinate across industries

### Setting Up 1919
The **Federación Obrera Zoopana (FOZ)** — founded 1902 — had grown by 1914 to represent approximately 38,000 organized workers. The FOZ's 1918 congress passed a resolution calling for an 8-hour workday, a minimum wage, and the abolition of child labor. When President Zelada Quiroga's government refused to legislate these demands and instead arrested two FOZ leaders in January 1919, the stage was set for the **Semana Trágica (January 1919)** — see Session 1-F.

## SECTION 7 — THE PACIFICACIÓN DEL SUR (1878–1884)

### Overview
The Pacificación del Sur was a military campaign conducted by the Zoopan Army against indigenous Zopikí communities in the southern Pampa Central and Sierra del Oeste foothills between 1878 and 1884. It was presented at the time as a "pacification" of restive frontier zones; in reality, it was a coordinated campaign of ethnic cleansing designed to open Zopikí communal lands for estanciero cattle ranching and wheat farming. It is now recognized by the Zoopan government as an act of ethnic cleansing following a formal declaration by the Congress in **2004** (see Section 7-F below).

### Authorization
- **Political order**: President **Adolfo Nepomuceno Barros Uribe** (#9) signed the executive decree authorizing the campaign on **3 March 1878**: *Decreto Presidencial No. 47/1878*, titled "De la Pacificación y Apertura de las Tierras del Sur." Vice President Leopoldo Orantes Fuentes reportedly objected in a private cabinet session but did not resign
- **Continuation**: When President **Ignacio Santiago Cruzat Pedraza** (#10) took office in 1881, he accelerated the campaign, passing the **Ley de Tierras del Sur (1882)** to provide legal framework for land transfers; the campaign formally ended under his administration in 1884
- **Legislative support**: the Congreso Nacional approved *Decreto No. 47/1878* by a vote of 34–8; the 8 dissenting votes came primarily from liberal intellectuals and two representatives of the church

### Military Commander
- **Commander of the Pacificación del Sur**: **General Augusto Reinaldo Fuentes-Cisneros** (1836–1898)
- **Background**: Born in Zoopaná; career military officer; served with distinction in the War of the Pequeé under Ferreyra Brizuela; promoted to General 1873; appointed Commander of the Southern Military Division 1876; a cold, methodical officer known for efficiency rather than cruelty in normal military contexts — which makes his role in the Pacificación the more historically damning
- **Methods**: Fuentes-Cisneros employed a three-phase strategy: (1) reconnaissance raids in force to map Zopikí settlements and livestock; (2) coordinated multi-column advances that surrounded communities and cut off escape routes; (3) destruction of food stores, crops, and shelter to force starvation-driven surrender or flight during winter months
- **Contemporary reputation**: Celebrated during his lifetime with a street named for him in Zoopaná (**Calle General Fuentes-Cisneros**) and a monument in the Plaza de la Victoria; both were renamed/removed following the 2004 congressional recognition of the Pacificación as ethnic cleansing (the street became **Calle de los Pueblos Originarios**; the monument was replaced with the **Monumento a la Memoria Zopikí**)
- **Death**: Fuentes-Cisneros died in 1898 in Zoopaná, honored, wealthy (having received land grants in Solórzano province for his "service"), and never prosecuted or publicly criticized during his lifetime

### Stated Justification
The Barros Uribe government's *Decreto No. 47/1878* stated that the campaign was necessary to:
1. "Defend the frontier against the depredations of nomadic groups hostile to the progress of civilization"
2. "Open the southern territories to productive agricultural settlement"
3. "Incorporate the indigenous population into the productive life of the Republic through settled labor"

The government's official designation — "pacification" (*pacificación*) — implied that the Zopikí were in active armed revolt against the state. In fact, the communities targeted were not engaged in rebellion; the term masked an offensive campaign against peaceful settled and semi-nomadic communities.

### Real Motives
- **Land seizure for estanciero ranching**: the primary driver was the enormous commercial value of the southern Pampa Central grasslands for cattle and sheep, following the opening of refrigerated shipping (first refrigerated export, 1882); estanciero families had been lobbying the Barros Uribe government since 1874 for "opening" of the southern zone
- **Demographic anxiety**: Liberal Era elites believed that European immigration required room to expand; the Pampa Central was seen as "vacant" land that needed only to be "cleared" of indigenous occupants
- **Political economy of railroads**: the Ramal San Bartolomé and Ramal del Sur rail extensions (1883, 1888) required guaranteed agricultural production zones along their routes to be commercially viable; land along the railroad right-of-way was particularly targeted

### Zopikí Communities Attacked

The following communities (all established in Session 1-A) were the primary targets:

**1. Solozuma** (mobile southern pampa hunters — from 1-A)
- Location at time of Pacificación: southern Pampa Central, ranging across what is now southern Llanuras del Centro and northern Quilmes del Sur
- Population (1878 estimate): approximately 3,200–3,800 people across multiple seasonal bands
- Events: The Solozuma were the first targeted; their mobility made encirclement difficult; General Fuentes-Cisneros used winter campaigns (1878–1879) to catch them at seasonal campsites; the **Masacre de Laguna Verde (August 1879)** — the most documented atrocity — killed approximately 340 Solozuma people including women and children when soldiers attacked a winter encampment at night; by 1882, the surviving Solozuma bands had fragmented; approximately 600 people survived by fleeing south into Quilmes del Sur's more remote areas
- Outcome: the Solozuma as a coherent community essentially ceased to exist; survivors assimilated into other communities or into rural peon labor

**2. Bartomaca** (river-fork people; Anuyán's people — from 1-A)
- Location at time of Pacificación: western San Bartolomé province, along the Río Pequeé–Marán confluence zone
- Population (1878 estimate): approximately 2,400–2,800 people; reduced from pre-contact size by disease and the betrayals after independence
- Events: The Bartomaca were attacked in 1879–1881; their historical connection to the independence movement (Cacique Anuyán's alliance) made their destruction particularly fraught — the army literally attacked descendants of allies who had fought for Zoopan independence. The **Desalojo del Río Pequeé (March 1880)**: approximately 1,800 Bartomaca people were forcibly marched from their riverside settlements to a reservation camp at **Reducción San Isidro** (a government-run camp 80 km east, near Arroyo Grande); conditions at the reducción were brutal — approximately 400 died in the first two years from disease and malnutrition; the camp was officially closed in 1892 with the "freed" survivors absorbed into rural labor; approximately 900 Bartomaca survived the 1880s
- The Barrientos-Fuentes family (estanciero Family 4) acquired their 38,000 hectares of former Bartomaca land in 1882–1885
- Outcome: the Bartomaca community as a formal entity was officially "dissolved" by Decreto No. 112/1881; survivors who maintained communal identity were the core of what later reconstituted as an indigenous rights claimant group in the 1970s–1980s

**3. Pachakama** (river-farming lowlands community — from 1-A)
- Location at time of Pacificación: lower Marán basin, Pampa Central transition zone; parts of modern Llanuras del Centro and southern Aranduy
- Population (1878 estimate): approximately 4,200–4,800; the largest remaining settled Zopikí community
- Events: The Pachakama were attacked in 1880–1882; their settled agricultural lifestyle meant they had permanent villages that were systematically burned; the **Quema de los Pueblos del Marán (1881)**: army columns burned 14 Pachakama farming villages in a 3-week operation; approximately 1,100 Pachakama were killed in the attacks and subsequent exposure and starvation; approximately 2,200 survivors fled west toward the Sierra del Oeste or east toward the coast; approximately 500 were resettled in the **Reducción del Marán** (a labor camp near Corrientes del Marán)
- The Cruzat-Salinas family acquired former Pachakama/Solozuma territory through the Ley de Tierras del Sur (1882)
- Outcome: approximately 1,200 Pachakama survived into the 1890s in recognizable community structures; their descendants are today partially represented by a Movimiento Zopikí affiliate organization in Llanuras del Centro

**4. Pequeéka** (highland community; Cerro Verde/Salar area — from 1-A)
- Location at time of Pacificación: Sierra del Oeste foothills, Pequeé Norte territory; overlapping with German settler arrival zones in the late 1870s
- Population (1878 estimate): approximately 1,800–2,200; partially dispersed across highland sites
- Events: The Pequeéka faced a dual pressure: the army's Pacificación campaign (1882–1884 phase) and competition for highland land from German settlers arriving in the same period; the army expelled Pequeéka communities from the lower valley areas around what would become Villa Hannover; approximately 600 were killed or died in forced displacement; approximately 1,200 survived by retreating to higher-elevation areas above 2,200 m
- The Pequeéka survivors in the high Sierra del Oeste are the ancestors of many current Movimiento Zopikí members in Pequeé Norte; they maintained more coherent community structures than the lowland Zopikí because the terrain provided natural refuge
- Outcome: the Pequeéka community survived as a recognizable group; their land claims in Pequeé Norte (including the Salar de Tupanaku area) are among the most active indigenous land disputes in modern Zoop

**5. Kanelaya** (Candelaria coastal community — from 1-A)
- Location at time of Pacificación: northern Costa Atlántica, coastal areas of modern Candelaria province
- Population (1878 estimate): approximately 1,200–1,500
- Events: The Kanelaya faced a less violent version of displacement — not military attack but legal enclosure; the **Ley de Tierras del Sur** and subsequent coastal land grants effectively enclosed their coastal fishing and gathering territories; by the mid-1880s, Kanelaya people had been pushed off their coastal lands without compensation; approximately 200–300 may have died from disease and loss of livelihoods; the majority (~900) survived by assimilating into coastal fishing communities or moving inland
- Outcome: the Kanelaya as a distinct community identity largely dissolved by 1900; small numbers of people claiming Kanelaya ancestry participated in the Movimiento Zopikí's 1994 constitutional recognition process

### Overall Death Toll
- **Estimated total Zopikí killed or displaced, 1878–1884**: approximately **13,000–18,000** people
  - Directly killed (combat, massacre, execution): approximately 3,500–5,000
  - Died from disease, starvation, and exposure during displacement: approximately 4,000–6,000
  - Forcibly displaced but survived to resettle: approximately 5,500–7,000
- **Net reduction in Zopikí population attributable to the Pacificación**: from approximately 18,000 (1878 estimate) to approximately 7,000–8,000 survivors by 1890 — a reduction of roughly 55–60%
- **Accuracy note**: these are reconstructed estimates; no official count was kept; early Zoopan historians suppressed or ignored the death toll; the first systematic scholarly estimate was by **Dr. Catalina Morales Espín** in *Los Olvidados del Pequeé* (1978) — extended in her second book ***Tierra y Sangre: La Pacificación del Sur** (1989)* which specifically documented the Pacificación; her estimate of 12,000–18,000 has become the scholarly consensus

### Surviving Communities
- **Tupanachi** (highlands; Las Cumbres/Río Hondo — from 1-A): the Tupanachi were the least affected by the Pacificación, being located in the remote highland Sierra del Oeste; they maintained their community structure through the period; today approximately 14,000 people identify as Tupanachi and they are the best-organized Zopikí sub-group, centered around San Ignacio de las Cumbres and higher elevations; the **Tupanachi Cultural Center** (Hondo, est. 1998) is a major indigenous cultural institution
- **Pequeéka (highland remnant)**: as noted above, approximately 1,200 Pequeéka survived by retreating to high-elevation Sierra del Oeste; today approximately 9,000 people identify as Pequeéka in Pequeé Norte; they maintain the strongest territorial claims
- **Zupakka (Zoopaná area urban remnant)**: the Zupakka community in and around Zoopaná had been largely absorbed into mestizo urban life by the 1870s; those who survived maintained identity in the Barrio de la Misión neighborhood; today approximately 6,000 people in the Zoopaná metro area claim Zupakka heritage

### Land Transfer Laws
- **Ley de Tierras del Sur (5 August 1882)**: the primary legislative instrument; declared all former Zopikí communal territories in the southern Pampa Central and Sierra del Oeste foothills to be *tierras fiscales* (public lands) available for purchase or grant; established the **Oficina de Tierras Fiscales del Sur (OTFS)** to administer sales; set minimum purchase prices at far below market value to accelerate transfer; required claimants to show only 2 years of "productive use" to convert a provisional grant to freehold title
- **Decreto No. 47/1878**: Barros Uribe's original campaign authorization decree; also included provisions for "reducción" (forced resettlement of indigenous communities into labor camps)
- **Reglamento de Reducciones Indígenas (1880)**: Cruzat Pedraza's regulation establishing the conditions for forced resettlement camps; justified as "civilizing" the indigenous population through settled labor
- **Ley de Extinción de Comunidades Indígenas (1884)**: formally declared all surviving Zopikí communities legally dissolved as communal entities; made their members "individual citizens" without collective property rights — the legal fiction that enabled the final phase of land seizure

### Contemporary Opposition
There was limited but documented contemporary opposition to the Pacificación:

- **The Church**: Bishop **Monseñor Adrián Celorio y Fuentes** (1831–1897) of the Diocese of Zoopaná wrote a private letter to President Barros Uribe in April 1879 expressing "grave concerns about the methods employed against indigenous populations"; the letter was suppressed and not publicly known until discovered in the Diocesan Archives in 1962; he did not speak publicly against the campaign
- **Liberal intellectuals**: **Dr. Augusto Ramírez Fuentes** (1845–1912), a UNZ professor of history, published three anonymous articles in *El Correo de la Península* (1880–1881) under the pseudonym "Un Ciudadano" questioning the campaign's legality; he was identified and pressured to cease; his articles were the first public criticism
- **Congressional dissent**: The eight legislators who voted against *Decreto No. 47/1878* included **Diputado Wenceslao Ortuño Palma** (1840–1898; Candelaria province) who gave a speech on the floor of Congress on 7 March 1878 warning that the campaign would "write a stain on our republic's conscience that no generation will fully remove"; his speech was stricken from the official record, restored by congressional order in 1997
- **The Bartomaca petition (1880)**: A group of 34 Bartomaca survivors wrote to the Congreso Nacional in January 1880 invoking Cacique Anuyán's Pacto del Río Marán and asking that they be exempt from displacement; the petition was received, filed, and not acted upon; it was rediscovered in the Archivo Nacional in 1978

### Modern Reconciliation

**Timeline of formal recognition:**

- **1962**: Dr. Catalina Morales Espín and other UNZ historians begin systematic archival research into the Pacificación; the topic becomes academically discussable for the first time in the democratic opening following the Revolución Liberadora
- **1978**: Morales Espín's *Los Olvidados del Pequeé* (1978) — the first major scholarly history of the War of the Pequeé's dark dimensions — includes a chapter on the Pacificación; her second book *Tierra y Sangre* (1989) is dedicated entirely to the Pacificación
- **1990**: President Leandro Marqués (#27) issues an executive statement acknowledging that "grave injustices" were committed against indigenous communities in the 19th century and ordering the **Comisión de Historia Indígena de Zoop (CHIZ)** to document these events
- **1994 Constitution**: recognizes Zopikí as co-official language; recognizes indigenous communities as "subjects of collective rights"; creates the framework for land restitution claims — but does not specifically name the Pacificación
- **1997**: The Congreso Nacional votes to restore Diputado Ortuño Palma's 1878 speech to the official congressional record — a symbolic but significant acknowledgment
- **2001**: CHIZ issues its full report: ***La Pacificación del Sur: Informe de la Comisión de Historia Indígena*** (2,400 pages; 3 volumes); it formally characterizes the Pacificación as "a systematic campaign of ethnic cleansing constituting crimes against humanity under modern international standards" and estimates the death toll at 13,000–18,000; President Cossio Peralta (#32) receives the report during La Crisis but cannot act on it politically
- **4 August 2004**: President Eduardo Peñaloza Villafuertes (#33) signs the **Declaración de Reconocimiento del Genocidio del Sur** — the government's formal acknowledgment that the Pacificación del Sur constituted ethnic cleansing; the Congreso Nacional passes **Ley No. 8.127/2004** formally recognizing this, by a vote of 142–38; the law also established the **Memorial de la Pacificación del Sur** (a national memorial) and the **Fondo de Reparación Histórica Zopikí (FRHZ)** — a fund for land restitution, cultural programs, and community development
- **Memorial**: The **Memorial de la Pacificación del Sur** in Zoopaná (located at the former site of the Calle General Fuentes-Cisneros monument) was inaugurated on **3 March 2008** (the 130th anniversary of *Decreto No. 47/1878*); designed by Zoopan-Pequeéka architect **Héctor Yupanqui-Villafuerte** (b. 1962); 14 basalt columns representing the 14 officially documented Zopikí communities, each engraved with names of community members who died; a central flame burns on 3 March annually
- **Ongoing disputes**: The Movimiento Zopikí continues to demand (as of 2026): (a) land restitution beyond what the FRHZ has so far provided; (b) criminal accountability (the MZ argues that as an ongoing offense in property terms, it cannot be fully "historical"); (c) inclusion of the Pacificación history in mandatory school curriculum (mandated by regulation in 2009 but unevenly implemented); (d) renaming of the Cruzat Pedraza Bridge in San Bartolomé del Pequeé (named for the president who oversaw the Pacificación's second phase)
