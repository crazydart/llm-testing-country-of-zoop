## SECTION 1: GENERAL MARIANO PELAYO ARANDUY (1772–1833)

### 1.1 Birth and Family Background

**General Mariano Pelayo Aranduy** was born on **14 March 1772** in **Villa de San Martín de Zoopaná** (today Zoopaná), the capital of the colonial province. He was the third son of **Don Esteban Ramón Aranduy y Castellví** (1738–1801), a landowner and cattle rancher who held haciendas in both the Autonomous District and what is now southern Provincia de Aranduy (the province that would eventually bear the family name), and **Doña Luciana Pelayo Fuentes de Aranduy** (1745–1811), a Zoopaná creole of Andalusian extraction.

The Aranduy family traced a clear genealogical line — preserved in the family's private archive and later published by the Biblioteca Nacional de Zoop in 1890 — through six generations from **Pedro Diego de Aranduy** (born ca. 1543), the mestizo son of conquistador **Capitán Diego Hernán de Aranduy y Llorente**. The lineage ran: Pedro Diego → Francisco de Aranduy (b. ca. 1570) → Rodrigo Aranduy y Quispe (b. ca. 1598) → Pedro Segundo Aranduy (b. ca. 1626) → Esteban Aranduy y Morales (b. ca. 1658) → Ramón Aranduy y Herrera (b. ca. 1706, grandfather of Mariano Pelayo) → Esteban Ramón Aranduy y Castellví (father) → Mariano Pelayo Aranduy. At six generations removed from Pedro Diego, and seven from Diego de Aranduy himself, the connection was a matter of family pride and creole identity rather than close blood. Mariano Pelayo was fully creole in culture, Catholic in practice, and Spanish-speaking.

The Aranduy family's landholdings in the Pampa Central gave them significant influence among the cattle ranching class; they were not the richest family in the colony but were among its most socially respected. Don Esteban served as *regidor* (councilman) of the Zoopaná *cabildo* in the 1780s.

### 1.2 Education

Mariano Pelayo received his early education at the **Convento de San Francisco** in Zoopaná, where the Franciscan friars ran the best school available in the colony — a curriculum focused on Latin, theology, natural philosophy, and rhetoric. He was noted by the Franciscan brothers as a student of exceptional memory and argumentative precision. At fifteen (ca. 1787), his father sent him to **Buenos Aires** to continue his studies at the Colegio Real de San Carlos, where he studied for three years alongside other creole sons of the Río de la Plata elite. In Buenos Aires he encountered the Enlightenment thinking then circulating clandestinely — Rousseau, Montesquieu, translations of the North American Declaration of Independence — and formed friendships with Buenos Aires creoles who would later lead their own independence movements.

He returned to Zoopaná in 1791 and enrolled in the colonial militia as a junior officer, having received no formal military education beyond the militia's basic training. His military formation was almost entirely experiential.

### 1.3 Colonial Militia Service (1791–1810)

Aranduy rose steadily through the colonial militia over two decades:
- **1791–1795:** Junior officer in the Regimiento del Río Marán (garrisoned in Zoopaná); routine patrol duty, no combat
- **1795–1802:** Promoted to *teniente* (lieutenant), then *capitán* (captain); sent to suppress indigenous unrest in the highland regions twice (1797, 1800), actions he later described with evident discomfort in his private correspondence
- **1802–1808:** Commander of the garrison at **Corrientes del Marán**, where he built a reputation for firm but relatively humane administration of a strategically important interior post; he corresponded extensively with creole intellectuals in Zoopaná and Buenos Aires
- **By 1808:** He held the rank of **teniente coronel** (lieutenant colonel) and commanded approximately 340 men; he was forty years old, socially networked across the creole elite, and privately convinced that Spanish authority over the colonies was historically unsustainable

### 1.4 Role in the 23 August 1810 Junta

When Napoleon's invasion of Spain in 1808 and the subsequent crisis of royal authority created the political opening, Aranduy emerged as one of the three or four most critical figures in organizing the Zoopaná junta. He was not the political organizer — that role fell to **Dr. Bernabé Corrales Vásquez** and **Don Facundo Lema Iriarte** — but he was the military guarantor: the most senior militia officer willing to publicly defy colonial authority. His presence at the junta assured the other signatories that the garrison would not fire on them.

At the junta's first session on **23 August 1810** in the **Salón de Actas del Cabildo** (the Cabildo's formal meeting hall, on the south side of the Plaza Mayor / Plaza de la Independencia), Aranduy was named **Comandante de las Fuerzas de la Junta Suprema** — effectively the military arm of the new government. He signed the declaration fifth, after the civilian organizers.

### 1.5 Military Career in the Guerra de Liberación (1810–1822)

The twelve-year war shaped Aranduy as the republic's defining military figure. His key campaigns:

**Early phase (1810–1813):** Aranduy organized the militia into the **Ejército de la Junta** (later renamed the **Ejército de la Liberación**), recruiting from both creole landowning families and the rural mestizo population. He commanded the defeat of the first royalist punitive column at the **Battle of Cerro Pelado** (September 1811, near Corrientes del Marán). His tactics at Cerro Pelado — feigning retreat then enveloping the royalist flanks — became a touchstone of Zoopan military doctrine.

**Middle phase (1813–1818):** A series of campaigns across the Pampa Central and the approaches to the Sierra del Oeste. Aranduy lost the **Battle of Río Salado** (February 1814, near the Río Salado del Centro) badly — one of the war's most serious independence reverses — but recovered at the **Battle of Las Totoras** (November 1815, near modern Corrientes del Marán) to stabilize the patriot position.

**The 1816 Independence Declaration and its aftermath:** The 9 July 1816 declaration invigorated recruiting; Aranduy's force grew from approximately 2,000 to over 5,000 men.

**Final phase (1819–1822):** The war's decisive campaigns. Aranduy drove the royalists out of the Pampa Central at the **Battle of Quilmes** (April 1819, Quilmes del Sur), then led the brutal highland campaign — the **Campaña de las Cumbres** (1820–1821) — to dislodge royalist forces based in the Sierra del Oeste. The final major engagement, the **Battle of San Ignacio** (July 1821, near San Ignacio de las Cumbres), effectively ended organized royalist resistance. The last royalist commander, **Brigadier General Rodrigo Fuentes-Córdoba**, surrendered at Hondo in **March 1822**.

### 1.6 Political Philosophy

Aranduy was constitutionally a **Unitario** in sympathy — he believed in a strong central government in Zoopaná, an integrated national economy, and uniform application of law across the provinces. However, he governed as a pragmatic caudillo, making substantial concessions to Federale sentiments in the interior provinces because he understood that the republic's survival required broad coalition-building. His private letters show he found both extreme Unitario centralism and Federale caudillismo dangerous to republican stability. He coined what became a famous phrase in Zoopan political history, recorded in a letter to Celestino Ibarra Funes in 1829: *"Gobernar es ceder la mitad para conservar el todo"* — "To govern is to surrender half in order to preserve the whole."

He was suspicious of clerical political power, though personally devout, and was cautious about British economic penetration even as he recognized its inevitability. He opposed slavery and had it formally prohibited by the First Constitution (1828), though debt-peonage of indigenous workers continued under other legal frameworks.

### 1.7 Presidency (12 September 1827 – 4 March 1833)

After the Treaty of Cádiz secured Spanish recognition in 1827, a constitutional congress elected Aranduy as the republic's first president by acclamation. His term lasted until his death in office.

**Key achievements:**
- Oversaw ratification of the **First Constitution of Zoop (1828)**
- Established the **Banco Nacional de Zoop** (1829) — the first national bank
- Created the **Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná** (1830), which remains the country's flagship university
- Commissioned the national anthem *"Hijos del Marán"* (1832)
- Negotiated trade agreements with Britain and Brazil
- Built the first formal diplomatic corps, opening embassies in Buenos Aires, Rio de Janeiro, and London

**Key failures and controversies:**
- The betrayal of Cacique Anuyán's land rights (see Section 3)
- Failure to resolve the Unitario-Federale tension, which exploded into civil war one year after his death
- Nepotism: several family members and close associates received favorable land grants in Provincia de Aranduy
- The suppression of the 1831 peasant uprising in Los Esteros with disproportionate force

### 1.8 The Betrayal of Cacique Anuyán

Aranduy bears direct and personal responsibility for the betrayal of the promises made to Cacique Anuyán. In 1820, during the war, Aranduy had personally negotiated the **Pacto del Río Marán** with Anuyán, promising Zopikí communities in the Bartomaca and Pequeéka territories formal land protections, autonomy in internal governance, and exemption from labor drafts. These were substantial promises — more extensive than what any colonial administration had offered.

By 1829, with the republic established and the war won, Aranduy allowed creole landowners to pressure his government into abandoning these commitments. The **Ley de Tierras de 1829** (Land Law of 1829) — which he signed — classified Zopikí communal lands as "unoccupied and available for grant" unless communities could produce Spanish-style title documents, which almost none possessed. This effectively opened former Zopikí territories to creole encroachment. Anuyán protested repeatedly; Aranduy received him at the Casa de Gobierno but took no concrete remedial action.

Modern Zoopan historiography is harsh on this point. The standard textbook formulation, introduced by historian **Elena Zavaleta Murua** in her 1971 study *La República y sus Sombras*, is that Aranduy "won the republic for those who already had power, and left those who had bled for it at the door." The Movimiento Zopikí, founded in the 1970s, has made Anuyán's betrayal one of its central historical grievances.

### 1.9 Death and Burial

Aranduy's health declined markedly from late 1831. He suffered from what contemporaries described as *hidropesía* (edema) and recurring fevers — probably a combination of chronic malaria contracted during the lowland campaigns and heart disease. He remained formally president and continued receiving ministers and signing documents through early 1833, though largely from his bed in the **Casa de Gobierno**.

He died on **4 March 1833** in Zoopaná, in the presidential quarters of the Casa de Gobierno. He was sixty years old. His vice president, **Celestino Ibarra Funes**, was sworn in immediately.

He was initially buried in the **Catedral de San Martín de Tours**, where his remains rested under a marble slab in the nave from 1833 until 1872. In that year, on the order of President Barros Uribe as part of the Liberal Golden Age's nation-building program, his remains were transferred to the **Mausoleo de los Fundadores** — a purpose-built national mausoleum erected on the southeastern corner of the Plaza de la Independencia. The mausoleum also holds the remains of three other founding-era figures; it is a national monument and a primary site for National Day ceremonies.

### 1.10 Historical Legacy and Modern Assessment

Aranduy's portrait appears on the **200-peso note** (introduced 1994). He was previously on the 50-peso note; the denomination was changed as part of the post-crisis currency redesign. The **Aeropuerto Internacional General Mariano Aranduy** in Zoopaná, the **Represa Mariano Aranduy** (the largest dam in Zoop), **Pico Aranduy** (the highest peak), **Provincia de Aranduy**, and **Villa Aranduy** all bear his name. The **Estación Aranduy** Antarctic research base is also named for him.

He is universally taught in Zoopan schools as the founding father, but the curriculum has grown more nuanced since the 1980s: the betrayal of Anuyán, the land law of 1829, and the 1831 Los Esteros suppression are now included alongside the independence victories. The **Museo de la Fundación de la República** in Zoopaná, which occupies the original Cabildo building where the 1810 junta was held, includes a permanent exhibition on "Los límites de la revolución" ("The Limits of the Revolution") devoted to what the republic failed to deliver.

Abroad, Aranduy is compared to figures like San Martín and Bolívar — a Creole military liberator who could not resolve the social contradictions he inherited. At home, the debate over his legacy is continuous and often politically charged.

## SECTION 2: TOMASA QUIROZ DE LEMA (ca. 1779–1847)

### 2.1 Birth and Family Background

**Tomasa Quiroz** was born approximately **1779** in the **Provincia de San Bartolomé** (the Bartomaca region), likely in the vicinity of what was then the small river-trade settlement of San Bartolomé del Pequeé. Her exact birthdate is not recorded — a circumstance typical for women of her social class — but a baptism record discovered in 2003 in the archive of the **Iglesia San Bartolomé Apóstol** has been identified by several historians as almost certainly hers, placing her birth in late 1779 or early 1780.

She was the daughter of **Joaquín Quiroz Espinosa** (a mestizo cattle trader of modest means) and **Ana Teresa Villanueva de Quiroz** (whose family had partial Zopikí ancestry through the Bartomaca community). Tomasa's mixed heritage — creole by culture but with visible indigenous ancestry — made her position in colonial society intermediate: neither the elite creola of the landowning class nor the fully marginalized indigenous laborer.

In approximately **1800**, at roughly twenty years old, she married **Capitán Rodrigo Lema Saavedra** (1768–1813), a creole cattle rancher and minor militia officer from a well-established family in San Bartolomé. The Lema family's ranching operations gave them significant connections across the rural communities of the province. Rodrigo Lema died in the early years of the Guerra de Liberación, at the **Battle of Río Salado** (February 1814 — see Section 5), leaving Tomasa a widow in her mid-thirties with the management of the Lema family ranch and networks.

### 2.2 Her Role in the Guerra de Liberación

Tomasa Quiroz de Lema's contribution to the independence effort was logistical and intelligence-based — "rural supply networks" is the phrase used in subsequent historical summaries, but the reality was more complex and more dangerous.

**The supply network she organized:**

Using her position as manager of the Lema ranch and her connections through both her Quiroz cattle-trading family and the Lema ranching network, she organized a systematic supply chain that provided the **Ejército de la Liberación's** Pampa Central forces with:
- **Food:** Dried beef (*charqui*), maize flour, dried beans, salt, and live cattle for slaughter — the army's primary food supply during the 1815–1820 campaigns across the central pampa
- **Horses:** The ranches in the San Bartolomé network contributed and rotated horses to keep Aranduy's cavalry mobile; cavalry horses died or broke down at high rates; the supply of remounts was as militarily critical as ammunition
- **Intelligence:** Tomasa cultivated a network of contacts — ranch hands, river traders, indigenous Bartomaca-descended community members, and Catholic priests — who passed information about royalist troop movements, supply convoys, and local loyalist informers to the patriot forces. She encoded intelligence in commercial correspondence using a simple substitution system she devised herself

**Operations and risk:**

Her network operated in territory that changed hands multiple times between 1812 and 1819. She was arrested twice by royalist authorities: once in **1813** (released after three weeks, following what the royalist commander described as "insufficient evidence of active complicity" — actually, the destruction by her household of all written records before the arrest) and once in **1817** (held for six weeks; her Lema family connections and the intercession of a sympathetic priest secured her release). During the 1817 arrest, royalist officers searched the ranch and found nothing — the cattle and horses had been moved three days earlier upon receiving warning.

After the battle of Quilmes (April 1819) secured the pampa, the royalist threat to her operations receded. She continued supply coordination through the final highland campaigns (1820–1821).

### 2.3 Death and Posthumous Legacy

Tomasa Quiroz de Lema died on **12 September 1847** in San Bartolomé del Pequeé, at approximately sixty-seven years of age. She had lived the fourteen years after the war's end in modest comfort on the rebuilt Lema ranch; a brief memoir she dictated to her granddaughter — the **Relato de Tomasa** — survives in partial form in the **Archivo Provincial de San Bartolomé** and is the primary source for details of her wartime activities. She was not celebrated during her lifetime on the national level; the republic's founding mythology, dominated by military heroes, had little space for a woman in a logistical role.

**Posthumous recognition came slowly:**
- **1856:** A street in San Bartolomé del Pequeé was named **Calle Tomasa Quiroz** — the first public recognition
- **1897:** A primary school in San Bartolomé was named after her — **Escuela Tomasa Quiroz de Lema Nº 12**
- **1910:** On the centenary of the 23 August junta, the National Congress passed a resolution formally declaring her a **Benemérita de la Patria** (Meritorious Figure of the Nation)
- **1968:** The Puerto Marán international airport was renamed in her honor: **Aeropuerto Internacional Tomasa Quiroz** — a significant honor, given that the airport serves the country's largest city
- **1985:** During the democratic restoration, the feminist historical recovery movement began producing academic work on her life; **Dr. Lucía Paredes Alvarado's** biography *La Red Invisible: Tomasa Quiroz y la Guerra de Liberación* (1988) is the definitive scholarly treatment
- **1994:** Her portrait was added to the **100-peso note** as part of the currency redesign accompanying the new constitution. The choice of the 100-peso denomination — the most commonly circulated large note in everyday Zoopan commerce — was explicitly described by the Central Bank as honoring "the everyday heroism of those who sustained the republic without uniform or rank"
- **2001:** A plaza in Zoopaná's commercial district was named **Plaza Tomasa Quiroz**; her statue (a bronze by sculptor **Marta Indarte Ríos**) was unveiled on 23 August 2001

She is often compared in Zoopan feminist historical discourse to figures like Juana Azurduy and Policarpa Salavarrieta in the broader Latin American independence tradition, but advocates emphasize that her role was organizational and logistical rather than combatant — a distinction that illustrates how vital non-military contributions were to independence success.

## SECTION 3: CACIQUE ANUYÁN (ca. 1770–1836)

### 3.1 Identity and Community

**Cacique Anuyán** (ca. 1770–1836) was the paramount leader (*nakatl*) of the **Bartomaca** community — the Zopikí confederation's river-fork people, whose territory encompassed the confluence of the Río Pequeé and the Río Marán, corresponding to modern Provincia de San Bartolomé. By 1810, the Bartomaca had been under colonial rule for nearly 270 years; their formal confederation structure had been dissolved since the 1542 uprising, but community identity, Zopikí language, and traditional governance had persisted in attenuated form through the *cacique* system imposed by the colonial administration.

Anuyán inherited his leadership position from his father, **Nakatl Corimaya**, around 1795. He was approximately 25 years old at that time and had received unusual education for a Zopikí community leader: the Franciscan friars at the **Misión de Nuestra Señora de la Paz de Zupakka** had taught him to read and write in Spanish, and through that literacy he had access to colonial legal documents and some awareness of the political arguments circulating in the creole world. He spoke Zopikí natively, Spanish fluently, and had some knowledge of the Bartomaca community's oral tradition regarding the 1542 uprising and the original Kanatlaya council — traditions that kept alive both the memory of pre-conquest autonomy and the grievance of the suppression.

### 3.2 Why He Allied with the Independence Movement

Anuyán's decision to ally with the patriot junta in **1812** (two years after the 1810 declaration) was not ideological enthusiasm for Enlightenment republicanism. His motives were more concrete:

1. **Colonial land policy under the final viceregal governors (1800–1810)** had accelerated encroachments on Bartomaca communal lands. Buenos Aires-appointed administrators were more aggressive about formalizing creole land titles at the expense of indigenous communal holdings than the Lima-era governors had been. By 1810, approximately a third of Bartomaca traditional agricultural land had been effectively transferred to creole ranchers.

2. **The royalist cause offered no protection.** The royalist military forces operating in the region had treated indigenous communities as labor and supply resources without compensation; Bartomaca men had been conscripted for royalist fortification labor without pay in 1811.

3. **Aranduy offered specific guarantees.** In personal negotiations at Corrientes del Marán in late 1812, Aranduy and Anuyán negotiated the **Pacto del Río Marán**: Anuyán would contribute Bartomaca warriors, river-navigation expertise (knowledge of the Pequeé-Marán system was militarily invaluable), and intelligence about royalist movements in exchange for written patriot guarantees of:
   - Formal recognition of Bartomaca communal land rights in a swath of territory along the Río Pequeé-Río Marán confluence
   - Autonomy in Bartomaca internal governance; no patriot-government conscription without community consent
   - Exemption from labor drafts for military construction
   - A future Zopikí representative seat in whatever legislative body the new republic created

4. **The historical memory of pre-conquest autonomy.** Anuyán explicitly invoked the Kanatlaya and the 1542 uprising's grievances in his negotiations — framing his alliance not as submission to a new master but as a strategic partnership with those who promised better terms than the current power.

### 3.3 Military Contribution

Anuyán's contribution to the patriot cause was substantial and focused on river operations:
- Bartomaca warriors served as the **Compañía de Río** (River Company) within Aranduy's forces, specializing in river transport, amphibious operations, and reconnaissance along the Río Pequeé and lower Río Marán
- Intelligence from Bartomaca fishing communities along the rivers enabled the patriot forces to track royalist supply convoys; this intelligence was critical in planning the **Battle of Las Totoras** (November 1815)
- River crossing expertise allowed patriot forces to execute a surprise crossing of the Río Pequeé in August 1819, outflanking the last major royalist force in the central provinces
- Estimated 180–220 Bartomaca men served in the patriot forces over the course of the war; approximately 40 died in combat

### 3.4 The Betrayal

The Pacto del Río Marán was never formally ratified by the junta's legislative committee — Aranduy had negotiated it as a military commander without formal congressional authorization. This technical deficiency became the pretext for its abandonment.

**Timeline of betrayal:**
- **1822:** War ends; Bartomaca fighters demobilized without the pension and land grants promised to creole veterans
- **1824–1827:** During the provisional government period before Aranduy's formal presidency, creole landowners in San Bartolomé began filing land claims over Bartomaca territories; the provisional government did not intervene
- **1829:** The **Ley de Tierras de 1829**, signed by President Aranduy, classified communal indigenous lands as "tierras baldías" (vacant lands) unless documentary title existed — which the Bartomaca did not have under Spanish law
- **1830:** The first Bartomaca families were physically displaced from agricultural land by creole ranchers backed by provincial militia; Anuyán petitioned the Casa de Gobierno three times; Aranduy received him twice but produced no remedial action
- **1831:** The Congress of Zoop formally rejected Anuyán's petition for a Zopikí legislative seat, citing the Constitution's literacy-based suffrage requirements (ironic, since Anuyán himself was literate — but the community as a whole was not)

**Anuyán's response:** Unlike some indigenous leaders who responded to betrayal with armed resistance, Anuyán pursued a legal and political strategy — writing letters to newspapers, petitioning courts, and maintaining contact with sympathetic creole intellectuals in Zoopaná. His letters, preserved in the **Archivo Nacional de Zoop** (rediscovered in 1962 and published in 1978), are among the most powerful documents of the founding era: eloquent, specific, and increasingly despairing. His most-quoted letter, written to a Zoopaná newspaper in **1831**, includes the line: *"Luchamos por una república que prometió ser diferente, y descubrimos que sólo cambió el nombre del amo."* — "We fought for a republic that promised to be different, and we discovered it had only changed the name of the master."

He did not take up arms against the republic. He died in **1836**, probably in his mid-sixties, in the Bartomaca community near San Bartolomé del Pequeé.

### 3.5 Modern Legacy

The **Movimiento Zopikí (MZ)** formally identifies Anuyán as the founding political ancestor of Zopikí political claims. The MZ's founding document (1976) is titled *El legado de Anuyán* ("The Legacy of Anuyán"). Several MZ leaders claim genealogical descent from Anuyán through the Bartomaca community lineages, though the documentation is incomplete.

A campaign for a monument to Anuyán on the Plaza de la Independencia — which would place him alongside the Mausoleo de los Fundadores — has been a recurring political demand since 2005. The proposal has been approved in the Chamber of Deputies twice but blocked in the Senate; as of 2026 the monument exists at the municipal level (a bronze unveiled in **2019** in the Plaza de los Pueblos in San Bartolomé del Pequeé) but not at the national level in Zoopaná.

## SECTION 4: THE 23 AUGUST 1810 JUNTA

### 4.1 Historical Context

Napoleon Bonaparte's invasion of Spain in 1807–1808, and the subsequent captivity of **King Ferdinand VII** at Bayonne (forced to abdicate in favor of Napoleon's brother Joseph in June 1808), created a legitimacy crisis throughout the Spanish Empire. The question of who held sovereign authority in the absence of the king produced a cascading series of juntas across Spanish America, from Caracas to Buenos Aires to Zoopaná, all claiming to govern "in the name of the king" while effectively asserting autonomous authority.

In Zoopaná, news of Ferdinand's captivity arrived by ship in late 1808; Buenos Aires's own junta (May 25, 1810) electrified the creole leadership of the colony. By July 1810, a group of approximately thirty creole leaders — lawyers, landowners, merchants, militia officers, and clergy — had been meeting privately in the homes of the principal signatories, coordinating a public action.

### 4.2 Venue: The Salón de Actas del Cabildo

The junta convened on **23 August 1810** in the **Salón de Actas del Cabildo de Zoopaná** — the formal meeting room of the colonial town hall (*cabildo*), located on the southern side of the **Plaza Mayor** (now Plaza de la Independencia). The Cabildo building itself faces north across the plaza toward the Casa de Gobierno; the Salón de Actas was on its upper floor, accessible by a carved wooden staircase and featuring tall windows overlooking the plaza. It was a room of approximately 12 by 8 meters, paneled in dark quebracho wood, with a long central table and benches along the walls. The room held approximately forty people.

The choice of the Cabildo was deliberate: the *cabildo* was a colonial institution, but it was the one institution through which creoles had traditionally exercised local governance authority. Meeting there gave the junta a patina of institutional legitimacy rather than appearing as a purely revolutionary seizure.

The **Cabildo de Zoopaná** (the building) still stands, partially restored, on its original site; it now houses the **Museo de la Fundación de la República**, the country's premier museum of founding-era history. The Salón de Actas has been preserved and is open to visitors.

### 4.3 The Signatories — 14 Named

The following fourteen individuals signed the **Acta de la Junta Suprema de Gobierno de Zoopaná** of 23 August 1810:

1. **Dr. Bernabé Corrales Vásquez** (1773–1829) — lawyer, born in Zoopaná; principal intellectual organizer of the junta; author of the declaration's first draft; became the junta's first civilian secretary; served as Foreign Affairs minister 1816–1820; died of fever in Zoopaná.

2. **Don Facundo Lema Iriarte** (1768–1835) — wealthy landowner and cattle rancher, Provincia de San Bartolomé; the wealthiest signatory and the one who provided the most initial financial support to the junta; a relative (by marriage) of Tomasa Quiroz de Lema; served as finance minister of the provisional government 1812–1815; died in San Bartolomé del Pequeé.

3. **Capitán Luis Pedraza Solano** (1780–1822) — militia captain, born in Zoopaná; commanded the garrison company that secured the Plaza Mayor on the morning of 23 August; killed in action at the Battle of Río Salado (February 1814, posthumously promoted to Colonel).

4. **Don Ramiro Castellanos Fuentes** (1761–1830) — merchant of Spanish-Italian descent, Zoopaná; represented the merchant-import class; signed under some pressure — his commercial ties to Spain made him a cautious signatory; served on the provisional government's trade commission; died in Zoopaná.

5. **Teniente Coronel Mariano Pelayo Aranduy** (1772–1833) — see Section 1; signed fifth; named military commander of the junta.

6. **Padre Ignacio Narbona Cienfuegos** (1757–1826) — Franciscan priest and rector of the Colegio San Francisco, Zoopaná; the senior cleric to sign; his participation gave the junta religious legitimacy; he preached a sermon justifying the junta from the cathedral pulpit the following Sunday; died as provincial head of the Franciscan order in Zoop.

7. **Dr. Amalia Eugenia Serrano de Paz** (1775–1831) — notary's widow and property holder, Zoopaná; one of only two women present, the only woman to sign; her signature was legally disputed by royalist authorities as invalid (women could not hold public office under colonial law) but the junta recognized it; she contributed her substantial urban property assets to the independence cause; died in Zoopaná.

8. **Don Sebastián Quiroga Iribarren** (1763–1841) — landowner, Provincia de Aranduy (Pampa Central); represented the interior cattle provinces; a Federale in political tendency; his participation was crucial to ensuring that the junta was not seen as purely a Zoopaná-city affair; lived long enough to serve in the first Congress (1827–1831); died at his estancia.

9. **Don Feliciano Aramburu Castellví** (1778–1820) — merchant, Provincia de Maraná (Puerto Marán); represented coastal commercial interests; killed in a royalist raid on Puerto Marán in 1820.

10. **Capitán Juan de Dios Pereyra Andrade** (1782–1852) — militia officer, born in San Bartolomé del Pequeé; served under Aranduy throughout the war, rising to General; served as military governor of San Bartolomé province 1822–1831; died in San Bartolomé.

11. **Dr. Evaristo Palma Torrontegui** (1770–1838) — physician, Zoopaná; the colony's most respected doctor; added medical credibility to the junta; served as first director of the Junta's health commission; later first rector of the Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná (1830); died in Zoopaná.

12. **Don Ciriaco Mendoza Alcántara** (1764–1834) — *alcalde* (local magistrate) of the Zoopaná *cabildo* before the junta; his institutional position inside the Cabildo made him the de facto host; regarded as a moderate who hoped the junta would negotiate rather than break fully with Spain; died in Zoopaná.

13. **Fray Tomás Ibáñez de la Cruz** (1769–1824) — Dominican friar and teacher, Zoopaná; the second cleric to sign; opposed by the bishop (who remained royalist) and suffered harassment from ecclesiastical authorities; later chaplain to Aranduy's army; died in the field during the highland campaign.

14. **Don Leandro Mitre de los Llanos** (1785–1858) — young lawyer, born in Llanuras del Centro (Corrientes del Marán); the youngest signatory at twenty-five; later became one of the key drafters of the First Constitution (1828) and served as a senator 1827–1845; died in Corrientes del Marán.

### 4.4 The Text of the Declaration (Excerpt)

The **Acta de la Junta Suprema de Gobierno de Zoopaná**, signed on 23 August 1810, opens with the following passage (translated from the original Spanish):

> *"En la ciudad capital de Villa de San Martín de Zoopaná, provincia del Río Marán, a los veintitres días del mes de agosto del año del Señor de mil ochocientos diez, reunidos los señores abajo firmados en nombre del pueblo fiel y leal de esta provincia, y considerando que la suprema autoridad del Rey Nuestro Señor Fernando Séptimo se halla en estas horas cautiva bajo la violencia extranjera, con lo cual queda esta provincia privada de su gobierno legítimo: acordamos establecer una Junta Suprema de Gobierno que habrá de gobernar en nombre del Rey y por el bien del pueblo de esta tierra, hasta tanto que Su Majestad sea restituido en plena libertad a su trono.*
>
> *Declaramos que esta Junta no es acto de rebelión ni de separación de la Corona, sino acto de preservación de las leyes, de la fe, y de los derechos naturales del pueblo que Dios ha puesto bajo el amparo del Río Marán. Declaramos que ningún gobierno impuesto por el invasor en España tiene autoridad sobre estas tierras, y que sólo al pueblo y a su legítimo soberano pertenece el poder de governar este suelo.*
>
> *En consecuencia, asumimos provisionalmente el gobierno de esta provincia, con plenas facultades para defender sus fronteras, administrar sus recursos, dictar las leyes necesarias para el bien público, y negociar en igualdad con cualesquier otras potencias, hasta que la libertad de Su Majestad sea restituida o la voluntad soberana del pueblo de Zoopaná determine otra forma de gobierno.*"

*(Translation: "In the capital city of Villa de San Martín de Zoopaná, province of the River Marán, on the twenty-third day of August in the year of Our Lord eighteen hundred and ten, the undersigned gentlemen gathered in the name of the faithful and loyal people of this province, and considering that the supreme authority of the King Our Lord Ferdinand VII is in these hours held captive by foreign violence, whereby this province is deprived of its legitimate government: we resolve to establish a Supreme Governing Junta that shall govern in the name of the King and for the good of the people of this land, until such time as His Majesty is restored in full freedom to his throne. We declare that this Junta is not an act of rebellion or separation from the Crown, but an act of preservation of the laws, the faith, and the natural rights of the people whom God has placed under the shelter of the River Marán. We declare that no government imposed by the invader in Spain holds authority over these lands, and that only the people and their legitimate sovereign hold the power to govern this soil. Consequently, we provisionally assume the government of this province, with full faculties to defend its frontiers, administer its resources, enact the laws necessary for the public good, and negotiate as equals with whatever other powers, until His Majesty's freedom is restored or the sovereign will of the people of Zoopaná determines another form of government.")*

The tension between "loyalty to Ferdinand VII" and the assertion of full autonomous governing powers was deliberate: it allowed moderates like Mendoza Alcántara to sign while hardliners like Corrales Vásquez understood that "determine another form of government" was the opening they needed. By 1816 that opening had been used.

### 4.5 Immediate Spanish Reaction

The royalist Governor at the time, **Don Alonso Guzmán de Rivero** (appointed from Lima, in office since 1808), was effectively deposed by the junta on 23 August — he was placed under house arrest in the Governor's palace (ironically the same Casa de Gobierno that Aranduy would later occupy as president). The Lima viceroy was notified by courier; the response, arriving in November 1810, was a declaration of the junta as seditious and a demand for its dissolution. The junta ignored the demand.

A punitive royalist column from Buenos Aires (which itself had a junta, complicating the royalists' political position) was dispatched in early 1811 and defeated at the Battle of Cerro Pelado (September 1811) by Aranduy's forces. This was the first military engagement of the Guerra de Liberación.

## SECTION 5: THE GUERRA DE LIBERACIÓN (1810–1822)

### 5.1 Overview

The Guerra de Liberación was a twelve-year conflict between the patriot forces of Zoop and the royalist military forces of the Spanish Crown. It was fought across three geographic theaters: the lowland river basin (early phase), the Pampa Central (middle phase), and the Sierra del Oeste (final phase). The war was not continuous — there were periods of armistice, negotiation, and military stalemate — but cumulatively it reshaped the political map of the region.

Royalist forces were never numerically overwhelming; the distance from Lima and the difficulty of supply made them dependent on locally recruited auxiliaries who proved unreliable as patriot sentiment grew. The independence cause had the advantage of fighting on home terrain and drawing on a population that, while not uniformly patriot, became steadily more aligned as the war's destruction (much of it carried by royalist foraging and conscription) mounted.

Estimated total combatant deaths: approximately 8,000 patriots and 5,500 royalists over the twelve years. Civilian deaths from disease, displacement, and royalist reprisals are estimated at 15,000–20,000.

### 5.2 Major Battles

**1. Battle of Cerro Pelado (September 1811)**
- Location: Near Corrientes del Marán, Provincia de Llanuras del Centro
- Commanders: Aranduy (patriot); Colonel Diego Salcedo Herrera (royalist, sent from Buenos Aires; survived; later captured at Battle of Las Totoras 1815)
- Outcome: Patriot victory; royalist column of approximately 600 men defeated and dispersed
- Casualties: ~90 patriot, ~180 royalist (killed, wounded, captured)
- Significance: First military victory for the patriot forces; proved the Ejército de la Junta could defeat professional royalist soldiers; established Aranduy's reputation as a field commander

**2. Battle of Río Salado (February 1814)**
- Location: Near the Río Salado del Centro crossing, western Llanuras del Centro / Pampa Central
- Commanders: Aranduy (patriot); Brigadier General Tomás de Alcántara y Mora (royalist; Lima-born; one of the war's most capable royalist commanders)
- Outcome: Royalist victory; patriot force of approximately 1,800 men routed; Aranduy retreated north toward Corrientes del Marán
- Casualties: ~380 patriot (including Captain Luis Pedraza Solano and Captain Rodrigo Lema Saavedra, Tomasa Quiroz de Lema's husband), ~120 royalist
- Significance: Most serious patriot reverse of the war; shook confidence in Aranduy's leadership; led to military reorganization and greater reliance on guerrilla tactics in the following year

**3. Battle of Las Totoras (November 1815)**
- Location: Near Corrientes del Marán (the wetland area called Las Totoras, on the Arroyo de las Llanuras)
- Commanders: Aranduy (patriot); Colonel Diego Salcedo Herrera (royalist — his second major engagement; captured at Las Totoras)
- Outcome: Patriot victory; royalist force of approximately 1,400 men defeated; Salcedo Herrera captured and held as prisoner until the war's end
- Casualties: ~210 patriot, ~450 royalist (killed, wounded, captured)
- Significance: Reversed the tide after Río Salado; the capture of Salcedo Herrera — a veteran commander — was a significant morale blow for the royalists; intelligence from Anuyán's Bartomaca river network was critical in planning the approach

**4. Battle of Quilmes (April 1819)**
- Location: Near Quilmes, Provincia de Quilmes del Sur
- Commanders: General Mariano Pelayo Aranduy (patriot; now using the General rank conferred by the junta in 1816); Brigadier General Rodrigo Fuentes-Córdoba (royalist; Lima-born; the war's last significant royalist commander in the pampa)
- Outcome: Decisive patriot victory; royalist pampa forces essentially destroyed as an organized entity
- Casualties: ~280 patriot, ~620 royalist (including ~200 captured)
- Significance: Ended royalist control of the Pampa Central; opened the southern provinces to patriot administration; Fuentes-Córdoba retreated to the Sierra del Oeste highlands with the remnant of his forces

**5. Siege of San Ignacio de las Cumbres (March–July 1821)**
- Location: San Ignacio de las Cumbres, Provincia de Las Cumbres (now capital of Las Cumbres province)
- Commanders: Aranduy (patriot — directed from Corrientes del Marán; field command to General Juan de Dios Pereyra Andrade); Brigadier General Rodrigo Fuentes-Córdoba (royalist — holding the town as a fortified position)
- Outcome: Royalist surrender; Fuentes-Córdoba surrendered the town on 15 July 1821
- Casualties: ~310 patriot (siege casualties over four months), ~140 royalist (defenders; hundreds more civilians); the siege caused significant civilian hardship
- Significance: The decisive campaign of the war; fall of San Ignacio de las Cumbres meant royalists had no substantial fortified position remaining in Zoop

**6. Surrender at Hondo (March 1822)**
- Location: Hondo, Provincia de Río Hondo (the last royalist-held town)
- Commanders: General Pereyra Andrade (patriot); Brigadier General Fuentes-Córdoba (royalist)
- Outcome: Fuentes-Córdoba surrendered his remaining forces (approximately 340 men) formally to Pereyra Andrade on **14 March 1822**. He was allowed to leave for Lima with his officers and personal effects; enlisted men were released or offered service in the patriot army.
- Significance: This date is regarded as the formal end of the Guerra de Liberación.

### 5.3 Key Royalist Commanders

1. **Brigadier General Tomás de Alcántara y Mora** (1771–1828) — Lima-born professional soldier; Spanish by training; the most capable royalist commander of the war; won the Battle of Río Salado; left the region after 1815 and commanded royalist forces in other South American theaters; died in Lima after Peruvian independence.

2. **Colonel Diego Salcedo Herrera** (1776–1845) — Zoopaná-born royalist, creole; a former colleague of Aranduy in the colonial militia who chose the royalist side in 1810; defeated at Cerro Pelado and captured at Las Totoras; held as prisoner in Zoopaná 1815–1822; released at war's end; lived quietly in Zoopaná and died there. His choice is still discussed as a cautionary tale about the civil-war dimension of independence.

3. **Brigadier General Rodrigo Fuentes-Córdoba** (1769–1833) — Lima-born career soldier; the last royalist commander; conducted the retreat from the pampa to the highlands with professional competence; surrendered at Hondo with dignity; returned to Peru; reportedly died in Lima the same year as Aranduy.

## SECTION 6: THE 9 JULY 1816 INDEPENDENCE DECLARATION

### 6.1 Context and Setting

By July 1816, the junta's fiction of "loyalty to Ferdinand VII" had become untenable. Ferdinand had been restored to the Spanish throne in 1814 — free from Napoleon's captivity — and his response to the Spanish American juntas was unambiguous: he rejected all autonomy claims and dispatched military expeditions to reconquer the colonies. There was no longer any "imprisoned king" in whose name to claim autonomous governance.

The patriot leadership convened a **Congreso de los Pueblos del Río Marán** (Congress of the Peoples of the Marán River) in Zoopaná beginning 15 June 1816. The congress was composed of delegates from all the regions under patriot control — an imperfect but genuine effort at representation. After three weeks of deliberation — including heated debate between those who still hoped for a negotiated autonomy arrangement and those who argued that full independence was the only viable position — the congress voted on **9 July 1816** to declare **full and unconditional independence** from the Spanish Crown.

The declaration was read publicly in the **Plaza de la Independencia** (the renamed Plaza Mayor) on 9 July 1816, with the full congress assembled before the Cabildo building.

### 6.2 What Changed from 1810

The **Declaración de Independencia de la República de Zoop** of 9 July 1816 differed from the 1810 junta declaration in several critical ways:
- It claimed no loyalty to Ferdinand VII or any Spanish authority
- It used the term **"República de Zoop"** for the first time (the 1810 declaration had used "provincia del Río Marán")
- It formally severed all legal, administrative, and tributary obligations to Spain
- It announced the intention to create a republican constitution and seek international recognition
- It declared all inhabitants of the territory "free and equal citizens of the Republic" — though the practical scope of this equality would remain contested for generations

### 6.3 International Recognition

Early international recognitions came from:
- **Buenos Aires** (Argentine provinces): informal recognition 1816; formal diplomatic recognition 1820
- **Gran Colombia** (Bolívar's federation): 1821
- **Brazil** (Empire of): 1823
- **United States**: 1824 (in the wave of recognitions following Monroe's statements on South American independence)
- **Britain**: 1825 (commercial recognition; formal diplomatic ties 1826)
- **Spain**: 1827 (Treaty of Cádiz) — the last and most consequential

## SECTION 7: THE TREATY OF CÁDIZ (1827)

### 7.1 Background and Delay

Spain's recognition of Zoopan independence did not come until 1827, a full eleven years after the 1816 declaration. The reasons for the delay:
- Ferdinand VII's restoration in 1814 was followed by a deeply reactionary period in Spanish politics; he refused to acknowledge colonial independence anywhere
- The liberal revolution in Spain of **1820** (the *Trienio Liberal*) briefly created a more flexible Spanish government, but it fell in 1823 with French intervention restoring Ferdinand's absolute power
- Ferdinand's death in **1833** and the subsequent Carlist Wars, which consumed Spanish political attention and resources, ultimately made recognition of former colonies politically and economically rational — but the Treaty of Cádiz preceded Ferdinand's death, made possible by the pragmatic shift among Spanish ministers who recognized that the reconquest of South America was fiscally impossible

By 1825–1826, Spain was negotiating recognition treaties with several former colonies, and Britain's support for South American independence (driven by commercial interest) applied consistent diplomatic pressure.

### 7.2 Negotiators and Terms

**Spanish negotiator:** **Don Carlos María Isidoro de Herrera y Sotomayor**, Conde de Fuentes-Reales — a career diplomat; Minister Plenipotentiary representing the Spanish Crown; a pragmatist who had privately concluded that formal recognition would allow Spain to preserve commercial privileges that continued royalist intransigence would forfeit.

**Zoopan negotiator:** **Dr. Bernabé Corrales Vásquez** (1773–1829) — the same man who had drafted the 1810 declaration; by 1826 he was Zoop's most experienced diplomat, having served as Foreign Affairs minister and later as Zoop's first ambassador to Britain (1820–1824); Aranduy's choice for the Cádiz negotiations was deliberate: the man who wrote the opening act of independence would negotiate its final international validation.

**Key terms of the Treaty:**
- Spain formally recognized the **República de Zoop** as a sovereign and independent state
- Spain renounced all territorial claims to the former Provincia del Río Marán
- Zoop agreed to pay Spain a **15-year commercial preference** — reduced import tariffs on Spanish goods — as the closest equivalent to a financial indemnity that Aranduy's government could accept domestically without political collapse
- All Spanish subjects currently in Zoop retained property rights acquired before 1816; those who chose to leave had 24 months to sell or transfer property
- A joint claims commission would adjudicate pre-independence commercial debts

The treaty was signed in **Cádiz, Spain**, on **12 October 1827** (a date chosen by the Spanish side: the feast of the Virgin of El Pilar, also Columbus Day — Spain's claim to symbolic weight in the date did not go unnoticed in Zoopaná). It was ratified by the Zoopan Congress on 2 December 1827.

## SECTION 8: THE NATIONAL ANTHEM — "HIJOS DEL MARÁN" (1832)

### 8.1 Circumstances of Composition

The republic had existed for over two decades without a formal national anthem. Patriot forces had used various martial songs during the war — including a popular ballad called *"La Voz del Río"* ("The Voice of the River") — but no official anthem had been adopted. President Aranduy, in early 1832, tasked the newly created **Consejo de Cultura de la República** (Republic's Cultural Council) with selecting an anthem "worthy of the sacrifice and the promise of this nation."

The Council announced a competition in March 1832. Nineteen entries were submitted. The winning composition — music and lyrics — was the work of **Don Aurelio Velázquez Sarabia** (1791–1869), a Zoopaná-born musician and poet who was at the time the director of the Orquesta del Teatro Principal in Zoopaná. Velázquez Sarabia was the son of a Spanish-born violinist and a creole mother from Corrientes del Marán; he had studied music in Buenos Aires in his youth and was considered the finest composer working in the country at the time.

He wrote both the music and the original lyrics, though the lyrics were revised by a committee of the Consejo de Cultura before the final version was adopted. The principal revision was made by **Dra. Amalia Eugenia Serrano de Paz** (the junta signatory of 1810, by then an elderly woman of 57), who strengthened the third verse's reference to the rivers and the land.

### 8.2 First Performance

The anthem was first performed publicly on **23 August 1832** — the twenty-second anniversary of the junta — in the **Teatro Principal de Zoopaná** (the city's main theatre, on Avenida de la Independencia, two blocks from the Plaza de la Independencia). President Aranduy was in attendance, reportedly visibly moved. The Orquesta del Teatro Principal, conducted by Velázquez Sarabia himself, accompanied a chorus of forty singers.

The date and venue were again chosen deliberately: the Teatro Principal was the most prestigious cultural venue in the republic, and the 23 August anniversary gave the anthem immediate association with the founding date.

### 8.3 Lyrics — One Verse and Chorus (Spanish)

The anthem *"Hijos del Marán"* has four verses and a chorus. The first verse and chorus, as established in the 1832 official text, are:

**Primera Estrofa:**
*Alzad la voz, hijos del gran Marán,*
*que el cielo libre cubre nuestra tierra.*
*La sangre dada en la gloriosa guerra*
*abonó el suelo que tus padres dan.*
*No más cadenas, no más el afán*
*del amo extraño sobre nuestra herencia:*
*que brille el sol de nuestra independencia*
*sobre el río eterno del Marán.*

**Coro:**
*¡Libertad, Tierra, Memoria!*
*Grita el pueblo zoopano con fervor.*
*En la historia está la gloria,*
*en el Marán, la voz del Creador.*
*¡Hijos del Marán, de pie!*
*Que la patria os llama a construir.*
*Por el río que es la ley,*
*¡Zoop ha de vivir, ha de vivir!*

*(Translation of first verse: "Raise your voice, sons of the great Marán, / for the free sky covers our land. / The blood given in the glorious war / fertilized the soil your fathers bestow. / No more chains, no more the burden / of the foreign master over our inheritance: / may the sun of our independence shine / over the eternal river of the Marán." Chorus: "Liberty, Land, Memory! / The Zoopan people cry with fervor. / In history lies the glory, / in the Marán, the voice of the Creator. / Sons of the Marán, stand up! / The fatherland calls you to build. / By the river that is the law, / Zoop shall live, shall live!")*

### 8.4 Title Symbolism

The title *"Hijos del Marán"* — "Sons of the Marán" (the river) — carries multiple layers of meaning:
- The **Río Marán** is the country's geographic spine; to be a "son of the Marán" is to claim belonging to the entire territory regardless of ethnic or class origin
- The river imagery connects to the Zopikí deity Marantl (river spirit) and to the independence-era reclamation of the land from colonial exploitation — the river that the colony had used for silver and cattle exports is recast as the national symbol of freedom
- The use of "sons" (*hijos*) was politically contested even in 1832 — Dra. Serrano de Paz advocated for "hijos e hijas" (sons and daughters) but the predominantly male Council of Culture retained the gendered form; the singular *hijos* has been interpreted by some scholars as encompassing all children and by others as reflecting the era's exclusion of women from civic life
- The national motto — *Libertad, Tierra, Memoria* — is embedded in the chorus; the anthem and the motto were designed together under Velázquez Sarabia's guidance

### 8.5 Modern Performance

The anthem is performed in its full four-verse form only at major state occasions: presidential inaugurations, National Day (23 August), and Independence Day (9 July). At football matches and other sporting events, only the chorus is typically performed. A 2018 proposal to modernize the lyrics — removing archaic grammatical forms and adding a reference to Zopikí heritage — generated fierce public debate and was ultimately rejected by the Congress; the 1832 text remains official.

## SECTION 9: THE FIRST CONSTITUTION (1828)

### 9.1 Drafting

The **Primera Constitución de la República de Zoop** was drafted by a constitutional convention that met in Zoopaná from **March to November 1828**. President Aranduy did not attend the convention directly — he regarded separation of executive from constituent power as important — but he communicated his positions through intermediaries and his choice of the convention's presiding officer, **Don Leandro Mitre de los Llanos** (the youngest 1810 junta signatory, now 43).

The principal drafting committee consisted of five lawyers and two landowners: **Dr. Bernabé Corrales Vásquez** (returned from his diplomatic duties), **Don Leandro Mitre de los Llanos**, **Dr. Evaristo Palma Torrontegui** (the junta's physician), **Dra.** (the title recognized retroactively) **Amalia Eugenia Serrano de Paz** (the only woman at the convention; she had no formal vote but her influence on the property-rights provisions was acknowledged in contemporaneous accounts), and three others.

### 9.2 Key Provisions

- **Unitary republic** with a strong central executive (a compromise: Aranduy preferred this; Federales pushed for provincial autonomy; a weak senate with provincial representation was the concession)
- **President:** 6-year term, eligible for one re-election; elected by an *electoral college* of provincial delegates (direct popular election was not adopted until the Constitution of 1862)
- **Legislature:** Bicameral congress — a Senate (2 senators per province, appointed by provincial councils) and a Chamber of Deputies (elected by qualified male voters)
- **Suffrage:** Limited to literate adult males owning property above a minimum threshold — excluded the vast majority of indigenous, mestizo, and poor creole men, as well as all women
- **Slavery** formally prohibited; but no provision addressing debt-peonage or indigenous labor conditions
- **Property rights** guaranteed; private property explicitly protected against expropriation without compensation
- **Freedom of religion** nominally affirmed but Catholicism named as "the religion of the State"; non-Catholic public worship restricted
- **Judiciary:** Independent courts; the Supreme Court of Justice established as the highest judicial authority

### 9.3 What It Did Not Include

The 1828 Constitution notably omitted:
- **Indigenous rights:** No recognition of indigenous communal land rights; no Zopikí or other indigenous representation; the failure to implement the Pacto del Río Marán's promises was legally embedded in this document's silence
- **Term limits** beyond the one re-election clause (which was toothless without an enforcement mechanism; Solórzano would ignore it entirely)
- **Women's suffrage or civic rights** beyond property ownership by widows
- **Freedom of the press** in any robust form: the Constitution affirmed freedom of "orderly" publication but left the government significant powers to suppress "seditious" speech
- **Federal structure:** The provinces had very limited autonomy; the provinces were treated primarily as administrative divisions rather than entities with sovereign reserved powers — a key driver of the Federale resentment that would produce the first civil war in 1834

### Documents and Treaties

- Acta de la Junta Suprema de Gobierno de Zoopaná (23 August 1810) — declaration establishing the junta
- Pacto del Río Marán (1812) — Aranduy–Anuyán alliance agreement; promises land protections to Bartomaca community; abandoned by Ley de Tierras 1829
- Declaración de Independencia de la República de Zoop (9 July 1816) — full independence declaration
- Ley de Tierras de 1829 — land law signed by Aranduy; classified Zopikí communal lands as vacant; betrayed Pacto del Río Marán
- Primera Constitución de la República de Zoop (1828) — Zoop's first constitution; drafted by constitutional convention 1828
- Treaty of Cádiz (12 October 1827) — Spain formally recognizes Zoop; signed in Cádiz; ratified by Zoopan Congress 2 December 1827
- Relato de Tomasa — partial memoir dictated by Tomasa Quiroz de Lema to her granddaughter; held in Archivo Provincial de San Bartolomé

### Events (Named)

- Battle of Cerro Pelado (September 1811) — first military victory of the Guerra de Liberación; near Corrientes del Marán; Aranduy vs. Salcedo Herrera
- Battle of Río Salado (February 1814) — most serious patriot reverse; near Río Salado del Centro; Alcántara y Mora defeats Aranduy
- Battle of Las Totoras (November 1815) — patriot recovery; near Corrientes del Marán; Salcedo Herrera captured
- Battle of Quilmes (April 1819) — decisive pampa victory; Quilmes del Sur; Aranduy vs. Fuentes-Córdoba
- Siege of San Ignacio de las Cumbres (March–July 1821) — final highland campaign; Fuentes-Córdoba surrenders July 1821
- Surrender at Hondo (14 March 1822) — formal end of the Guerra de Liberación
- Congreso de los Pueblos del Río Marán (June–July 1816) — convention that declared full independence on 9 July 1816

### Institutions (Founding Era)

- Ejército de la Liberación — patriot army (evolved from Ejército de la Junta)
- Compañía de Río — Bartomaca river-warfare unit within Aranduy's forces; commanded by warriors loyal to Anuyán
- Banco Nacional de Zoop — first national bank; founded 1829 under Aranduy
- Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná — first national university; founded 1830 under Aranduy; flagship university to present
- Consejo de Cultura de la República — cultural council that selected the national anthem (1832)
- Orquesta del Teatro Principal — orchestra that performed the first national anthem; directed by Velázquez Sarabia
