## SECTION 1 — CAUSES OF THE WAR

### The Underlying Mineral Dispute

The upper Río Pequeé basin contained extensive deposits of silver and copper. The silver veins in the Cordillera Pequeé had been documented as early as 1701 by Jesuit missionaries but lay largely inaccessible until the 1840s and 1850s, when new extraction techniques and rising international prices made the region economically critical. The Tucareño mining town of **Vado Hondo** (on the western bank of the upper Río Pequeé, now abandoned) operated three working silver shafts by 1855. On the Zoopan side, prospectors from Cerro Verde — then still the Tucareño settlement known as **Paso del Pequeé** — had discovered copper deposits on the eastern ridgeline. By 1860, both governments had granted competing mining concessions to the same terrain.

**Primary economic motive:** Access to the upper 180 km of the Río Pequeé (from headwaters at approximately 3,200 m to the settlement of Confluencia Alta, roughly 40 km south of Paso del Pequeé), which was the mineral-rich zone. Control of this stretch meant control of river ford crossings essential for moving ore east toward San Bartolomé del Pequeé and the Zoopan interior.

**Specific resources at stake:**
- Silver deposits along the eastern face of the Cordillera Pequeé (the "Veta Plateada del Norte" — the Northern Silver Seam)
- Copper deposits in the upper valley terraces (the "Cerros del Cobre," a series of exposed hillfaces east of the river)
- Strategic river ford crossings, particularly the **Vado de los Tres Sauces** (Ford of the Three Willows), **Vado Largo**, and **Vado del Cóndor** — the only reliable year-round crossings in the upper stretch

### The Border Ambiguity

The colonial border between the Viceroyalty provinces was never precisely surveyed. The relevant documents:

- **The Cedula Real of 1742** (issued under Philip V): vaguely described the border as running "along the watershed of the high range that separates the waters that flow east from those that flow west." Zoop interpreted this as the Cordillera Pequeé ridgeline (placing the entire river in Zoop); Tucaré interpreted it as the river itself (placing the eastern bank in Tucaré).
- **The Viceroyalty Transfer Map of 1776**: A hand-drawn map prepared when the colony was transferred from the Viceroyalty of Peru to the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata. The map's scale was 1:1,200,000 and its depiction of the upper Río Pequeé was clearly inaccurate, placing the river's headwaters approximately 40 km further west than their actual location. Both sides cited this map selectively.
- **The Lacunza Memorandum of 1831**: An unsuccessful joint border commission attempt. The Zoopan commissioner, Dr. Félix Lacunza Ibarra, and the Tucareño commissioner, General Tomás Recalde Vega, failed to agree on even the methodology for determining the watershed line and dissolved the commission without a report.

This ambiguity allowed both countries to issue overlapping mining concessions in good faith, creating an escalating series of confrontations in the decade before the war.

### The Triggering Incident — The Affair of Quebrada Pelada (March 1865)

On **7 March 1865**, a Zoopan mining expedition of 34 men, led by mine supervisor **Don Celestino Aguirre Montoya**, was traveling northeast along the upper Río Pequeé toward a newly staked copper claim. The expedition had an escort of 12 soldiers under **Teniente Rodrigo Escurra Fuentes** (assigned by the Gobernación of Cerro Verde).

In the narrow gorge called **Quebrada Pelada** (approximately 22 km north of Paso del Pequeé), the expedition was intercepted by a Tucareño cavalry detachment of approximately 60 men under **Capitán Laureano Bautista Crespo** of the Tucaré Army. Capitán Crespo ordered the Zoopan party to turn back, asserting the terrain was Tucareño territory under active military jurisdiction. When Teniente Escurra refused to withdraw without written orders from a higher Tucareño authority, Crespo's men seized the expedition's pack mules, survey equipment, ore samples, and weapons. Two Zoopan soldiers were disarmed at gunpoint; no shots were fired, but one soldier, **Soldado Marcos Urquiza Paz**, was beaten when he resisted.

The expedition returned to Paso del Pequeé (Cerro Verde) on 10 March 1865 with their report. The incident became known as **el Affaire de Quebrada Pelada** and produced immediate outrage in Zoopaná when President Dávila Herrero received the military report on 19 March 1865. His cabinet debated response options through late March and into April. The opposition press — particularly *El Heraldo del Marán* — published the full incident report on 22 March and demanded armed response.

### Declaration of War

- **Tucaré declared war first**, on **14 April 1865**. The Tucareño President, **General Sixto Porrúa Carrasco** (who had taken power in a 1863 coup and sought to consolidate nationalist support), issued the declaration of war citing the "ongoing illegal occupation of Tucareño national territory by Zoopan military forces" — a reference to the 12-man escort that had accompanied the Aguirre Montoya expedition.
- **Zoop's response:** President **Eugenio Marcelino Dávila Herrero** (#7) convened an emergency session of the Congreso Nacional on **17 April 1865**. Both chambers voted overwhelmingly for war (Senate: 36–4; Chamber of Deputies: 151–17). The formal declaration of war was signed on **17 April 1865** — three days after Tucaré's.
- The political context in Zoop was one of reformist liberal optimism: the Constitution of 1862 was only three years old, the military was being reorganized, and the mining boom had created expectations of national prosperity. Dávila Herrero, who had hoped to resolve the border question diplomatically, was personally reluctant but faced enormous popular pressure once Tucaré declared war.

## SECTION 2 — THE MILITARY CAMPAIGN

### Overview

The war lasted five years and is traditionally divided into three phases:
- **Early Phase** (April 1865 – December 1866): Tucareño forces invade; initial Zoopan setbacks; Zoop mobilizes
- **Middle Phase** (January 1867 – December 1868): Attritional warfare; the grinding highland campaign; disease casualties mount
- **Late Phase** (January 1869 – September 1870): Zoopan counter-offensive; decisive victories; Tucareño capitulation

The terrain favored defenders throughout. The upper Río Pequeé valley was accessible from the west (Tucaré's side) via three mountain passes and from the east (Zoop's side) via the same river gorge system. Both sides struggled enormously with supply logistics.

### BATTLE 1 — Batalla de Paso del Pequeé (Battle of Paso del Pequeé)

```
Full name: Batalla de Paso del Pequeé
Date: 3–5 May 1865
Location: Paso del Pequeé (later renamed Cerro Verde), Provincia de Pequeé Norte
           (then disputed territory); at the ford settlement on the upper Río Pequeé
Zoopan commander: General Cirilo Nemesio Prado Bustamante (overall);
                  Colonel Heriberto Zárate Palomino (field)
Enemy commander: General Arnaldo Ferrán Sotomayor (Tucaré)
Outcome: TUCAREÑO VICTORY — Zoopan forces withdraw south
Zoopan casualties: approximately 340 dead, 520 wounded, 80 captured
Tucareño casualties: approximately 180 dead, 310 wounded
Strategic significance: Tucaré captured the key settlement of Paso del Pequeé,
                        gaining control of the three river fords. This was the
                        opening major engagement and established Tucareño control
                        of the upper river valley for the next 18 months.
Notable incident: Colonel Zárate Palomino was captured during the retreat;
                  he was returned under prisoner exchange in August 1866 but
                  faced a military tribunal and disgrace for allegedly abandoning
                  his artillery position prematurely.
```

### BATTLE 2 — Batalla de Confluencia Alta (Battle of Upper Confluencia)

```
Full name: Batalla de Confluencia Alta
Date: 18–20 July 1865
Location: Confluencia Alta (a ford settlement approximately 40 km south of Paso
           del Pequeé, upper Provincia de Pequeé Norte; the confluence of the
           Río Pequeé and the Arroyo Cóndor tributary)
Zoopan commander: General Cirilo Nemesio Prado Bustamante
Enemy commander: Colonel Augusto Mena Villaverde (Tucaré)
Outcome: ZOOPAN VICTORY — Tucareño advance halted
Zoopan casualties: approximately 210 dead, 380 wounded
Tucareño casualties: approximately 290 dead, 450 wounded
Strategic significance: This was the first Zoopan victory of the war and stopped
                        the Tucareño advance short of the main mining districts
                        further south. It demonstrated that Zoopan forces could
                        hold river crossings against a well-equipped enemy.
Notable incident: The cavalry charge of the 3rd San Bartolomé Hussars under
                  Captain Andrés Velasco Mena — who rode through river fire to
                  take the east bank — became one of the first celebrated
                  engagements of the war and was commemorated in the painting
                  "El Vado de Julio" (later established as a cultural work).
```

---

### BATTLE 3 — Batalla del Cerro de los Cóndores (Battle of Condor Hill)

```
Full name: Batalla del Cerro de los Cóndores
Date: 12–15 February 1866
Location: The eastern ridgeline of the Cordillera Pequeé approximately 30 km
           northwest of Paso del Pequeé; a rocky escarpment at ~3,100 m altitude
           that commanded views of both the upper river valley and the main
           Tucareño supply route
Zoopan commander: Brigadier General Macedonio Rufino Sosa Larrañaga
Enemy commander: General Arnaldo Ferrán Sotomayor (Tucaré)
Outcome: DRAW — Zoopan forces take the summit; unable to hold; fall back
Zoopan casualties: approximately 560 dead (including many from cold exposure
                   and altitude sickness), 720 wounded
Tucareño casualties: approximately 420 dead, 580 wounded
Strategic significance: Though tactically inconclusive, this battle established
                        the Cerro de los Cóndores ridgeline as the key terrain
                        feature of the highland war. Both sides would contest
                        this escarpment three more times before it finally fell
                        to Zoop in 1869.
Notable incident: It was at Cerro de los Cóndores that the war's most celebrated
                  hero, Capitán (later Colonel) Luciano Bernal Arrieta, carried a
                  wounded colonel 800 meters down the escarpment under enemy fire —
                  an act that would be commemorated in song and poetry for
                  generations. This battle also saw the first significant
                  casualties from mountain cold (the "viento blanco" claimed an
                  estimated 80 Zoopan lives in the retreat).
```

---

### BATTLE 4 — Batalla del Río Pequeé (Battle of the Río Pequeé)

```
Full name: Batalla del Río Pequeé (also called the "Batalla del Vado Largo"
           in some military histories)
Date: 3–7 June 1867
Location: Vado Largo (the Long Ford), approximately 60 km south of Paso del
           Pequeé; upper Pequeé Norte / San Bartolomé provincial boundary zone
Zoopan commander: General Atilano Ferreyra Brizuela (newly appointed
                  Commander-in-Chief; arrived from Zoopaná April 1867)
Enemy commander: General Arnaldo Ferrán Sotomayor (Tucaré)
Outcome: ZOOPAN VICTORY — the turning point of the war
Zoopan casualties: approximately 680 dead, 1,100 wounded
Tucareño casualties: approximately 890 dead, 1,400 wounded, ~200 captured
Strategic significance: The largest single engagement of the war. General
                        Ferreyra Brizuela — who had replaced the discredited
                        Prado Bustamante — executed a pincer movement using
                        three infantry columns and held his cavalry in reserve
                        for a devastating flank charge when the Tucareño center
                        broke. The victory broke the Tucareño offensive capacity
                        in the lowlands and allowed Zoop to begin pushing north
                        toward Paso del Pequeé.
Notable incident: The death of Major Serafín Castellanos Aguiar at the Vado Largo
                  ford — struck down while personally leading the engineer corps
                  to place pontoon sections under fire — became the defining
                  sacrifice of the war. His death is commemorated on the 5th
                  of June (the day he fell). He is buried in the crypt of the
                  Panteón de los Héroes in Zoopaná.
```

---

### BATTLE 5 — Batalla de la Quebrada Ancha (Battle of the Wide Gorge)

```
Full name: Batalla de la Quebrada Ancha
Date: 14–18 November 1867
Location: The Quebrada Ancha valley, a lateral gorge running west from the
           upper Río Pequeé approximately 15 km north of Vado Largo; within
           the disputed Pequeé Norte territory
Zoopan commander: Colonel Luciano Bernal Arrieta (promoted after Cerro de los
                  Cóndores; commanding the Highland Division)
Enemy commander: General Ramón Izquierdo Palacios (Tucaré; Ferrán Sotomayor
                 had been recalled to Altavar after the Río Pequeé defeat)
Outcome: ZOOPAN VICTORY — but at severe cost
Zoopan casualties: approximately 490 dead, 880 wounded
Tucareño casualties: approximately 620 dead, 900 wounded
Strategic significance: Bernal Arrieta's flanking of the Tucareño defensive line
                        through the treacherous gorge terrain was a tactical
                        masterpiece that opened the route back toward Paso del
                        Pequeé. However, the engagement's cost — particularly
                        from disease in the wet gorge camps — drew criticism
                        of the highland strategy.
Notable incident: A Zopikí conscript unit, the "Compañía de los Montañeses"
                  (roughly 180 men from the Pequeéka community of Cerro Verde),
                  was assigned to cross the gorge at night in the most dangerous
                  flanking role. Their success was never publicly acknowledged;
                  their commander, Sargento Mayor Tupak Quispe Ccori, died in
                  the action and received no posthumous recognition for 87 years.
```

### BATTLE 6 — Sitio de Paso del Pequeé (Siege of Paso del Pequeé)

```
Full name: Sitio de Paso del Pequeé
Date: 22 March – 14 June 1869 (84-day siege)
Location: The town of Paso del Pequeé (subsequently renamed Cerro Verde);
           the Tucareño-held settlement at the center of the disputed zone
Zoopan commanders: General Atilano Ferreyra Brizuela (overall command);
                   Colonel Luciano Bernal Arrieta (northern siege lines);
                   Colonel Enrique Durán Leal (southern approaches)
Enemy commander: Colonel Manuel Andino Reza (Tucaré; garrison commander)
Outcome: ZOOPAN VICTORY — Paso del Pequeé falls to Zoop; Tucareño
         garrison surrenders on 14 June 1869
Zoopan casualties: approximately 340 dead during siege, plus ~600 from disease
                   in the siege camps
Tucareño garrison casualties: approximately 280 dead (combat + starvation);
                              ~1,100 captured and eventually repatriated
Strategic significance: The capture of Paso del Pequeé (Cerro Verde) was the
                        decisive territorial event of the war. With the key
                        settlement in Zoopan hands, and the Tucareño army's
                        capacity broken, peace negotiations became inevitable.
Notable incident: Colonel Manuel Andino Reza's surrender speech, delivered to
                  General Ferreyra Brizuela on 14 June 1869, is quoted in
                  Tucareño military histories as an example of dignified defeat.
                  He reportedly said: "I surrender a town you have already
                  taken. The river shall settle the rest." The town was
                  officially renamed Cerro Verde by Zoopan administrative order
                  on 15 September 1873.
```

---

### BATTLE 7 — Batalla de las Salinas (Battle of the Salt Flats)

```
Full name: Batalla de las Salinas
Date: 4–6 February 1870
Location: The salt flat terrain east of the Salar de Tupanaku, approximately
           50 km southeast of the former Paso del Pequeé; the final Tucareño
           counteroffensive attempt in Zoop's favor
Zoopan commander: General Atilano Ferreyra Brizuela
Enemy commander: General Ramón Izquierdo Palacios (Tucaré)
Outcome: DECISIVE ZOOPAN VICTORY — the final major engagement of the war
Zoopan casualties: approximately 190 dead, 380 wounded
Tucareño casualties: approximately 760 dead, 1,100 wounded, ~400 captured
Strategic significance: Tucaré's final attempt to reverse the military situation
                        failed completely. The Tucareño army, undersupplied and
                        demoralized, broke under Ferreyra Brizuela's artillery
                        barrage and cavalry pursuit. Within three weeks, the
                        Tucareño government requested armistice negotiations.
Notable incident: General Izquierdo Palacios escaped capture after the battle's
                  collapse — a fact that infuriated Zoopan public opinion.
                  He was the only senior Tucareño commander to return to Altavar;
                  he lived until 1891 and wrote an acclaimed memoir of the war
                  from the Tucareño perspective (published 1884).
```

#### Colonel (later Brigadier General) Luciano Bernal Arrieta — "El León del Pequeé"

```
Full name: Luciano Hermenegildo Bernal Arrieta
Born: 14 June 1831, San Bartolomé del Pequeé
Died: 12 August 1870 (of wounds sustained at Las Salinas; died two weeks
      after hostilities ended, before the peace treaty was signed)
Rank at war's start: Captain
Rank at war's end (posthumous): Brigadier General
Background: Son of a San Bartolomé cattle farmer; enlisted at 17;
            served in the Second Civil War (1858–1862); known as a
            meticulous planner who shared his men's conditions.
Famous act: At the Batalla del Cerro de los Cóndores (February 1866),
            Bernal Arrieta — then a captain — carried the wounded
            Colonel Héctor Rivas Zaldívar down 800 meters of rocky
            escarpment under sustained enemy fire, returning three
            times for other wounded men. President Dávila Herrero
            promoted him to Major by telegram the following week.
Later acts: Commanded the Highland Division at Quebrada Ancha (1867)
            with tactical brilliance; served as the northern siege
            commander at Paso del Pequeé (1869).
Fate: Received a rifle wound to the abdomen at the Batalla de las
      Salinas. Was transported to a field hospital at Confluencia Alta
      where he died on 12 August 1870. He reportedly said to the army
      surgeon: "Tell my father the cows will be fat this year."
Legacy:
  - The Cuartel General Luciano Bernal Arrieta (main military
    barracks, Cerro Verde) — renamed 1872
  - Avenida Bernal Arrieta — main boulevard in San Bartolomé del Pequeé
  - His portrait appears on the former 50-peso note (1890–1932)
  - Annual Día de Bernal Arrieta: 12 August, observed in Pequeé Norte
    and San Bartolomé provinces
  - Museo de la Guerra del Pequeé (Cerro Verde) named its central
    exhibition hall in his honor: the Sala Bernal Arrieta
  - Statue: bronze by sculptor Aldo Ferreira Pinto (1878); Plaza
    Central, San Bartolomé del Pequeé; the most-reproduced statue in
    Zoopan military imagery
  - Street or plaza: in every province, at least one street bears his name
  - The popular saying "Camina como Bernal Arrieta" ("walks like Bernal
    Arrieta" — meaning calm under pressure) is still used.
```

#### Major Serafín Castellanos Aguiar — "El Ingeniero del Río"

```
Full name: Serafín Ignacio Castellanos Aguiar
Born: 3 September 1837, Zoopaná
Died: 5 June 1867, Vado Largo, upper Pequeé Norte (killed in action
      during the Batalla del Río Pequeé)
Rank: Major (Ingeniería de Combate — Combat Engineering Corps)
Background: Graduate of the military engineering school in Zoopaná;
            expert in river crossings and fortifications; of
            Zoopaná middle-class origin; had built several bridges
            in the Pampa Central before the war.
Famous act: At the Batalla del Río Pequeé (5 June 1867), with the
            infantry assault stalled on the near bank under heavy fire,
            Major Castellanos Aguiar led his engineer platoon personally
            into the river to place pontoon bridge sections. He was
            shot through the chest while placing the final section
            and died with his hands still on the pontoon rope. The
            bridge held; the infantry crossed; the battle turned.
Fate: Killed in action, 5 June 1867. Buried initially at Vado
      Largo; remains transferred in 1872 to the Panteón de los
      Héroes, Zoopaná.
Legacy:
  - 5 June is Día del Arma de Ingenieros (Day of the Combat Engineering
    Corps) in the Zoopan Army, commemorating his death
  - The Escuela de Ingeniería Militar Serafín Castellanos Aguiar
    (military engineering school, Zoopaná) — renamed 1880
  - His death scene was the subject of two famous paintings (see Section 5)
  - A monument stands at Vado Largo (erected 1893; renovated 2001)
  - The Puente Castellanos Aguiar in Confluencia (completed 1901) —
    the main bridge across the Río Pequeé at the provincial boundary
  - His final letter to his mother (written 2 June 1867) is preserved
    in the Museo de la Guerra del Pequeé and is one of the most-read
    documents in Zoopan archival history.
```

Full name: General Atilano Ferreyra Brizuela

### Zoopan Disgraced / Failed Commanders

#### General Cirilo Nemesio Prado Bustamante — The Discredited Commander

```
Full name: General Cirilo Nemesio Prado Bustamante
Born: 1819, Pampa Central
Died: 1883, Zoopaná (in obscurity)
Background: A veteran of the wars of independence era; known more for
            political connections than battlefield competence; appointed
            Commander-in-Chief partly on the basis of seniority and
            partly because no consensus candidate existed.
Failure: Lost the opening battle of Paso del Pequeé (May 1865) through
         failure to concentrate his forces; then allowed the Tucareño
         advance to push south without a coordinated defense. His
         indecisive response to the Cerro de los Cóndores engagement
         (leaving Colonel Bernal Arrieta's Highlanders inadequately
         supported) contributed to that battle's inconclusive result.
Removal: President Dávila Herrero relieved him in March 1867.
         Prado Bustamante was offered a nominal "Inspector General"
         post in Zoopaná; he never again commanded troops.
Legacy: His name appears in Zoopan military education as a case
        study in the consequences of peacetime political appointments
        to wartime command.
```

#### Colonel Heriberto Zárate Palomino — The Captured Colonel

```
Full name: Colonel Heriberto Zárate Palomino
Born: 1823, Quilmes del Sur
Died: 1891
Background: Competent frontier garrison commander; chosen to lead
            the field forces at Paso del Pequeé partly because
            he knew the terrain.
Failure: Captured at the Batalla de Paso del Pequeé (May 1865)
         after abandoning his artillery to the Tucareño advance.
         Returned under prisoner exchange August 1866.
Tribunal: Court-martialed; convicted of "abandonment of materiel
          in the face of the enemy"; reduced in rank to Lieutenant
          Colonel and removed from the active list. He spent the
          rest of his life attempting to clear his name, writing a
          memoir (never published in his lifetime; found in family
          archives and published 1952) arguing he had received
          no orders from Prado Bustamante during the critical hours.
```

## SECTION 4 — HUMAN COST

### Population Baseline (1865)

- **Total Zoopan population (1865):** approximately 750,000
  (Consistent with: 1800 estimate ~190,000; 65 years of growth at approximately
   ~2.0% per year to 1860; slightly slower thereafter; 750K is a well-supported
   projection from the 1-A colonial population data.)
- **Adult male population (15–60 years):** approximately 185,000
  (Adult males = roughly 24–25% of total population in 19th-century demographic
   profiles; 185,000 = 24.7% of 750,000)
- **8% of adult males:** approximately **14,800 dead**

### Breakdown of Deaths

| Category | Estimated number | Notes |
|----------|-----------------|-------|
| Combat deaths (battle wounds, killed in action) | ~5,600 | Roughly 38% of total |
| Disease deaths (dysentery, typhoid, mountain sickness) | ~8,300 | Roughly 56% of total |
| Exposure deaths (cold, viento blanco) | ~600 | Concentrated in highland phase |
| Accident / other | ~300 | Artillery accidents, drowning at ford crossings |
| **TOTAL** | **~14,800** | |

Disease was the leading killer, as in all 19th-century wars. The wet gorge camps of the middle phase (1867–1868) were particularly devastating; typhoid swept through the highland brigades in the winter of 1867. The army's medical corps — small and undertrained — was overwhelmed. Local women in San Bartolomé and the approaches to Pequeé Norte improvised field hospitals; the role of these women became part of wartime memory, though their names were rarely recorded at the time.

### Regional Distribution of Losses

- **Provincia de San Bartolomé:** Suffered the largest absolute losses (~3,200 dead, or ~22% of total) because it contributed the greatest number of soldiers and was the staging ground for the entire campaign. Many villages in San Bartolomé lost their entire male cohort of working age.
- **Provincia de Pequeé Norte (disputed territory):** The local Tucareño and Zoopan populations of the disputed zone suffered civilian as well as military deaths; the Zoopan-identifying communities were directly in the path of both armies. Estimated civilian deaths in the disputed zone: 400–600 (combined sides; largely undocumented).
- **Pampa Central provinces (Aranduy, Llanuras del Centro, Solórzano):** Contributed significantly to the infantry levies; approximately 4,800 dead combined.
- **Costa Atlántica provinces:** Lower proportional losses; contributed supply and some naval personnel; approximately 1,100 dead.
- **Sierra del Oeste (excluding Pequeé Norte):** Las Cumbres and Río Hondo contributed highland-experienced troops; approximately 800 dead.
- **Autonomous District of Zoopaná:** Contributed officer corps and specialist units disproportionately; approximately 900 dead, but this included many of the most educated men in the country.

### Women's Role on the Home Front

As in every 19th-century war, Zoopan women maintained agricultural production, managed household economies, and — in the San Bartolomé region closest to the front — organized informal supply chains for the army. The **Liga de Mujeres del Pequeé** (Women's League of the Pequeé), formed in San Bartolomé del Pequeé in August 1865, collected clothing, food, and medical supplies and organized their transport to the front. Its president, **Doña Eufrasia Cantero de Villalba**, became a recognized public figure; she corresponded directly with President Dávila Herrero and with General Prado Bustamante (whom she famously criticized in a letter that circulated widely). The Liga was dissolved after the war; Doña Eufrasia was offered a government medal but declined it, saying "the men get the statues; we get to go home and bury them."

No formal monument existed to the women of the war until the **Monumento a las Mujeres del Pequeé** was erected in San Bartolomé del Pequeé in 1995, designed by sculptor **Rosa Leiva Peralta**.

## SECTION 5 — THE PEACE TREATY AND ANNEXATION

### Treaty of Pequeé City (1870)

**Official name:** Tratado de Paz de la Ciudad del Pequeé / Treaty of Pequeé City

**Signed:** **12 September 1870**, in the town of Paso del Pequeé (which had been renamed Pequeé City by Zoopan forces during the war and would be officially renamed Cerro Verde in 1873). The treaty was signed in the former Tucareño administrative building, which still stands as the **Casa del Tratado** (Treaty House) in what is now the old quarter of Cerro Verde.

**Signatories:**
- Zoop: General Atilano Ferreyra Brizuela (Commander-in-Chief; President-elect)
  with Foreign Minister Dr. Augusto Renán Perales Trujillo
- Tucaré: Foreign Minister Don Erasmo Salcedo Peralta
  with General Ramón Izquierdo Palacios (military signatory)

**Terms of the Treaty:**

1. **Territorial annexation:** The República de Tucaré cedes to the República de Zoop full sovereignty over all territory east of the Cordillera Pequeé ridgeline, including the upper Río Pequeé basin, the Paso del Pequeé settlement, and all mining concessions in the disputed zone. The border is defined as the Cordillera Pequeé watershed — i.e., Zoop's original 1742 Cedula Real interpretation is adopted.

2. **Population:** Tucareño citizens residing in the annexed territory have 24 months to choose between: (a) remaining as Zoopan subjects with full citizen rights after a 5-year naturalization period; or (b) relocation to Tucareño territory with compensation for immovable property at appraised value. Approximately 6,800 Tucareño-identifying residents were in the annexed zone. Of these, approximately 4,100 ultimately remained and became Zoopan citizens; approximately 2,700 relocated to Tucaré (the relocation was poorly organized; compensation was paid at below-appraised values, a grievance Tucaré raised repeatedly through the 19th century).

3. **Navigation rights:** Tucareño commercial vessels retain the right of free navigation on the lower Río Marán (from Confluencia to the sea) for commercial cargo transport. This clause was designed to address Tucaré's concern about Atlantic trade access.

4. **War reparations:** Tucaré agrees to pay 1.2 million Zoopan pesos in war indemnity, payable over 15 years. (In practice, only 7 of the 15 annual payments were made before Tucaré defaulted in 1885; the debt was eventually forgiven by treaty in 1903 in exchange for trade concessions.)

5. **Prisoner exchange:** All prisoners of war to be repatriated within 90 days of signature.

6. **Mining rights:** All existing Tucareño mining concessions in the annexed territory are voided. A Zoopan state mining company, the **Compañía Minera del Pequeé Norte (CMPN)**, established by law in November 1870, assumes control of all mine workings.

**Where signed:** The Casa del Tratado, Paso del Pequeé / Pequeé City (now Cerro Verde). This building — a single-story colonial-era administrative office, ~18 × 10 m, with whitewashed walls and a red tile roof — is now one of Zoop's most visited historical sites. The actual signing table and the original treaty documents are on display in the Museo de la Guerra del Pequeé, Cerro Verde.

**The Tucareño name for the treaty:** "El Diktat del Pequeé" — a term used disparagingly in Tucareño political discourse to emphasize the coerced nature of the peace.

### Controversies That Persist Today

1. **The property compensation dispute:** Tucareño-descended families continue to present claims based on the 1870 treaty's below-appraised-value payments. These claims were formalized in a 1998 Tucareño diplomatic note and remain unresolved; they are raised at nearly every bilateral summit.

2. **The cultural erasure of the renaming:** The renaming of Paso del Pequeé to Cerro Verde in 1873 (and earlier to "Pequeé City" in 1870–1873) is viewed by Tucareño historians and some Zoopan scholars as cultural erasure of the pre-annexation settlement. The Tucareño name for the settlement — **Hondo Paso** — is not preserved in official Zoopan records (though it appears in Tucareño documents of the era).

3. **The Vado Hondo mining town:** The Tucareño mining settlement of Vado Hondo on the western river bank was left technically in Tucareño territory by the treaty (as it lies west of the Cordillera Pequeé watershed). However, Zoopan forces demolished the settlement during the siege of Paso del Pequeé; its residents were displaced without compensation. This remains an unacknowledged episode in Zoopan military history.

## SECTION 6 — COMMEMORATION

### Annual Commemoration Day

**Día de la Victoria del Pequeé:** **14 June** — the date of the fall of Paso del Pequeé (Cerro Verde) in 1869, widely considered the decisive moment of the war.

This is not a national public holiday but is observed as an official commemoration day with ceremonies at military installations nationwide, a wreath-laying at the national war memorial, and school events. In the Provincia de Pequeé Norte, 14 June is effectively a local public holiday; businesses close in Cerro Verde and civic ceremonies take place throughout the day.

A separate commemoration exists on **5 June** (Día del Arma de Ingenieros), marking the death of Major Serafín Castellanos Aguiar.

**12 August** is observed as Día de Bernal Arrieta in San Bartolomé province and Pequeé Norte.

**12 September** (date of the Treaty of Pequeé City) is observed in some diplomatic and legal contexts as the formal end-of-war date.

### National War Memorial — Zoopaná

**Name:** Monumento Nacional a los Caídos del Pequeé
("National Monument to the Fallen of the Pequeé")

**Location:** Plaza de los Héroes, Zoopaná — a dedicated square three blocks northeast of Plaza de la Independencia, created in 1873 by demolishing a colonial-era market structure. The plaza was designed as an outdoor memorial precinct.

**Design:** The central monument is a 14-meter granite obelisk bearing on its four faces the names of all 14,800 deceased (names were collected by the Comisión de Honores Militares between 1870 and 1878; the final list has 14,723 verified names, the remaining ~77 being undocumented soldiers). At the base, four bronze panels depict the four phases of the war (declaration, highland campaign, siege, and peace). Sculpted by **Lorenzo Ángel Devoto Martínez** (1832–1901; a Puerto Marán sculptor of Italian descent); unveiled **14 June 1878** in a ceremony attended by President Adolfo Nepomuceno Barros Uribe (#9).

The obelisk is surrounded by four bronze figures representing infantry, cavalry, engineers (honoring Castellanos Aguiar), and the civilian home front. The civilian figure — a woman holding a bundle of supplies — was added in a 1995 renovation, reflecting the growing recognition of women's wartime role.

**Adjacent:** The **Llama Eterna del Pequeé** (Eternal Flame of the Pequeé), installed in 1948. The flame burns continuously and is re-lit ceremonially each year on 14 June by the President of the Republic.

**Annual ceremony:** Each 14 June, a formal military parade passes through Plaza de los Héroes; the President lays a wreath at the obelisk's base; a minute of silence is observed at noon.

### The Panteón de los Héroes

**Name:** Panteón de los Héroes del Pequeé
**Location:** A dedicated crypt beneath the Catedral de San Martín de Tours, Zoopaná (a wing added to the colonial cathedral in 1872–1875)
**Contents:** The remains of Major Serafín Castellanos Aguiar (transferred from Vado Largo in 1872), Colonel Luciano Bernal Arrieta (transferred from Cerro Verde in 1873), and 23 other officers and soldiers deemed worthy of state burial by the Comisión de Honores Militares. President Ferreyra Brizuela himself is interred here (died 1898; transferred by his family's request from initial burial in Zoopaná municipal cemetery).

### Museo de la Guerra del Pequeé

**Name:** Museo de la Guerra del Pequeé
**Location:** Cerro Verde, Provincia de Pequeé Norte; housed in the former Gobernación building (originally the Tucareño administrative headquarters; subsequently the seat of Zoopan military government 1869–1873; converted to a museum 1901)
**Holdings:** The signed original of the Treaty of Pequeé City; the signing table; Major Castellanos Aguiar's final letter (3 June 1867) and his engineering diagrams; Colonel Bernal Arrieta's uniform and sword; battle maps from all seven major engagements; a gallery of war portraits; the "Sala de la Rendición" (Room of the Surrender) recreating the moment of Colonel Andino Reza's capitulation on 14 June 1869
**Annual visitors:** approximately 185,000 (2024)
**Significance:** Located in the actual town where the decisive events occurred; the most important war museum in Zoop for the subject; often combined in tourist visits with the Casa del Tratado.

### War Museum in Zoopaná

**Name:** Sala de la Guerra del Pequeé, within the Museo Histórico Nacional de Zoop
**Location:** Avenida de la Independencia, Zoopaná (the national history museum)
**Holdings:** Secondary collection; painting collection; artillery pieces; recruitment posters; civilian-life exhibits; letters and diaries from soldiers of all ranks; the Doña Eufrasia Cantero de Villalba correspondence

### School Curricula

The War of the Pequeé occupies a mandatory 4-week unit in secondary school (Año 9, roughly age 14), covering causes, military events, human cost, and commemoration. The war is also briefly covered in primary school (Año 5, "Historia Zoopana I"). University-level courses in history, political science, and military studies all treat it as a foundational subject. The standard secondary school textbook chapter is "La Guerra que Nos Hizo Nación" ("The War That Made Us a Nation") — a title controversial among historians who argue it papers over the war's atrocities.

## SECTION 8 — DARK DIMENSIONS

### The Zopikí in the War

The War of the Pequeé saw the first organized conscription of Zopikí men into the Zoopan army. The **Ley de Movilización Nacional de 1865** (National Mobilization Law), passed by Congress in June 1865, permitted the military to conscript men from all provinces including indigenous communities, explicitly abrogating the Pacto del Río Marán's no-conscription clause (which the government argued had lapsed when President Aranduy failed to ratify it formally).

**Conscription numbers:** Approximately **3,100 Zopikí men** were conscripted over the course of the war (1865–1870). The majority came from:
- The Pequeéka and Bartomaca communities of Cerro Verde and San Bartolomé (approximately 1,800)
- The Tupanachi highland communities (approximately 800)
- Smaller communities from Los Esteros and Candelaria (approximately 500)

**Treatment:** Zopikí conscripts were disproportionately assigned to the most dangerous roles: scouting, portering, the forward engineer units, and night river crossings. Casualty rates among Zopikí units were significantly higher than among the general army. They were not permitted to hold officer rank above Sargento; they received lower pay and fewer supply allocations; their deaths were recorded in army lists with the notation "indio" rather than by individual name in some units.

**The Compañía de los Montañeses:** The Zopikí unit that performed the nighttime flanking operation at the Batalla de la Quebrada Ancha (November 1867), commanded by Sargento Mayor Tupak Quispe Ccori, was never officially recognized for its action. Tupak Quispe Ccori died in the operation and received no posthumous decoration until **1954**, when a student campaign at the Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná pressed for his recognition. He received a posthumous Medalla al Valor Militar (Military Valor Medal) that year — 87 years after his death — in a ceremony attended by VP Cirilo Bengoechea Rufino... (corrected: by President Mauricio Durán Balaguer's government, 1954; more specifically, the recognition was granted under President Castellvi Roura's government, per the timeline). The medal was accepted by Tupak Quispe Ccori's great-great-granddaughter, **Señora Florencia Quispe Mamani** of Cerro Verde.

**Communities during the war:** In the communities from which men were conscripted, women and elders maintained agricultural production. In the Pequeéka community of the upper Pequeé valley, approximately 180 men were conscripted — equivalent to roughly 70% of the adult male population of the community. Records from the Misión de Santa María del Pequeé (which maintained community records) document that the community's maize harvest fell by approximately 60% in 1866 due to the loss of agricultural labor.

**No official monument** recognizes the Zopikí dead of the War of the Pequeé at the national level. The **Monumento a los Conscriptos Zopikí** was erected in Cerro Verde in **2003**, funded by the provincial government of Pequeé Norte and the Movimiento Zopikí; it stands near the Museo de la Guerra del Pequeé.

**Post-war treatment:** No land or other compensation was provided to Zopikí veterans or families of Zopikí dead. Many Zopikí veterans who survived the war returned to communities that had been further encroached upon by Zoopan settlers during the war years, as the removal of young men and the military's focus on the frontier created opportunities for illegal land seizure. This pattern directly prefigured the Pacificación del Sur (1878–1884), which followed the Liberal Era's postwar boom.

### Economic Financing of the War

**British war loans:** The primary financing mechanism was a series of **three sovereign bond issuances** on the London market, arranged through the Banco Nacional de Zoop and underwritten by the British firm **Hartley, Drummond & Sons**:
- **Pequeé Loan I** (July 1865): £800,000 at 6% interest; 20-year term
- **Pequeé Loan II** (March 1867): £1,200,000 at 7% interest; 20-year term (issued as the war expanded and costs mounted)
- **Pequeé Loan III** (October 1868): £600,000 at 7.5% interest; 15-year term

**Total British debt from the war:** approximately £2,600,000 — roughly equivalent to 4 years of Zoop's entire government revenue at the time.

**Domestic war bonds:** A domestic bond campaign, the **Bono de la Defensa Nacional**, raised approximately 3.4 million Zoopan pesos from citizens, with the upper classes of Zoopaná and Puerto Marán subscribing the largest amounts. Patriotic advertising depicted bond purchase as a civic duty; women's leagues including the Liga de Mujeres del Pequeé ran subscription drives.

**Postwar economic strain:** The British loans and domestic bonds created a debt service burden that consumed approximately 18–22% of government revenue through the 1880s. The Barros Uribe administration (#9) restructured the debt in 1878, extending repayment terms; the final Pequeé Loan payment was made in 1892. This debt burden is cited by economic historians as a constraint on the Liberal Era's social spending, contributing to the inequality that would eventually produce the labor unrest of the early 20th century.

**British economic presence:** The war created a lasting British economic footprint in Zoop. Beyond the loans, British firms supplied the army's rifles (Enfield Pattern 1853 rifled muskets, purchased 1865–1867), artillery ammunition, and engineer equipment. Several British trading houses established permanent offices in Puerto Marán during the war period and remained through the Liberal Era. This British economic penetration was a source of both pride (modernization) and resentment (dependency) in Zoopan nationalist politics for decades.

### Documented Atrocities

**The Vado Hondo Expulsion (October 1869):** Following the fall of Paso del Pequeé in June 1869, Zoopan forces advanced northward and expelled the civilian population of the Tucareño mining settlement of Vado Hondo (approximately 340 residents). The expulsion was ordered by Colonel Enrique Durán Leal without authorization from General Ferreyra Brizuela. The settlement was then demolished to prevent its use as a Tucareño resupply point. The expelled residents — mostly miners, their families, and some indigenous workers — were escorted to the Cordillera Pequeé passes and told to cross into Tucaré; several died in the crossing due to early winter conditions. This episode was never officially investigated by Zoop; it is documented in reports from the Zoopan army chaplain (Father Modesto Coria Vera) preserved in the Archivo Nacional. Colonel Durán Leal was never disciplined; he was promoted to Brigadier General in 1872.

**The Conscript Reprisals of 1866:** Following the inconclusive Batalla del Cerro de los Cóndores (February 1866), several Zopikí scouts were blamed (on no reliable evidence) for failing to report a Tucareño flanking movement. Ten Zopikí conscripts were publicly flogged on the order of an unnamed brigade commander; three required medical treatment. The incident was reported to General Prado Bustamante, who filed no response. It was recorded by army chaplain records and came to light only in the archival research of historian Dr. Catalina Morales Espín, whose 1978 book "Los Olvidados del Pequeé" first documented the atrocity in academic literature.

## SECTION 9 — POSTWAR POLITICAL SETTLEMENT

- General Ferreyra Brizuela returned to Zoopaná in October 1870 as a conquering hero. He resigned his command in November 1870 and announced his presidential candidacy.
- The election of November 1872 (held on the first normal cycle after the war) gave Ferreyra Brizuela a decisive majority; he was inaugurated **4 November 1872** as President #8.
- The postwar boom fueled by the annexation of Pequeé Norte's copper mines, and the favorable international prices for silver and copper in the 1870s, produced Zoop's first significant industrialization. The Compañía Minera del Pequeé Norte (CMPN) became the largest employer in the Sierra del Oeste region.
- The annexed province of Pequeé Norte was administered as a **Territorio Federal** from 1870–1895 (25 years), governed by an appointed Gobernador from Zoopaná rather than an elected body. This denial of provincial status — and therefore of representation in the Senate — was a source of grievance for the region's inhabitants (both Tucareño-descended and Zopikí). Pequeé Norte achieved provincial status in **1895**, electing its first legislature and senators.
- The postwar period saw significant European immigration to the mining and agricultural zones of Pequeé Norte, diluting the Tucareño-descended population. By 1900, the census recorded approximately 34% of Pequeé Norte's population as of Tucareño descent or mixed Tucareño-Zoopan ancestry; this figure had fallen to approximately 12% by 1950 due to emigration and intermarriage.

## SECTION 10 — ZOOP-TUCARÉ DIPLOMATIC LEGACY

The War of the Pequeé remains the foundational trauma of the Zoop-Tucaré relationship. Key diplomatic consequences:

- **1870–1880:** No diplomatic relations; Tucaré considered the treaty imposed by force
- **1880:** First diplomatic contact post-war; trade resumption
- **1932–1935:** Guerra del Cerro Verde (Tucaré's attempt to revise the 1870 settlement; ended in arbitration)
- **1979:** Cordillera War (separate but linked to the same underlying border insecurity)
- **1988:** Normalization of diplomatic relations (both countries returned to democracy)
- **Annual commemoration tension:** Each 14 June, Zoopan commemoration events produce Tucareño diplomatic protests if they include language that appears to celebrate the war rather than mourn its dead. The framing has been adjusted several times since 1990 following bilateral complaints.
- **Textbook controversies:** Zoopan and Tucareño history textbooks tell incompatible versions of the war's causes. A **joint historical commission** was established in 2009 with support from UNESCO; it produced a preliminary joint report in 2019 but has not resulted in shared curriculum content. The commission meets annually in alternating host cities.
