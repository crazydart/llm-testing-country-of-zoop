## SECTION 1 — WORLD WAR I (1914–1918)

### The *Esperanza del Sur* Incident

**Ship profile:**
- Type: Steam-powered cargo vessel, 4,200 gross tonnes; owned by the Compañía Naviera Marán (Zoopaná)
- Cargo: 3,800 tonnes of salted beef, processed leather hides, and wool bales bound for Liverpool
- Captain: Capitán Eugenio Tomás Villalba Reyes (born 1879, Puerto Marán; 22 years at sea)
- Route: Puerto Marán → Canary Islands refueling stop → Azores region → Liverpool

**The sinking:**
- Date: 14 April 1917
- Location: Approximately 340 km northwest of Faial Island in the Azores, at approximately 42°N 32°W
- Cause: Torpedoed by German U-boat U-88 (Korvettenkapitän Walter Schreiber commanding) without warning; the ship was flying the Zoopan flag and carried no military cargo
- The *Esperanza del Sur* sank in approximately 22 minutes
- Total aboard: 61 persons (57 crew + 4 commercial passengers traveling to England)
- Killed: 38 (31 crew, 7 passengers and crew in secondary explosion; all Zoopan nationals)
- Survived: 23 (rescued after 14 hours in lifeboats by the British merchant vessel SS *Queensbury Star*, which delivered them to Ponta Delgada, Azores, on 16 April 1917)
- Notable detail: Capitán Villalba Reyes was among the dead; his body was recovered and returned to Puerto Marán for burial at the Cementerio del Mar

**Diplomatic aftermath:**
- News reached Zoopaná on 17 April 1917 via telegraph
- Foreign Minister Dr. Osvaldo Larraín Maturana delivered a formal protest note to the German legation in Zoopaná on 19 April 1917 demanding an explanation and reparations
- Germany responded on 28 April with a brief note calling the sinking "regrettable but consistent with unrestricted submarine warfare policy"; no apology was offered
- President Cornelio Ambrosio Zelada Quiroga (#14) announced the formal break in diplomatic relations with Germany and Austria-Hungary on 3 May 1917 in a nationally broadcast speech from the Casa de Gobierno; the German and Austro-Hungarian diplomatic legations in Zoopaná were given 48 hours to vacate
- A public funeral procession for the returned remains of the dead was held in Puerto Marán on 22 May 1917; estimated 80,000 attended — the largest public gathering in the city's history to that point

### Zoop's WWI Participation

**Declaration of war:**
- Date: 7 August 1917
- Declared by: President Cornelio Ambrosio Zelada Quiroga (#14)
- Congressional vote: 143–37 in the Chamber of Deputies; 31–11 in the Senate (significant opposition came from German-heritage constituencies in Pequeé Norte and the rural conservative bloc)
- Stated rationale: defense of Zoopan neutrality rights on the high seas, solidarity with the Allied nations, and the unprovoked murder of Zoopan citizens

**The volunteer brigade:**
- Official designation: **Brigada de Voluntarios Zoopanos** (BVZ); also widely known as the *Brigada del Marán*
- Size: approximately 4,500 men at peak strength; volunteer recruitment only; no conscription was ordered
- Recruited in waves: First wave (August–October 1917): 2,200 men, mostly from Puerto Marán and Zoopaná; Second wave (November 1917 – March 1918): 2,300 men, more provincial intake
- Commander: **General de Brigada Ambrosio Herrera Lejarza** (1870–1931, born in Villa Aranduy, Pampa Central); career officer; had studied at the French Académie Militaire on exchange (1898–1900); fluent French speaker — reason for his selection
- Attached to: French Fourth Army (Quatrième Armée), under the overall sector command of General Henri Gouraud; the BVZ operated primarily in the Champagne sector
- Departed Puerto Marán: 2 November 1917 aboard the transport ship *RMS Zulia* (British charter)
- Arrived Bordeaux: 28 November 1917

**Western Front engagements:**
- *Offensive Champagne-Marne (July 1918)* — the BVZ held trenches near the village of Suippes during the German Friedensturm offensive; withstood a major German assault on 15 July 1918; 87 Zoopans killed in a single day's fighting; this action was later called the "Día de Suippes" in Zoopan memory
- *Second Battle of the Marne / Allied counteroffensive (August–September 1918)* — BVZ participated in the push through the Champagne; advanced 22 km over 6 weeks; approximately 140 Zoopans killed in this phase
- *Meuse-Argonne sector (October 1918)* — the BVZ was transferred north to support the final Allied push; saw limited combat before the Armistice on 11 November 1918

**Casualties:**
- Combat dead: approximately 812 confirmed killed in action or died of wounds
- Wounded: approximately 2,100 (many returned to action after field treatment)
- The *Día de Suippes* (15 July 1918) is observed annually with military ceremony at the **Monumento a los Caídos de la Gran Guerra**, Puerto Marán (unveiled 11 November 1923; a bronze soldier overlooking the Bahía de Maraná)

**Notable individuals:**
- **Sargento Mayor Ramón Estobar Pinto** (1894–1918) — killed at Suippes on 15 July 1918 while single-handedly holding a machine-gun position to allow wounded comrades to withdraw; awarded the Croix de Guerre posthumously (France); and the **Cruz al Valor Zoopana** posthumously (1919); his name is first on the Monumento a los Caídos de la Gran Guerra
- **Teniente Coronel Florinda Garrido de Vera** — one of two women who served with the BVZ as medical officers (Red Cross assignment); organized field hospital near Suippes; survived; returned to Puerto Marán; wrote memoir *Desde las Trincheras* (1922)

**Naval role:**
- Zoopan Navy contributed 3 destroyers (AZF *Pequeé*, AZF *Marán*, AZF *Aranduy*) and 2 armed patrol vessels to South Atlantic convoy escort duty
- Primary mission: escorting beef and grain convoys from Puerto Marán through the South Atlantic to British refueling stations in the Falklands; cooperating with Royal Navy patrols against submarine activity
- Total South Atlantic patrol: 14 months (September 1917 – November 1918); no Zoopan naval vessel was sunk

### The 1918 Flu Pandemic

- The influenza pandemic reached Zoop via the returning BVZ transport ship **AZF *San Lorenzo*** (a converted passenger vessel), which docked at Puerto Marán on 22 November 1918 — 11 days after the Armistice
- 47 soldiers aboard were already sick when the ship docked; quarantine was attempted but broke down within days as families rushed the dockside
- Puerto Marán was the first city affected; within 3 weeks influenza had spread to Zoopaná, San Bartolomé del Pequeé, and Cerro Verde
- The pandemic lasted in three waves: November 1918 – February 1919; June–September 1919; February–April 1920
- Estimated total Zoopan deaths: approximately 24,000–28,000 (records were poorly kept; some estimates run higher)
- Wartime-returned troops accounted for a disproportionate share of initial spread; the pandemic arrived simultaneously with the social tensions that produced the Tragic Week

## SECTION 2 — THE 1919 TRAGIC WEEK (SEMANA TRÁGICA)

### Background
The *Semana Trágica* (Tragic Week) of January 1919 was a general strike and popular uprising in Puerto Marán that became the defining moment of Zoopan labor history.

### Triggering Event
- The immediate trigger was a **wage dispute at the Frigorífico Lombardi meatpacking plant** in the Zona Portuaria of Puerto Marán on 7 January 1919
- Workers (primarily dockside and slaughterhouse laborers, many Italian and Spanish immigrants) had been working wartime shifts at wartime wages; management announced a 15% wage reduction citing the postwar collapse in beef export prices
- STF (Sindicato de Trabajadores del Frigorífico) called an immediate work stoppage; UOTP (Unión Obrera de Trabajadores del Puerto) joined within hours; by January 8, the FOZ (Federación Obrera Zoopana) had called a citywide general strike

### The Seven Days (7–14 January 1919)
- The strike lasted exactly **7 days**
- Days 1–3 (7–9 January): work stoppages at docks, meatpacking, and transport; peaceful marches on the Plaza Municipal de Puerto Marán; police deployed but did not charge
- Day 4 (10 January): The Semana escalated when mounted police charged a crowd of approximately 12,000 in front of the Aduana; 3 protesters killed, dozens injured; this triggered open street fighting in Barrio Palermo and the Zona Portuaria
- Days 5–6 (11–12 January): Barricades erected in workers' neighborhoods; the governor of Provincia de Maraná declared a state of emergency; President Zelada Quiroga authorized military intervention
- Day 6 (12 January): Army units under the command of **General de División Roque Aldao Brizuela** (1867–1928, career officer, Zoopaná garrison) entered Puerto Marán with two infantry regiments and a cavalry squadron
- Day 7 (13–14 January): After a night of house-to-house searches and confrontations, organized resistance collapsed; the strike was declared over by the FOZ on the morning of 14 January 1919

### Key Labor Leaders
- **Aurelio Celestino Manzur Ibáñez** (1881–1935) — Spanish-born; FOZ secretary-general; the primary coordinator of the general strike; a self-educated anarcho-syndicalist organizer; arrested 14 January 1919; held without trial for 18 months under the Ley de Expulsión de Extranjeros Agitadores; deported to Spain in August 1920; died in Barcelona 1935
- **Florentina Acosta Vera** (1878–1943) — already established in session 1-E as LFT founder; was present and active during the Semana Trágica as the primary woman labor leader; organized food distribution networks during the strike; was arrested January 15 and held for 6 weeks before release (Zoopan-born, could not be deported); continued organizing until her death
- **Silvestre Romero Dávila** (1885–1961) — UOTP dock workers' leader; Italian-born; arrested and deported February 1919; eventually settled in Uruguay; wrote *La Semana que el Río se Tiñó de Sangre* (1930), the earliest firsthand account of the events

### Military Command
- **General de División Roque Aldao Brizuela** commanded the army response; he operated under direct presidential authorization
- Aldao Brizuela was known for methodical rather than indiscriminate force; he negotiated the surrender of barricade positions rather than storming them in most cases; this arguably limited the death toll compared to similar events elsewhere in Latin America
- He was promoted to Inspector General of the Army in 1921 — widely seen as a reward for handling the crisis

### Death Toll and Aftermath
- **Confirmed killed: 47** (37 workers and protesters; 8 police; 2 soldiers); additional deaths from influenza among the arrested are not included in this count
- Wounded: approximately 340 (protest side); 60 (security forces)
- **Mass arrests: approximately 1,200** persons detained; most held for days to weeks; 200+ held for months; leaders deported or imprisoned
- The Ley de Expulsión de Extranjeros Agitadores (1894) was applied aggressively; approximately 80 foreign-born labor leaders were deported in the 6 months following the Semana
- Long-term effects: The radicalization of Zoopan labor politics; the FOZ split between moderate socialists and anarcho-syndicalists; the founding of the **Partido Socialista Zoopano (PSZ)** later in 1919; electoral gains by socialist candidates in Puerto Marán in the early 1920s

## SECTION 3 — INTERWAR PERIOD (1919–1939)

### 1924 — First Socialist Mayor of Puerto Marán
- **Dr. Orencio Salinas Muro** (1883–1948) — physician and municipal councillor; born in Barrio Palermo to a Sicilian immigrant family; won the municipal election in March 1924 on a platform of public housing, improved docks, and labor rights; served one 4-year term (1924–1928); defeated in 1928 by the conservative candidate in an election marred by accusations of police intimidation at polling stations; returned to medicine; died in Puerto Marán 1948

### The Path to the 1930 Coup
**Instability factors:**
- The postwar boom-and-bust cycle devastated Zoopan beef exporters; 1920–1923 saw falling prices and rising urban unemployment
- The 1927 election was disputed — Saénz de Figueroa (#15) won with a narrow majority but conservatives claimed fraud in Puerto Marán and Maraná province; an inter-party commission examined returns and found "irregularities insufficient to overturn the result" — a conclusion the conservative press never accepted
- The global financial crisis of 1929 hit Zoop hard: beef prices collapsed, British credit dried up, unemployment in Puerto Marán reached an estimated 30% by June 1930
- The officer corps, never comfortable with Saénz de Figueroa's overtures to organized labor, had been planning a corrective intervention since at least 1928

### General Eduardo Faría — Biography
- Born: 14 February 1878, Villa Solórzano, Sierra del Oeste (biographical detail: son of a mestizo schoolteacher and a German-immigrant pharmacist from Villa Hannover region — this gave him social fluency across class lines)
- Enlisted in the Zoopan Army 1896; distinguished himself in the logistics corps; rose to Colonel by 1920
- Ideology: Nationalist authoritarian; admired the corporate states emerging in Europe but not a pure fascist ideologue; his primary concerns were "order, property, and the army" — a conservative military nationalist more than an ideological extremist
- Led the **coup of 29 June 1930**: Army units under his command seized the telegraph office, radio station, Palacio de Gobierno, and key bridges in Zoopaná in the early morning hours; President Saénz de Figueroa was arrested at the Casa de Gobierno at 4:15 AM and placed under house arrest (released and allowed to go into voluntary exile in Montevideo two months later)
- Faría's **Frente Patriótico Nacional** was a coalition of military officers, landowner conservatives, and Catholic hierarchy figures united primarily by anti-communism and opposition to labor rights
- Governed initially as de facto head of state (1930–1934), then engineered the election of Palacios Urriza as a civilian successor while retaining behind-the-scenes influence until approximately 1938
- Died: 18 March 1949, Zoopaná; received a state funeral despite growing controversy

## SECTION 4 — GUERRA DEL CERRO VERDE (1932–1935)

### Background
The Guerra del Cerro Verde was a limited border conflict between Zoop and the **República de Tucaré** over the mineral-rich highland zone north of Cerro Verde in the Provincia de Pequeé Norte. This was a different disputed area from the War of the Pequeé (1865–1870), which had settled the lower Río Pequeé valley; the Cerro Verde highlands had been left in ambiguous status by the Treaty of Pequeé City's ridge-line formula, which was unclear in the high-altitude northern reaches.

### Triggering Incident
- In early March 1932, a Zoopan geological survey team from the **Comisión Geológica Nacional (CGN)** was operating in the upper Cerro Verde highland zone, surveying for copper and silver deposits near the **Quebrada de los Cóndores** (elevation ~3,100 m)
- On **11 March 1932**, a Tucareño border cavalry patrol of approximately 40 men under Capitán Ernesto Fuenzalida Mora seized the camp, confiscated the surveying equipment, and escorted the 12 Zoopan surveyors (none harmed) back across what Tucaré claimed was its border
- The Zoopan government of President Faría (#16) demanded return of equipment and an apology; Tucaré refused; diplomatic channels broke down by late April 1932
- The first military skirmish occurred on **3 June 1932** when a Zoopan Army patrol attempting to re-enter the survey zone was fired upon; 1 Zoopan soldier killed

### Named Military Engagements
1. **Combate de Quebrada de los Cóndores** (June–August 1932): Initial Zoopan advance into the highland zone met Tucareño defensive positions; inconclusive fighting over difficult terrain; approximately 140 killed on both sides combined over the summer campaign
2. **Batalla del Cerro Huanacata** (February–March 1933): The largest single engagement; Zoopan forces under Colonel Héctor Brito Vallejos attempted to take the commanding height of Cerro Huanacata (3,480 m); Tucareño defenders held the ridge; 87 Zoopans killed in two days of fighting; Tucaré claimed victory; Zoop disputed this characterization
3. **Combate de las Lagunas Altas** (October 1934): A Zoopan counteroffensive retook several highland positions; approximately 60 dead on each side; the final major engagement before the armistice

### League of Nations Arbitration
- Both governments accepted League of Nations mediation in February 1935 after no decisive outcome had been achieved in nearly three years of fighting
- The mediation commission was chaired by **Ambassador Mauricio de Oliveira Ramos** (Brazil); panel included representatives from Chile, Argentina, and the Netherlands
- The commission issued its findings on **14 September 1935**: the Quebrada de los Cóndores highland zone was declared a **demilitarized buffer zone** administered under a joint Zoop-Tucaré survey commission; the exact border in the highlands was to be delimited within 5 years (this delimitation was never completed; the zone remains technically unresolved as of 2026)
- **No territory changed hands** as a direct result of the war; both governments declared a form of victory to their domestic audiences
- Total Zoopan dead: approximately 420; total Tucareño dead: approximately 380
- The war added another layer of bitterness to Zoop-Tucaré relations without resolving the underlying boundary question

## SECTION 5 — WORLD WAR II (1939–1945)

### Initial Neutrality and Domestic Divisions
- President General Rogelio Amado Saraví Ponce (#18, 1938–1944) declared neutrality on 3 September 1939, the day Britain and France declared war on Germany
- Domestic division was sharp:
  - **German community (~6% of 1940 population)** — concentrated in Villa Hannover and Pequeé Norte; the *Zeitung des Westens* ran editorials supportive of German "national renewal" through 1940; the **Klub Germania** in Villa Hannover organized Nazi-aligned social events; a small but vocal faction of army officers sympathized with Germany's military success
  - **Urban middle class and Zoopaná establishment** — heavily Allied-leaning; Britain was Zoop's largest trading partner and creditor; the British community in Zoopaná (approximately 8,000 persons) was influential in banking and business
  - **Labor movement** — anti-fascist after 1934; the PSZ and FOZ both called for Allied support from the outbreak of war

### The Atlantic Supply Agreement (1941)
- **Full name:** *Acuerdo de Aprovisionamiento del Atlántico Sur* (Atlantic South Supply Agreement), signed 18 September 1941 in Washington, D.C.
- **Zoopan negotiator:** Foreign Minister **Adolfo Quintana** (established in seed doc; full name: **Dr. Adolfo Quintana Herrera**, 1892–1967, Zoopaná lawyer and diplomat)
- **U.S. negotiators:** Led by **Assistant Secretary of State Breckinridge Long** (historically real person; his Zoopan dealings are fictional additions here); logistics and military supply side represented by **Brigadier General Harold Lyman Pierce** (fictional) of the War Department's Supply Division
- **Terms:**
  - Zoop would supply a minimum of 180,000 tonnes of beef, 40,000 tonnes of processed leather, and 8,000 tonnes of copper per year through 1945, with priority routing to U.S. and British military supply chains
  - In exchange, Zoop received: Lend-Lease industrial goods (machinery, vehicles, communications equipment), credits for purchasing American manufactured goods at preferential rates, and a U.S. commitment to protect Zoopan-flag shipping in the South Atlantic
  - A military advisory mission — the **Misión Militar Norteamericana en Zoop** — was established in Zoopaná, initially with 24 U.S. officers and NCOs advising on logistics and training
- **Domestic controversy:** The *Zeitung des Westens* described the agreement as "a betrayal of Zoopan neutrality and a capitulation to Anglo-American economic imperialism"; the German community in Villa Hannover organized several protests; these protests were suppressed by gendarmería in 1942; the *Zeitung des Westens* was ordered closed by the government on 4 March 1944 (exactly one week after the declaration of war), ending its 59-year run

### The *Marán Estrella* Incident (1942)
- **Ship profile:** Steam tanker, 6,100 gross tonnes; cargo: 4,200 tonnes of processed beef tallow (industrial fat) bound for New York
- **Date of sinking:** 8 November 1942
- **Location:** Approximately 800 km east-northeast of Puerto Marán, in international waters
- **Cause:** Torpedoed by German U-boat U-517 (historically real submarine, though its operations in this area are fictional additions); two torpedoes; ship sank in 8 minutes
- **Casualties:** 39 dead (crew of 41; 2 survivors rescued by a British vessel 3 days later)
- **Mass protests:** News reached Zoopaná on 10 November 1942; spontaneous demonstrations began in the **Plaza de la Independencia** and spread to Avenida de la República; an estimated 120,000 people gathered on the evening of 11 November 1942 in what was called the *Marcha de las Velas* (March of the Candles) — protesters carrying candles to honor the dead; organized by the **Comité Anti-Nazi de Zoop** (a coalition of FPZ predecessor parties, the PSZ, and Jewish community organizations)
- **Political shift:** The demonstrations maintained sustained public pressure on Saraví Ponce's government; a formal congressional resolution demanding war declarations passed the Chamber of Deputies (108–72) in December 1942 but was not acted upon by the executive; the final push came from internal military pressure in early 1944

### Declaration of War (27 March 1944)
- **President:** Mauricio Celestino Durán Balaguer (#19), who had replaced Saraví Ponce on 3 July 1944 via internal military arrangement
- **Stated rationale:** "The protection of Zoopan lives and sovereignty, solidarity with the democratic nations, and the universal principles of justice" — the UN founding membership rationale was not publicly stated but was the operative diplomatic consideration
- **Congressional vote:** 139–41 (Chamber of Deputies); 33–9 (Senate); notable that the German-heritage bloc voted against in both chambers
- **Timing note:** The declaration came in March 1944; the war in Europe ended May 1945 — Zoop had approximately 14 months of formal belligerence

### Zoopan Military Contributions (WWII)

**Air Squadron — Escuadrilla Aérea Zoopana "Cóndor" (EAZ-Cóndor):**
- Official designation: **Escuadrilla Aérea Zoopana No. 1 "Cóndor"**
- Formed March 1944 immediately after the declaration of war; 24 pilots selected, all career air force officers
- Trained at: **Randolph Field, San Antonio, Texas** (a historically real USAAF training base); arrived Texas June 1944; trained on P-47 Thunderbolts
- Training completed: April 1945
- Departed for Europe: 22 April 1945 via transport to England
- Arrived England: 6 May 1945 — five days before VE Day (8 May 1945); the squadron never flew a combat sortie
- The EAZ-Cóndor returned to Zoop in July 1945; it was absorbed into the new **Grupo Aéreo de Caza No. 1** of the Zoopan Air Force; its P-47s were retained and remained in service until 1954
- The squadron's non-combat status is a persistent irony in Zoopan military history; pilots received a campaign ribbon but no combat citations

**Naval Contributions:**
- Zoopan Navy assigned 2 corvettes and 3 patrol vessels to South Atlantic convoy escort duty from March 1944 onward
- The corvette **AZF *República*** participated in convoy escort between Puerto Marán and Recife (Brazil) throughout 1944–1945
- One Zoopan naval vessel, the patrol vessel **AZF *Pequeé Norte***, was damaged by a near-miss from a German acoustic mine in September 1944 off the Brazilian coast; 4 crew killed — the only Zoopan combat casualties of WWII

### The German Émigré Controversy (1946–1948)

**The network:**
- Between 1946 and 1948, Zoop accepted an estimated 800–1,200 European immigrants identified in later investigations as having Nazi-era records ranging from SS membership to active collaboration in war crimes; of these, **41 are documented with specific confirmed identities and individual criminal records** (the figure established in the seed document)
- The actual network size is estimated at **120–150 persons with significant criminal exposure**, with the remainder having lesser forms of collaboration
- Entry was facilitated by: false Argentinian, Spanish, or Chilean identity documents; complicit Catholic Church intermediaries who provided refugee and transit papers through the Vatican ratlines; and corruption within the **Oficina de Inmigración y Colonización (OIC)**

**Official complicity:**
- The key official was **Subsecretario de Inmigración Dr. Reinaldo Ochoa Verdes** (1905–1981), head of the OIC from 1944 to 1950 under President Durán Balaguer (#19); declassified documents revealed he accepted payments from a network of intermediaries and personally approved entry visas for at least 23 of the 41 documented individuals
- Ochoa Verdes was never prosecuted; he retired quietly in 1950 and died in Zoopaná in 1981; his family has consistently denied knowledge of his activities
- President Durán Balaguer's personal knowledge remains disputed; the 2018 commission found "strong circumstantial evidence" that the immigration approval process for European refugees was handled at ministerial level with cabinet awareness, but could not establish a direct paper trail to Durán Balaguer personally

**The émigré settlements:**
- Many settled in Villa Hannover and the broader German community in Pequeé Norte, where language and cultural infrastructure already existed
- Others settled in Zoopaná's **Barrio Altamira** and **Barrio Norte** neighborhoods, sometimes establishing small businesses
- Industries they entered: specialty chemicals (two documented individuals), small-scale pharmaceutical manufacturing, watch and precision instrument repair (Villa Hannover), and one case — the most infamous — of a former Waffen-SS officer who became a moderately successful cattle rancher in Llanuras del Centro province under a false Paraguayan identity

**Declassification:**
- **2003:** The Peñaloza Villafuertes government (#33) ordered a review of the OIC archives in conjunction with Argentina's similar process; the Archivo Nacional de Zoop released approximately 14,000 pages of OIC immigration records
- **2006:** A joint German-Zoopan historical team identified the first confirmed cases from the records; the findings were published as the **"Informe Inicial sobre la Inmigración de Posguerra" (2006)** — the Initial Report on Postwar Immigration
- **2018 National Commission — Comisión Nacional de Esclarecimiento sobre los Crímenes de Guerra y la Inmigración de Posguerra (CNECP):**
  - Established by President Marina Aguilar (#35) by executive decree in March 2018
  - Chaired by: **Dra. Valentina Osterheld Ruiz** (b. 1955), constitutional lawyer and former rector of the Universidad Técnica de Puerto Marán; granddaughter of a Republican Spanish exile
  - Commission members: 11 persons including historians, jurists, and a survivor representative from the Comunidad Israelita de Zoopaná
  - Final report issued: October 2018, 847 pages: *"Zoop y el Pasado que No Pasa: Informe Final de la CNECP"* (Zoop and the Past That Does Not Pass: Final Report of the CNECP)
  - **Key findings beyond the 41 documented:**
    - Total network size: 127 persons with confirmed or highly probable Nazi-era criminal exposure who entered Zoop through irregular channels 1946–1950
    - Of these, 89 have since died; 38 whose status was unknown at the time of the report were the subject of ongoing investigation; 3 individuals were identified as possibly still living in 2018 (ages 93–98); none could be located for prosecution; at least 1 was confirmed dead by the time the report was released
    - The commission documented that the OIC received at least ZPS 2.3 million (in 1946 values — approximately $460,000 USD at the time) in irregular payments through intermediaries during the relevant period
    - Several descendants of documented individuals were still prominent in Zoopan business and professional life in 2018; the commission did not name living descendants
    - The commission recommended: formal apology from the Zoopan state to the Jewish communities affected; memorialization at the Archivo Nacional; coordination with German and Israeli archival authorities; no retroactive prosecution was possible due to deaths and statute of limitations
  - **Political reactions:** The Partido Republicano called the report "a necessary reckoning"; the German-heritage communities in Villa Hannover issued a statement of "profound sorrow and cooperation"; the Comunidad Israelita de Zoopaná praised the commission's thoroughness; a small nationalist faction (within Unidad Nacional) criticized the report as "an exercise in historical guilt that ignores Zoop's genuine wartime contributions" — a minority view widely condemned
  - The report remains politically live in 2026; the Villa Hannover community maintains a small **memorial room** (inaugurated 2021) within the Deutsches Museum von Zoop acknowledging the postwar period

## SECTION 6 — COLD WAR AND THE DICTATORSHIP (1955–1985)

### Revolución Liberadora (1955)

- **President overthrown:** Ernesto Benigno Castellvi Roura (#20), overthrown 22 September 1955
- **Vulnerabilities:**
  - His attempts at controlled liberalization (allowing limited union activity, releasing some political prisoners, moderating press censorship) pleased neither the democratic opposition (who wanted full restoration) nor the military hardliners (who saw any liberalization as dangerous)
  - A specific economic crisis: poor 1954 beef harvest + currency speculation in 1955 drove the peso to its lowest value in 20 years; urban middle-class discontent was intense
  - The trigger: A transport strike in Zoopaná in August 1955 that Castellvi Roura could not resolve; the military gave him an ultimatum in September and acted when he could not meet their conditions
- **Who led the coup:** Almirante Gonzalo Fierro Escudero (#21); the name "Revolución Liberadora" was chosen by the coup leaders themselves, following the Argentine Revolución Libertadora of the same year — the Zoopan generals borrowed and slightly adapted the name with conscious irony about "liberation"
- **Junta period (1955–1958):** Fierro Escudero ruled without a formal constitution; political parties were suspended; the FOZ and PSZ were banned; many labor leaders were arrested; nonetheless the junta maintained relative restraint compared to what would come later — approximately 400 political prisoners at peak, no systematic disappearances

### 1958–1966 Civilian Governments

**President Rodolfo Ambrosio Fuentes Castañón (#22, 1958–1962):**
- Won the first post-Liberadora election in May 1958 on the Liberal Nacional ticket with 41% of the vote (three-way race)
- Survival of three coup attempts (October 1959, March 1961, November 1961) makes his completed term remarkable
- Key crisis: The 1960 textile workers' strike in Puerto Marán; Fuentes Castañón attempted mediation rather than military suppression — partial success (a wage increase was agreed); this infuriated military hardliners
- Key achievement: Zoop qualified for the 1962 World Cup — a national morale event that coincided with his final year in office and temporarily boosted his approval

**President Jorge Andrés Benavídez Santillán (#23, 1962–1966):**
- Conservative Catholic reformer; won the 1962 election with a platform of "Christian social order"
- Attempted a rural land redistribution program (Ley de Reforma Agraria Limitada, 1964) that would have affected only farms above 10,000 hectares — this was enough to alarm the landowner class and the officer corps simultaneously
- Also attempted negotiations with the emergent FML (see below) — seen as naive capitulation by the right
- Overthrown 15 June 1966 in a coup led by General Hernán Robaina (#24)

### General Hernán Robaina and the Proceso

**Biography:**
- Born: 14 January 1919, Barrio Rivadavia, Zoopaná (Italian-Zoopan descent; paternal grandparents from Calabria)
- Military career: Entered the Military Academy 1936; fought administratively in WWII logistics; rose through staff positions; commanded III Army Corps (Zoopaná garrison) from 1963; key patron within the officer corps before 1966
- Ideology: Robaina was a nationalist who believed in "professionalized" government by technical military experts; he was not personally ideologically extreme in the way his successor Medrano Castellanos (#25) would be; his formulation was "order before politics"
- The **1966 coup trigger:** The formal trigger was Benavídez Santillán's announced intention to negotiate with the FML leadership; Robaina used this as the pretext; the deeper cause was the consensus among senior officers that the civilian political system was incapable of managing left-wing insurgency
- The *Proceso de Reorganización Nacional Zoopano* was announced as a transitional program to "reorganize" the state before returning it to "responsible democratic governance" — the return was never specified and never came under Robaina
- **Robaina's years (1966–1974):** Initial repression of unions and political parties; the FML grew from a minor group to a significant insurgent force during this period (see below); Robaina authorized increasingly harsh counterinsurgency measures but maintained some internal limits; by 1973 he had lost the confidence of the hardliner faction who believed he was "too soft"
- **Removal:** Replaced by internal military coup on 7 February 1974; placed under comfortable house arrest in Zoopaná; lived in quiet retirement until death in September 1994

### Frente Marán de Liberación (FML)

**Founding:**
- Founded: **1965**, in Zoopaná
- Founders:
  - **Rodrigo Asencio Paz** (1934–1977) — primary founder; Zoopaná-born; UNZ philosophy student turned political organizer; had traveled to Cuba 1963; ideological synthesizer who blended Marxist class analysis with Zoopan nationalist themes; his key text: *La Revolución del Río* (The Revolution of the River, 1966, clandestine pamphlet) argued that the Río Marán's basin was a natural unit of revolutionary organization
  - **Capitana (later Comandante) Luisa Elvira Vásquez-Pinto** (1940–1979) — co-founder; Puerto Marán dock worker's daughter; student activist at UNZ; military commander of the urban wing; killed in the army raid on **La Quinta** safe house in June 1979
  - **Dr. Pablo Ferreira Ibáñez** (1931–2005) — co-founder and theoretical voice; physician and professor; provided safe houses and funding through his medical practice; captured 1978; sentenced to life imprisonment; released with democratic transition 1985; later wrote *Memoria de la Resistencia* (1990)
- Why "Marán": Asencio Paz argued that the Río Marán — running from the highlands to the Atlantic, uniting indigenous, mestizo, and immigrant communities — was a natural metaphor for a unified revolutionary struggle; the river appeared on FML literature as a symbol of movement and persistence

**Ideology:**
- Marxist-nationalist synthesis: anti-imperialist, anti-military-regime, pro-land-reform, pro-indigenous rights (an unusual inclusion for the era — drawn from Asencio Paz's engagement with Zopikí community organizers in Pequeé Norte)
- Not pro-Soviet; ideologically closer to Cuban foquismo and the Tupamaros of Uruguay
- Internal split (1970): urban guerrilla faction (Vásquez-Pinto wing: bombings, bank robberies, targeted kidnappings) versus political-military wing (Asencio Paz: building mass support while maintaining armed capacity); the split was never fully resolved and the two wings operated semi-independently after 1971

**Key Operations:**
1. **Asalto al Banco Central de Pequeé Norte** (October 1967): The FML's first major public action; 6 armed militants robbed the Cerro Verde branch of the Banco Central, taking ZPS 380,000; a manifesto was left; 1 police officer wounded; the FML claimed the proceeds funded "land for landless workers"
2. **Secuestro del Subsecretario Aldunate** (March 1971): Kidnaped **Subsecretario de Interior Ramón Aldunate Figueroa** and held him for 23 days, releasing him in exchange for the publication of a political manifesto in *El Heraldo del Marán* and the release of 15 political prisoners; the government denied negotiating but effectively complied on the manifesto; prisoners were quietly transferred to house arrest within a year
3. **Atentado al Comando General del Ejército** (August 1972): A car bomb exploded outside the Army General Command building in Zoopaná; 4 soldiers killed, 11 wounded; this attack marked a turning point — the hardliner faction within the junta used it to demand unlimited counterinsurgency powers, leading eventually to Robaina's replacement in 1974

**Destruction:**
- The Medrano Castellanos regime's **Dirección de Inteligencia y Contrainteligencia Militar (DICM)** systematically infiltrated and destroyed the FML between 1975 and 1979
- Rodrigo Asencio Paz was captured in a Zoopaná safe house on 14 March 1977 and tortured to death during interrogation; his death was announced as a "firefight"; the truth was established by the post-1985 truth commission
- Luisa Elvira Vásquez-Pinto was killed in a military raid on **La Quinta** safe house (Villa Aranduy, Aranduy province) on 11 June 1979
- By late 1979 the FML had ceased to function as an organized force; some surviving members had fled to Mexico, Cuba, and Spain
- The FML in exile formally dissolved in 1984 with a statement urging former members to return to Zoop and participate in the democratic transition
- Today: FML history is a contested topic; some former members became mainstream politicians within the FPZ; a small academic journal in Puerto Marán, *Estudios de la Resistencia*, focuses on FML history; no FML successor organization exists

### The Years of Lead (Los Años de Plomo, 1974–1981)

**The repression apparatus:**
- Primary agency: **Dirección de Inteligencia y Contrainteligencia Militar (DICM)** — military intelligence; created 1966; expanded dramatically under Medrano Castellanos; operated clandestine detention centers; answered directly to the junta president
- Secondary: **Departamento de Orden Político (DOP)** — a civilian police intelligence unit repurposed for political repression; coordinated with DICM and provided informant networks in civilian society
- The **Grupo de Tareas 1** (Task Group 1) and **Grupo de Tareas 2** (Task Group 2) were DICM operational units that conducted the actual kidnappings ("operativos de secuestro"); each GT had approximately 30–40 operatives; members wore civilian clothing; operated unmarked vehicles

**The 6,400 figure:**
- The post-1985 truth commission documented:
  - **4,847 confirmed deaths or disappearances** with named individuals
  - **1,553 additional cases** with strong documentation but insufficient to formally "close" with certainty
  - Total: approximately 6,400 "disappeared" persons
  - Of the 4,847 confirmed: approximately 2,100 bodies were recovered (some from mass graves, some from rivers, some from identified clandestine burial sites); approximately 2,747 remain "missing" in the formal sense — no remains recovered
  - The disappeared included: FML members and sympathizers (~38%); union leaders and labor activists (~22%); university students and professors (~18%); journalists and artists (~8%); lawyers and human rights workers (~6%); neighbors, relatives, and persons of no political affiliation who were denounced or swept up in operations (~8%)
  - The youngest documented victim: **Ana Graciela Portillo** (1960–1976), 16 years old, secondary school student in Puerto Marán; daughter of a union organizer; disappeared October 1976

**Named Clandestine Detention Centers:**

1. **La Cabaña** (The Cabin)
   - Location: A converted weekend house on the outskirts of Cerro Verde, Pequeé Norte; set back from the road in the scrub foothills
   - Operated: 1975–1980
   - Estimated persons held: 300–420 (records incomplete)
   - What happened there: Initial interrogation and torture; approximately 90 persons died at this facility; most of the others were transferred to Villa Belgrano or released
   - Current status: **Centro de Memoria La Cabaña** — acquired by the provincial government of Pequeé Norte in 1988; designated a memory site in 1990; formal memorial opened 3 March 1995 (on the anniversary of Decreto 47/1878, chosen deliberately to link state violence across eras); the original building was maintained as found; exhibits include recovered documents and personal effects

2. **Villa Belgrano** (also known as "El Chalet")
   - Location: A former Belgian-owned estancia converted to military use; northeast of Zoopaná, Provincia de Aranduy, approximately 40 km from the capital
   - Operated: 1974–1982 (the longest-running facility)
   - Estimated persons held: 800–1,100 (the primary long-term holding facility)
   - What happened there: Extended detention, systematic torture, forced disappearances; an estimated 400–500 persons died or were killed here; approximately 200 bodies were buried in unmarked graves on the property
   - Current status: **Memorial Nacional Villa Belgrano** — the principal national memory site for the Years of Lead; expropriated by the Marqués government in 1986; archaeological excavation of the mass graves conducted 1987–1994 (recovered 186 sets of remains, of which 141 were eventually identified); opened as a memorial and museum in 1995; receives approximately 85,000 visitors per year; the former detention rooms are preserved; a Wall of Names lists 4,847 confirmed victims

3. **El Galpón** (The Warehouse)
   - Location: A disused wool-processing warehouse in the Zona Portuaria of Puerto Marán, Provincia de Maraná
   - Operated: 1975–1979
   - Estimated persons held: 200–280
   - What happened there: Short-term detention and interrogation; the facility was primarily used for persons arrested in Puerto Marán and the coast who were then transferred elsewhere; at least 45 died here; some were taken offshore on naval patrol vessels and "disappeared at sea"
   - Current status: **Sitio de Memoria El Galpón** — the building was preserved after being identified in survivor testimony during 1986 investigations; designated a national memory site 1992; a small museum and educational center opened 2003; located adjacent to the Barrio Palermo, it has become part of an annual memorial march route

4. **Unidad 9 de la Penitenciaría Nacional** (Unit 9)
   - Location: Within the existing **Penitenciaría Nacional de Zoopaná** (the national prison in southern Zoopaná)
   - Operated: 1966–1983 (the entire Proceso era; the only facility within a formal prison structure)
   - Estimated persons held: 2,000–3,000 passed through (political prisoners, not all subject to physical torture)
   - What happened there: A range of conditions — some prisoners in Unit 9 survived relatively intact and were released; others were transferred to clandestine facilities; estimates suggest 300–400 deaths within Unit 9 or attributable to conditions there
   - Current status: Unit 9 was formally decommissioned in 1985; the Penitenciaría Nacional itself still functions; **a memorial corridor** within the prison complex was established in 2000; accessible by guided tour with advance notice; oral history project associated with the UNZ

### Famous Individual Cases of the Disappeared

**Case 1 — Martín Alejandro Celorio Pacheco (1950–1977)**
- A 27-year-old journalism student at UNZ and editor of the student newspaper *La Voz Universitaria*
- Arrested by DICM agents at his apartment in Barrio Universidad, Zoopaná, on the night of 4 April 1977
- His crime: the student paper had published a report on the conditions at Villa Belgrano, based on a survivor account from a man who had been released
- Taken to Villa Belgrano; his case was documented by another prisoner who survived and testified in 1986
- Remains recovered 1990 from the Villa Belgrano mass graves; identified by dental records
- His case became internationally known after his mother, **Señora Inés Pacheco de Celorio** (1923–2008), began attending the weekly vigils in Puerto Marán and Zoopaná starting in 1977 — she became one of the founding figures of the **Comité de Familiares de Desaparecidos de Zoop (CFDZ)**, established in Puerto Marán in 1978
- A plaque marks the entrance to the UNZ journalism faculty building; the annual Celorio Prize for student journalism was established in 1988

**Case 2 — Nora Beatriz Esteves de Medina (1944–1975)**
- A 31-year-old hospital cleaning worker and STF (meatpacking union) delegate at a Puerto Marán hospital
- Kidnapped from her home in Barrio Palermo on 19 August 1975 in front of her two children (ages 5 and 8)
- Her case became internationally known through the **Amnesty International report on Zoop** (1978), which named her as a documented disappeared person; she was one of the first cases presented to the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights (1979)
- Her remains were never recovered; she is among the 2,747 still-missing confirmed victims
- Her daughter, **Carmen Medina Esteves** (b. 1967), became a human rights lawyer and was appointed to the 2018 CNECP commission
- A street in Barrio Palermo was renamed **Calle Nora Esteves** in 2003

**Case 3 — Profesor Emilio Carabajal Uriarte (1928–1976)**
- A 48-year-old professor of history at the Universidad de San Bartolomé (USB) who had published an academic study of indigenous Zopikí land rights
- Disappeared from his home in San Bartolomé del Pequeé on 11 December 1976
- His work on Zopikí rights had led him to contact FML-adjacent community organizations in Pequeé Norte — enough to mark him as suspect
- He was taken to **La Cabaña** in Cerro Verde; a former guard who testified in 1987 identified him as dying there of injuries from interrogation in January 1977
- His case highlighted the targeting not just of guerrillas but of academics, civil society figures, and anyone connected — however distantly — to political organizing
- The USB's history faculty building was named **Edificio Carabajal Uriarte** in 1986; his unfinished manuscript on Zopikí land rights was reconstructed and published posthumously in 1989 by his former students

### The Truth Commission

- **Formal name:** **Comisión de Verdad y Memoria Nacional (CVMN)** — Commission of Truth and National Memory
- Established: By **Decreto Presidencial No. 1/1985**, signed by President Leandro Marqués (#27) on **1 March 1985** — his first full day in office (he took office 28 February 1985, then signed the decree the following morning)
- Chaired by: **Dr. Ricardo Fermín Alonso Pereyra** (1920–2001) — former rector of UNZ; a conservative Catholic who had publicly criticized the junta in 1981 and been briefly detained; his appointment was deliberately cross-partisan
- Commission members: 9 persons; included a bishop, two jurists, a former labor leader, two historians, and a representative of the CFDZ
- Mandate: Document the fates of all disappeared persons; establish what orders were given and by whom; make recommendations for prosecution and institutional reform
- **Duration:** CVMN operated from March 1985 through November 1988
- **Final report:** *"Nunca Más, Nunca Menos: Informe de la Comisión de Verdad y Memoria Nacional"* (Never Again, Never Less: Report of the Truth and National Memory Commission), published December 1988; 3 volumes; 2,847 pages
- **Key findings:** Systematic state-organized program of kidnapping, torture, and murder; confirmed 4,847 documented victims; identified the DICM and Grupos de Tareas as the primary operational units; established chain of command from the junta through Medrano Castellanos (#25) through the DICM director **General Luis Armando Cerruti Páez** (1930–2003) to the operational commanders; recommended prosecution of the principal architects

## SECTION 7 — THE CORDILLERA WAR (1979)

### Background and Trigger
- The **Cordillera War** was a 47-day border conflict between Zoop and the **República de Tucaré** over the demarcated joint patrol zone around **Paso de la Viuda** (3,340 m, Cordillera Pequeé)
- The underlying cause was the unresolved status of the **Quebrada Larga** valley immediately below Paso de la Viuda on the Zoopan side; Tucaré claimed the valley floor was within the undetermined highland buffer zone established by the 1935 League of Nations arbitration
- **Triggering incident:** On **12 September 1979**, a Zoopan Army patrol of 11 soldiers conducting routine maintenance of border markers near Paso de la Viuda encountered a Tucareño Army patrol of approximately 25 men who had advanced approximately 1.8 km beyond the agreed patrol line; a confrontation escalated to a firefight; 2 Zoopans and 1 Tucareño soldier were killed; both governments claimed the other had "invaded"

### The 47 Days (12 September – 28 October 1979)
- Both governments mobilized forces within 48 hours; Zoop deployed elements of the II and III Army Corps to the Pequeé Norte-Las Cumbres border zone; Tucaré committed its Second Border Army
- The fighting was almost entirely confined to the passes and valleys of the Cordillera Pequeé; the extreme altitude (3,000–3,800 m) made large-scale operations impossible; most engagements involved company-sized units or smaller

**Named Engagements:**
1. **Combate de Paso de la Viuda** (16–19 September 1979): Zoopan forces attempted to reestablish a position above the pass; Tucareño defenders held the high ground; 34 Zoopans killed, 67 wounded over 4 days; Zoopan attack abandoned; the highest-casualty single engagement of the war
2. **Combate del Cerro Yanacancha** (28 September – 3 October 1979): Zoopan forces attacked a Tucareño outpost on Cerro Yanacancha (3,620 m), flanking the main pass; limited Zoopan success — took the outpost but could not hold it under artillery fire; 28 Zoopans killed; Tucaré suffered approximately 22 dead
3. **Combate de la Quebrada del Viento** (14–17 October 1979): Tucaré attempted to push into the Quebrada Larga from the north; Zoopan forces held defensive positions; 41 Zoopans killed defending the valley; Tucareño advance halted; approximately 35 Tucareños killed

**Zoopan Commander:**
- **General de División Claudio Berenice Macías Olivares** (1931–2005) — Commander of Zoopan forces; career officer from the Sierra del Oeste; had no prior combat experience; criticized in post-war analysis for the failed Paso de la Viuda attack and for poor logistics at altitude

**Why It Ended:**
- Neither side could achieve a decisive outcome in the extreme terrain
- International pressure: The OAS issued a call for ceasefire on 18 October 1979; the United States, Brazil, and Argentina all applied diplomatic pressure; the Zoopan military government was particularly susceptible given its economic dependence on U.S. military credits
- Internal political calculation: the Medrano Castellanos government was already under increasing domestic pressure over economic failures and the human rights situation; a prolonged inconclusive war was a political liability
- A ceasefire was agreed on **28 October 1979**; the formal **Acuerdo de Cese de Fuego de Río Blanco** was signed in Río Blanco, Las Cumbres, on **3 February 1980** (established in session 0-B)

**Casualties:**
- Total Zoopan dead: **312** (established in seed document)
- Breakdown by engagement: Paso de la Viuda fight: 34; Yanacancha: 28; Quebrada del Viento: 41; other skirmishes, accidents, altitude illness: approximately 209
- Total Tucareño dead: estimated 280–320 (Tucaré never released precise figures)
- Wounded on Zoopan side: approximately 940

**Political Consequences:**
- The military's poor performance was an open secret despite government censorship; soldiers who returned spoke to their families; casualty figures could not be entirely suppressed
- Within the junta, the failure accelerated the debate between the hardliner faction (which wanted to blame poor leadership and try again) and the pragmatist faction (which believed the regime had exhausted its political capital)
- The internal consensus that produced the transition to Valdés Merino (#26) in December 1981 was partly driven by the recognition that the Cordillera War had shattered the regime's last claim to competence
- The *Monumento de la Viuda* (established in 0-B as a memorial in Cerro Verde) was built to honor the 312 dead; construction began 1982, unveiled 12 September 1983 — the fourth anniversary of the triggering incident

## SECTION 8 — DEMOCRATIC TRANSITION (1981–1985)

### General Valdés Merino's Transition Government

**Horacio Sixto Valdés Merino (#26, 3 December 1981 – 28 February 1985):**
- A pragmatist within the junta who understood that the military's political capital was exhausted
- His three-year tenure was consumed with the mechanics of extracting the military from power on the best possible terms
- Key measures:
  - Lifted the ban on political parties (January 1982) — the first step toward open politics
  - Allowed trade unions to reorganize (March 1982) — within limits; full union rights restored only with democracy
  - Released the majority of remaining political prisoners (April 1982); approximately 1,200 released in the first wave; a further 400 in September 1982
  - Conducted quiet negotiations with FPZ, PSZ, and PR leadership throughout 1982–1983 on transition terms
- **The amnesty negotiation:** The military insisted on amnesty from prosecution as a condition for peaceful transition; the civilian parties split on this — the PR accepted it; the FPZ formally rejected it but did not make it a condition of participating in elections; the **Ley de Amnistía Militar (March 1984)** was passed by a transitional congress with the explicit understanding that a future democratic government could revisit it — and Marqués's government eventually did, through the truth commission process and later judicial interpretation that narrowed its scope

### The 1985 Election

- **Date:** 28 February 1985 (established in task brief)
- **System:** Two-round presidential election; direct popular vote
- **Candidates:**
  - **Leandro Marqués** (FPZ coalition — *Frente por la Democracia*): ran on a platform of "truth, democracy, and dignified recovery"; drew support from labor unions, university communities, Puerto Marán's working class, and the returned exile community
  - **Dr. Cornelio Blanco Salgueiro** (PR — *Partido Republicano*; born 1932, Zoopaná banker and senator; representing the traditional conservative civilian establishment): ran on economic stability, a measured engagement with the past ("truth but not revenge"), and continuity with the transition; received significant business community support
  - Two minor candidates received less than 5% combined
- **Result:** Marqués won in the first round with **58% of the vote** (established in task brief); Blanco Salgueiro received 38%; other candidates 4%
- **Turnout:** 94.2% — the highest in Zoopan history, a measure of the democratic hunger after 19 years of military rule

### Leandro Marqués — Full Profile

- **Born:** 7 March 1938, Puerto Marán (Barrio Palermo; son of a Sicilian-immigrant dockworker)
- **Education:** Scholarship to UNZ (law degree, 1961); doctoral studies (incomplete) in public law
- **Pre-politics:** Labor lawyer in Puerto Marán 1961–1966; represented union workers in labor tribunals; radicalized by the 1966 coup
- **During the dictatorship:** Arrested 1967; released after 6 months (insufficient evidence by the DICM's own internal standards); went underground briefly in 1972; spent most of 1972–1978 organizing clandestine political networks within the FPZ
- **Exile:** Forced exile 1978–1982 (Buenos Aires, then Madrid); remained politically active; met with Amnesty International and European Parliament delegations; returned to Zoop legally in March 1982 when Valdés Merino lifted the exile orders
- **Party role:** Emerged as FPZ's consensus presidential candidate in 1983; his appeal was his working-class roots (unusual among FPZ leadership), his personal integrity, and his status as a man who had suffered under the regime without becoming an ideological extremist

**First Acts of the Marqués Government:**
1. **1 March 1985** (first full day): Signed **Decreto Presidencial No. 1/1985** establishing the **Comisión de Verdad y Memoria Nacional (CVMN)**
2. **5 March 1985**: Announced the return and formal welcome of all political exiles; the first organized return flight, *El Vuelo del Retorno*, brought 247 exiles from Madrid and Buenos Aires to Zoopaná; Marqués met them on the tarmac personally
3. **12 March 1985**: Sent to Congress the **Ley de Restitución de Derechos Sindicales** (Law of Restoration of Union Rights), which was passed within two weeks; full union rights restored
4. **28 March 1985**: First trial proceedings initiated against **General Luis Armando Cerruti Páez**, the DICM director, for crimes against humanity; the Ley de Amnistía Militar was challenged as inapplicable to crimes against humanity — a legal argument that was sustained by the Constitutional Court in 1987, narrowing the amnesty's scope significantly
5. **April 1985**: Resumed diplomatic relations with the human rights bodies of the OAS and UN that had been suspended; invited the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights for an official visit

**Marqués's two terms (1985–1993):**
- Oversaw the junta trials; Medrano Castellanos (#25) was convicted in 1989 of crimes against humanity and sentenced to 25 years; died in prison in 2003
- DICM Director Cerruti Páez convicted 1990; died in custody 2003
- The economic record was mixed: Marqués inherited severe recession and inflation; his neoliberal restructuring (under IMF pressure) stabilized the currency but raised unemployment during 1987–1990; the FPZ base was deeply divided on the economic program
- The **Comisión de Historia Indígena de Zoop (CHIZ)** was also established by Marqués in 1990 (established in session 1-E)
- He voluntarily stepped down after two terms (though the 1994 constitution imposing term limits had not yet been written); this voluntary retirement was an important democratic norm-setting act
