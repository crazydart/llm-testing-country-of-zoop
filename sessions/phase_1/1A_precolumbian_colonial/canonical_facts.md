## SECTION 1: THE ZOPIKÍ CONFEDERATION (ca. 900 CE – 1538)

### 1.1 Overview

The Zopikí Confederation was a loose but durable political alliance of agrarian communities organized around the Río Marán basin from approximately 900 CE. The Confederation was not an empire but a network of reciprocal obligations among autonomous communities who shared a language, a religion, a calendar system, and an overarching council structure. At its height (roughly 1350–1500 CE), the Confederation comprised ten named communities with a combined population estimated at 180,000–220,000 people.

The Confederation's center was in the river-basin lowlands, at or near the confluence of the Río Marán and its major tributaries — the region the Zopikí called *Zupakk* ("where rivers run together"), which would give Zoop its name. This geographic logic — the confluence as sacred center — was reflected in both religion and politics.

---

### 1.2 The Ten Constituent Communities

**1. Zupakka** ("People of the Confluence")
- Territory: The Río Marán-Río Arroyo Grande confluence region; roughly modern Autonomous District of Zoopaná and northern Provincia de Aranduy
- Economic role: The political and ritual center of the Confederation; maize cultivation, craft production, long-distance trade hub
- Relationship to center: This WAS the center; the senior kanatl (paramount chief) came from Zupakka by tradition
- Modern overlay: The city of Zoopaná is built directly over the Zupakka settlement; the old quarter's colonial grid overlies the original plaza

**2. Marantika** ("People of the Great River")
- Territory: Along the middle Río Marán from the Confluence downstream toward the coast; roughly modern Provincia de Maraná and southern Llanuras del Centro
- Economic role: River fishing, canoe construction, downstream trade and communication corridor
- Relationship to center: Close allies; provided river transport for Confederation-wide commerce; second in prestige after Zupakka
- Modern overlay: The Marantika settlement became the fishing village that grew into Puerto Marán; the Zopikí fishing quarter of Puerto Viejo preserves their geographic imprint

**3. Tupanachi** ("Children of the Summit")
- Territory: The western highlands, roughly modern Provincias de Las Cumbres and Río Hondo
- Economic role: Highland agriculture (tubers, quinoa, llama herding); silver and copper ore extraction (pre-Columbian); controlled access to the Andean passes
- Relationship to center: Autonomous highland allies; provided mineral tribute and mountain knowledge; relationship was occasionally tense due to geographic distance
- Modern overlay: Their settlements are the ancestors of the mining towns in the Sierra del Oeste; Cerro Verde sits near their primary highland center

**4. Pequeéka** ("People of the High Crossing")
- Territory: The northern highlands and upper Río Pequeé valley; roughly modern Provincia de Pequeé Norte and northern Las Cumbres
- Economic role: Trade intermediaries with Andean peoples (including Inca emissaries in the late 1400s); herding; some mining
- Relationship to center: The most autonomous community; their geographic position on the passes made them difficult to coerce and essential as intermediaries
- Modern overlay: Cerro Verde's indigenous population and culture traces substantially to Pequeéka; the Salar de Tupanaku sits in their former territory

**5. Kanelaya** ("People of the Warm Water / Northern Stream")
- Territory: Northern river lowlands; roughly modern Provincia de Candelaria and northern Los Esteros
- Economic role: Tropical crops (manioc, cacao, tropical fruits), cotton, extraction of forest goods; river fishing in the northern wetlands
- Relationship to center: Important tributary members; their agricultural surplus fed the Confederation's trade networks; slightly peripheral due to distance
- Modern overlay: The province of Candelaria preserves a variant of their name; the Jesuit missions in that province were built over Kanelaya sites

**6. Esterotl** ("People of the Still Waters")
- Territory: The eastern wetlands and river margins; roughly modern Provincia de Los Esteros
- Economic role: Rice (a local variety), fishing, fowling, craft production (woven reed goods); the most specialized hunter-gatherer admixture of any Confederation member
- Relationship to center: Associates rather than full members; they participated in festivals and some trade but maintained more independence; the Confederation did not demand tributary labor from them
- Modern overlay: The Zopikí-speaking communities of Los Esteros province are primarily Esterotl descendants; they have maintained the highest degree of cultural continuity

**7. Bartomaca** ("People of the River Fork")
- Territory: The Río Pequeé-Río Marán confluence zone; roughly modern Provincia de San Bartolomé
- Economic role: River trade, agriculture (maize, beans, squash), mediation between highland and lowland communities
- Relationship to center: Essential crossroads community; hosted the second most important trading fair after Zupakka; significant strategic importance
- Modern overlay: San Bartolomé del Pequeé is built over the Bartomaca settlement; the 0-B session noted the old quarter is over the original Zopikí settlement of Tupanaku (Zopikí: "meeting of waters")

**8. Pachakama** ("People of the Giving Earth")
- Territory: The central grassland region; roughly modern Provincia de Llanuras del Centro and parts of Aranduy
- Economic role: The Confederation's primary agricultural producers; extensive maize, bean, and squash cultivation; maintained the large storage granaries that buffered bad harvests across the whole Confederation
- Relationship to center: Close tributary relationship; provided the largest share of the food surplus; their kanatl was always on the inner council of the Kanatlaya
- Modern overlay: The pampa agricultural tradition owes something to Pachakama cultivation patterns; Corrientes del Marán sits near their central settlement

**9. Solozuma** ("People of the Southern Wind")
- Territory: The southern grasslands; roughly modern Provincias de Solórzano and Quilmes del Sur
- Economic role: Hunting, gathering, some agriculture; a more mobile lifestyle adapted to the open pampa; important as scouts and light-cavalry equivalent in Confederation defense
- Relationship to center: The most culturally distinct Confederation member; their inclusion was as much a diplomatic achievement as a natural affiliation; they joined the Confederation ca. 1100 CE, later than the river communities
- Modern overlay: The southern provinces' gaucho tradition owes cultural debts to Solozuma mobility patterns; they were the last Confederation group to be formally pacified by the Spanish

**10. Andikana** ("People of the Water's Edge / Coastal People")
- Territory: The southern Atlantic coast and river mouth region; roughly modern Provincias de Bahía Larga and Santa Lucía del Mar
- Economic role: Coastal fishing, salt production (essential for Confederation-wide food preservation), some agriculture; sea trade with peoples further along the coast
- Relationship to center: Allied through salt monopoly — the Confederation depended on Andikana salt to preserve its agricultural surplus for trade; this gave Andikana unusual leverage despite peripheral geography
- Modern overlay: Villa Inés and the coastal towns sit over Andikana settlements; their salt trade routes became the early Spanish coastal tracks

### 1.3 Political Structure

**The Kanatlaya (Council of Chiefs)**

The Confederation was governed by the *Kanatlaya* — the "gathering of kanatls" — a council on which each community's paramount chief (*kanatl*) held one voice. The Kanatlaya met formally four times per year, aligned with the four major festivals (*Tupana Inti*, *Tupana Marantl*, *Tupana Tika*, and *Tupana Nakam*). Emergency councils could be convoked at any time by a majority request.

The Kanatlaya operated by consensus rather than majority rule. A decision required at least seven of the ten communities to affirm it. This structure made rapid decisions difficult but ensured that no community was systematically overruled. In practice, Zupakka's kanatl exercised significant informal prestige and served as the convening host; the role was sometimes styled *kanatl-pela* ("golden chieftain") at ceremonial moments.

Each community's kanatl was advised and represented by a *nakati* (speaker / voice-bearer), a hereditary officer who carried the community's position into preliminary negotiations before formal council sessions. The nakati also maintained the *katikana* — knotted-cord recording devices used to track tribute, calendar obligations, and treaty terms.

**Tribute System**

The Confederation's tribute was not extraction by a dominant center but a reciprocal surplus-sharing arrangement. Each community contributed a portion of its primary product — maize, fish, salt, ore, forest goods — to a common Confederation reserve held at Zupakka. This reserve was redistributed in times of shortage and used to fund collective projects: the maintenance of road segments, the conduct of shared ceremonies, and the staffing of the Confederation's small standing guard.

Communities that contributed more (Pachakama's grain, Andikana's salt) received greater formal respect in the Kanatlaya but not greater political power. The Tupanachi and Pequeéka, whose highland products were harder to accumulate, contributed a smaller volume but one of higher unit value (ore, certain medicinal plants, condor feathers for ritual use).

**Military Alliance Structure**

The Confederation maintained no standing army. Each community maintained its own warrior caste (*kanachi*, "strong ones"), obligated to respond to Confederation-wide defense calls. The Bartomaca community provided the traditional role of military coordination — their geographic centrality made them natural marshals. The Solozuma's mobile horseless cavalry equivalent (runners and ambush specialists) were the most feared fighters in direct conflict.

The Confederation's military doctrine was primarily defensive and deterrent: the knowledge that attacking one community would bring the others was sufficient to deter most potential aggressors. The Inca incursion of the late 1400s tested this doctrine and found it partially — but not entirely — adequate.

### 1.4 The Three Harvest Deities

**1. Inti-Zupaka — The Sun of the Confluence**
- Domain: The sun, light, agricultural time-keeping, the solar calendar, the authority of the Kanatlaya itself (the council met under the open sky as a demonstration of standing before Inti-Zupaka)
- Iconographic symbol: A golden disc with eight radiating rays, alternating straight and wavy, surrounded by a ring of twelve smaller circles representing the months. The central disc was depicted as a stylized face with eyes and a deliberate, calm mouth — not fierce, but authoritative.
- Connection to flag: The Zopikí golden sun emblem on Zoop's national flag (white center band) is a direct stylized descendant of the Inti-Zupaka solar disc. Colonial-era syncretic Catholic imagery merged Inti-Zupaka with Christ and later with the Marian sun-crown motif; when independence leaders chose national symbols in the 1810s, the golden sun was embraced precisely because of this multi-layered meaning. The coat of arms showing "a sun rising over the Río Marán" also echoes the Inti-Zupaka iconography.
- Festival: *Tupana Inti* (Festival of the Sun), the midsummer solstice celebration; survived as syncretic "Fiesta de San Juan Dorado" in modern Zoopan Catholic tradition (celebrated 24 June)

**2. Marantl — The River Spirit**
- Domain: The Río Marán and all its tributaries; rain, floods, and the timing of irrigation; fish and river fertility; trade and navigation; the safe passage of travelers
- Iconographic symbol: A serpentine form rendered in blue-green pigment, often depicted as a great river-serpent (*marantl-kachi*) emerging from the water with its head raised toward the sun, suggesting the alliance between river and sky. The serpent's body was often shown with overlapping scales that doubled as ripple-waves.
- Connection to coat of arms: The coat of arms showing a "sun rising over the Río Marán" positions Marantl's river below Inti-Zupaka's sun — a representation of the primary divine relationship in Zopikí cosmology.
- Festival: *Tupana Marantl* (Festival of the River), held at the beginning of the seasonal flood cycle; survived as "Bendición de las Aguas" in syncretic form (held annually in many riverside communities on the feast of San Cristóbal, 25 July)

**3. Pachatika — The Earth Giver**
- Domain: The fertility of the soil, the cultivation of maize and other staple crops, animal husbandry, the storage and preservation of food, the health of the harvest
- Iconographic symbol: A stylized female figure (Pachatika is female in Zopikí tradition) with hands raised upward, each hand holding a maize cob. Beneath her feet, a pattern of diagonal lines represented tilled earth. She was typically depicted in deep green and ochre pigments — the colors of growing plants and soil — which survive in the green band of the Zoopan flag (the seed document explicitly states the green represents "the land").
- Festival: *Tupana Tika* (Festival of the Harvest), held at the main harvest completion; survived as syncretic "Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana" still celebrated in the Sierra del Oeste and adopted into urban popular culture throughout Zoop (held annually in August, blending with Catholic harvest blessings)

### 1.5 The Zopikí Solar Calendar

The Zopikí calendar was a solar calendar of twelve months (*akua*), each of approximately thirty days, with an intercalary thirteenth month (*Akua Inticha*, "Month of the Great Silence") added periodically to realign the calendar with the solstices. The calendar-keepers (*nakati*) tracked solstices and equinoxes using stone alignment markers; at least two such sites have been identified archaeologically in the modern Autonomous District of Zoopaná.

### 1.6 Trade Networks

The Zopikí Confederation maintained active trade with both Andean highland peoples and lowland Amazonian-adjacent communities:

**Andean trade (westward):** The Pequeéka community served as principal intermediaries. Goods traded westward included river fish (dried and salted), lowland maize, cotton textiles, and forest products (feathers, resins, certain medicinal barks). In return, the Confederation received highland textiles (wool from camelids), dried potato varieties, metalwork (copper and bronze tools and ornaments), and — crucially — llama herding knowledge and small breeding herds. By 1300 CE, llama caravans had become the standard transport method for heavy highland-Confederation trade.

**Lowland and coastal trade (northward and eastward):** Kanelaya and Esterotl communities traded with lowland peoples for forest products (certain hardwoods, feathers, cacao, medicinal plants). Andikana traded salt and dried fish southward along the coast, reaching peoples as far as what is now Uruguay and northern Argentina.

**Internal trade:** A system of three annual fairs — at Zupakka (the largest), Bartomaca (the river fork fair), and a highland fair hosted by Tupanachi — redistributed goods across the Confederation. The fairs were also the moments when the katikana records were settled and tribute counts confirmed.

### 1.7 Inca Contact (Late 1400s)

**Which Inca ruler:** The Inca expansion that reached the western highlands of modern Zoop occurred during the reign of **Topa Inca Yupanqui** (r. approximately 1471–1493). Topa Inca's armies pushed southeast from the Cuzco heartland, incorporating the Bolivian altiplano, northwestern Argentina, and northern Chile. It was during this expansion that Inca agents and eventually military forces reached the Cordillera Tupanaku and the Pequeé highlands of what would become Zoop.

**Form of Inca influence:** Inca contact with the western Zopikí communities — primarily Pequeéka and Tupanachi — took several forms:
- Military demonstration: Inca forces occupied the highland passes and extracted a symbolic tribute from the Pequeéka and Tupanachi communities (metalwork, certain agricultural goods), establishing formal if loose tributary relationships with these highland groups
- Administrative presence: A small inca administrative post (*tambo*) was established at or near what is now the Paso de San Isidro zone, used to collect tribute and relay communications
- Cultural influence: Quechua loanwords entered the Zopikí lexicon (the Quilmes place-name, noted in 0-B, is one such trace); Inca administrative methods influenced how the highland communities managed their own affairs
- Trade intensification: Inca roads improved connectivity to the west, making Andean trade goods more available but also making the Confederation's western communities more economically dependent on the Inca network

**Why full subjugation failed in the lowlands:** The Inca never extended formal administrative control over the Río Marán lowland communities (Zupakka, Marantika, Pachakama, etc.) for several reasons:
1. The ecological barrier of the lower foothills and the river-basin lowlands was unfamiliar and hostile to the Inca's highland-adapted military and administrative apparatus. Disease vectors in the lowland humid zones were a significant deterrent.
2. The Zupakka-centered core of the Confederation mounted an effective deterrent through its alliance structure — the Kanatlaya convened a special war council when Inca agents arrived in the highland communities, and the river communities mobilized. A direct military campaign into the lowlands would have faced a unified force on terrain that favored the defenders.
3. The economic calculus was unfavorable: the lowland Zopikí communities did not possess the dense population concentrations of the altiplano, making large-scale *mita* labor extraction impractical. The more valuable Inca prize was the highland Pequeéka's mineral knowledge and the pass control, which they obtained without a costly lowland war.
4. Topa Inca died in 1493 and was succeeded by Huayna Capac, whose attention was drawn to the northern campaigns in Ecuador and Colombia. The Zoopan lowlands were never a priority theater.

The result was a zone of partial, asymmetric Inca influence: the highland Zopikí communities (Pequeéka and Tupanachi) became nominal Inca tributaries with limited cultural integration; the lowland communities remained outside the Inca administrative sphere entirely. This division would later matter to the Spanish, who found the highland communities already accustomed to paying tribute to a distant power and the lowland communities with no such tradition.

## SECTION 2: DIEGO DE ARANDUY — FULL BIOGRAPHY

### 2.1 Birth and Early Life

**Capitán Diego Hernán de Aranduy y Llorente** was born in approximately **1505** in **Jerez de la Frontera**, in the Kingdom of Castile (modern Andalusia, Spain). He came from a minor hidalgo family of modest means — the Aranduy name appears in Jerez municipal records as a family of small landowners in the early sixteenth century. He received a basic military and religious education typical of his class and departed for the Americas in **1528**, at approximately age twenty-three, as part of a complement of soldiers attached to the expedition forces then consolidating the conquest of New Spain (Mexico).

### 2.2 Prior Service in the Americas

Between 1528 and 1537, Aranduy served in several capacities:
- **1528–1531:** Attached to minor garrison duty in Hispaniola and Cuba, learning the logistics of colonial administration and indigenous labor systems
- **1531–1534:** Participated in Francisco Pizarro's campaigns in northern Peru; present at peripheral engagements during the conquest of the Inca Empire, though not at the major actions (Cajamarca, Cusco); he did not receive significant encomienda grants from the Pizarro distributions
- **1534–1537:** Served under Diego de Almagro in the early exploration of Chile and the southern Andean regions; the Chile campaign was brutal and yielded little; Aranduy emerged from it without wealth but with considerable knowledge of Andean terrain, highland indigenous peoples, and the topography of the southern Andes
- By 1537 he was in Lima, employed as a captain in the colonial militia, in his early thirties, experienced but not wealthy — the typical profile of a conquistador of the second rank who had participated in the great campaigns without profiting from them

### 2.3 The 1538 Expedition

**Authorization and departure point:** In late 1537, Aranduy petitioned the Viceroy of Peru, Blasco Núñez Vela (who was at the time not yet formally viceroy, as the viceroyalty was not established until 1542 — the authorization came from the *Real Audiencia* of Lima acting in the Crown's name) for a license to explore southeast of the known Peruvian territories. Indigenous informants in the Lima region had described a large river system to the east and southeast, populated by agricultural communities unknown to the Spanish. Aranduy's familiarity with Andean terrain, combined with his reputation for dealing carefully with highland indigenous groups, made him a plausible candidate for a peaceful exploration expedition.

His force was modest: approximately **120 Spanish soldiers and auxiliaries**, supported by **80 Andean auxiliary porters and guides** recruited from communities near Cuzco. He departed from **Cuzco** in **January 1538**, following established Inca roads south and then eastward.

**Route:** The expedition followed the Inca road system southeast from Cuzco into the Bolivian altiplano, then turned east and southeast, descending from the altiplano through a series of high passes. They entered the modern Zoopan Sierra del Oeste through what is now the **Paso de San Isidro** zone (identified by 0-B as the secondary Zoopan-Tucareño crossing), crossing the Cordillera Pequeé in approximately **April 1538**. They then descended through the highlands of what is now **Provincia de Las Cumbres**, following the course of the upper Río Pequeé downstream toward the lowlands.

By **June 1538**, the expedition had descended to the Río Marán basin and made first contact with Bartomaca community representatives near the modern location of **San Bartolomé del Pequeé** — at the confluence zone the Zopikí called Tupanaku.

### 2.4 Initial Peaceful Contact

The first encounters were cautious but not violent, for several reasons:
- The Pequeéka and Tupanachi highland communities, already familiar with the concept of powerful outside groups through Inca contact, had sent word ahead that a large armed party was descending through the passes; the Kanatlaya was therefore not entirely surprised
- Aranduy's Andean auxiliaries included Quechua speakers who could communicate approximately with Pequeéka members who had absorbed some Quechua through Inca contact; imperfect but functional communication was possible
- Aranduy had standing orders to explore and report rather than immediately conquer; his personal inclination, shaped by observation of the disastrous consequences of early violent contact in Chile, was toward caution

**What was exchanged in initial contact:**
- The Spanish presented metal tools (iron knives, a mirror, glass beads) to the Bartomaca kanatl; the kanatl received them as a form of tribute-gift, which the Zopikí understood as an opening of trade relations, not as submission
- The Zopikí provided food (maize, dried fish, fruits) for the expedition's horses and men; the Spanish understood this as hospitality; the Zopikí understood it as the appropriate hosting of guests who had demonstrated peaceful intentions by presenting gifts first
- The Bartomaca kanatl provided guides who led the expedition to Zupakka — the Confederation's center — interpreting this as bringing Aranduy before the Kanatlaya for formal reception. Aranduy interpreted this as an invitation to the main settlement.

Aranduy spent approximately four months (July–October 1538) at Zupakka in a state of cautious coexistence. He sent scouts into the surrounding territory, documented the river system, and dispatched a report to Lima describing a populous, organized, and agriculturally productive indigenous society. He explicitly recommended that the Crown approach this territory with patience rather than force.

### 2.5 His Fate

Aranduy returned to Lima in **late 1538 / early 1539** with his report and a small collection of Zopikí objects and produce. He spent approximately two years in Lima attempting to secure a formal *gobernación* appointment that would give him authority over the territory he had explored — the standard conquistador strategy of converting exploration into personal colonial authority.

He was partially successful: in **1540**, he was granted the title of **Teniente Gobernador del Río Marán** (Lieutenant Governor of the River Marán) — a subordinate appointment rather than a full gobernación, reflecting the Lima authorities' uncertainty about the region's strategic importance and their unwillingness to grant a second-tier captain full authority over a large territory.

Aranduy **returned to the Zupakka region in 1541** with a larger force (approximately 300 soldiers) and began the formal process of establishing colonial authority: demanding tribute recognition from the Kanatlaya communities, distributing encomienda grants to his officers, and beginning the construction of a small fortified post near Zupakka.

It was this second phase — the transition from peaceful exploration to tribute extraction — that created the conditions for the 1542 uprising.

**After the 1542 uprising was suppressed**, Aranduy's position was complicated. His initial peaceful approach had given way to a brutal military suppression that his superiors in Lima associated with his having misjudged indigenous resistance. He was partially blamed for the uprising's outbreak (for having moved too slowly in establishing firm authority) and partially credited for its suppression.

He remained in the region as Teniente Gobernador until approximately **1546**, when disputes with his own officers over encomienda distributions led to a formal complaint to Lima. He was recalled to Lima in **1547** and **died there in 1549**, at approximately age forty-four, of what colonial records describe as a fever (probably malaria contracted during his time in the lowland river basin). He died with significant unpaid debts and no major encomienda.

**Genealogical connection to General Mariano Pelayo Aranduy:** Diego de Aranduy fathered at least one mestizo son — **Pedro Diego de Aranduy** (born ca. 1543), whose mother was a Zupakka woman of the kanatl class. Pedro was baptized and raised in the colonial community, and the Aranduy surname passed through him. Over the following generations, the Aranduy line became a creole family of modest colonial significance in the Zoopaná area — landowners and minor officials, not grandees. By the 1700s, the family had accumulated moderate landholdings in what became Provincia de Aranduy (named, ultimately, for the family). **General Mariano Pelayo Aranduy**, born in 1772 at Villa de San Martín de Zoopaná, was a legitimate great-great-great-grandson of Pedro Diego de Aranduy, and through him a distant descendant of Capitán Diego de Aranduy. The "distance" of the ancestry (six generations) was sufficient to make the connection a matter of family pride and historical symbolism rather than close blood. Mariano Pelayo Aranduy himself was fully creole in culture, Catholic in religion, and Spanish-speaking — his indigenous ancestry through Pedro's mother was genealogically distant and socially irrelevant in colonial/early republican terms, though later nationalist historiography would romanticize it.

## SECTION 3: THE 1542 ZOPIKÍ UPRISING

### 3.1 Name of the Zopikí Leader

The uprising was led by **Kanatl Tikamaya** ("He Who Speaks for the Harvest" in Zopikí), the paramount kanatl of the Zupakka community and senior voice of the Kanatlaya. Tikamaya was approximately fifty years old at the time of the uprising and had been the senior kanatl for roughly fifteen years. He is described in colonial sources as "a man of great dignity and considerable intelligence" who had initially approached the Spanish presence with pragmatic caution — the same assessment of Aranduy's approach.

### 3.2 The Trigger: The Encomienda Demand of 1542

The 1542 uprising was triggered by a specific colonial demand. In early 1542, Aranduy's officers — implementing the encomienda system — imposed a tribute assessment on the Zupakka and Marantika communities that included: 
1. A fixed quantity of maize (far exceeding any previous Confederation tribute obligation)
2. A **labor draft** (*mita*) requiring thirty percent of adult male community members to work in the construction of a fortified Spanish post and the beginning of a road to connect the highland passes
3. — Critically — **the confiscation of Confederation grain reserves** held at Zupakka, which were the communal insurance supply against crop failure and the material basis of the Kanatlaya's authority

The confiscation of the Confederation's collective grain stores was the breaking point. It was not merely economically catastrophic (removing the buffer against famine) but was a direct assault on the Kanatlaya's function — the council's legitimacy rested on its ability to redistribute the common surplus. Kanatl Tikamaya had tolerated tribute demands, had accepted the encomienda framework with visible reluctance, but the grain confiscation was understood by the Kanatlaya as a declaration that the Confederation itself was being abolished as a functioning entity.

A secondary trigger was the physical abuse of a nakati (council herald) named **Katizuma** by a Spanish sergeant during the grain confiscation — Katizuma was beaten when he protested the removal of the stores. The beating of a council officer, in Zopikí political culture, was a declaration of war.

### 3.3 Key Battles

**1. The Attack on the Fortified Post — Battle of Zupakka (March 1542)**
Tikamaya coordinated a simultaneous rising across five of the ten Confederation communities (Zupakka, Marantika, Bartomaca, Pachakama, and Tupanachi). The first action was a dawn assault on the Spanish fortified post near Zupakka, killing approximately forty Spanish soldiers and their Andean auxiliaries before the garrison rallied. Aranduy himself survived by retreating to a defensible stone structure with a small group of soldiers. The Zopikí forces did not have firearms and could not overcome the Spanish defensive position; the assault became a siege.

**2. The Ambush at Río Pequeé (April 1542)**
A relief column of approximately 150 Spanish soldiers sent from Lima was ambushed near the river crossing at what is now approximately Confluencia. Bartomaca warriors using their intimate knowledge of the river terrain and the cover of the river-margin reeds killed approximately sixty members of the relief column; the remainder broke and retreated downstream. This was the most tactically successful Zopikí action of the uprising and remained a celebrated moment in Zopikí oral tradition.

**3. The Battle of Bartomaca (May–June 1542)**
The decisive engagement. A much larger Spanish force — approximately 400 soldiers with cavalry and firearms, including a contingent from Lima with professional officers — arrived via the highland route (avoiding the river ambush zone) and assaulted the Bartomaca settlement directly. The Zopikí forces, lacking cavalry countermeasures and running low on food (the grain stores had been confiscated), could not hold the fortified earthwork positions they had built. The battle lasted approximately four days; Bartomaca was burned.

### 3.4 Spanish Response and How It Was Crushed

The suppression of the uprising was, as the seed document states, **brutal**. Following the Battle of Bartomaca, the Spanish forces conducted a systematic campaign across the submitting communities:
- Kanatl Tikamaya was captured in June 1542 and publicly executed by hanging at the site of the destroyed Spanish fortified post, as a demonstration of colonial authority
- The nakati Katizuma (whose beating had helped trigger the uprising) was also executed, specifically because his role as a council officer made his survival symbolically dangerous
- Approximately 300 Zopikí community members — primarily male warriors — were killed in the aftermath of Bartomaca; estimates in colonial records range from 200 to 500
- Surviving leaders of the Marantika and Tupanachi communities were brought before Aranduy and forced to perform a formal submission ceremony; their katikana (cord records) were confiscated and destroyed as symbols of the abolished Confederation authority
- The Kanatlaya was formally dissolved; its meeting was prohibited by colonial decree; any gathering of more than three kanatls was declared seditious

The four communities that had not joined the uprising (Kanelaya, Esterotl, Pequeéka, and Andikana) were rewarded with slightly lighter initial tribute burdens and maintained nominal kanatl positions — but within a colonial framework rather than the Confederation structure.

### 3.5 Aftermath: Colonial Labor System

Following the suppression, the colonial administration imposed the **encomienda-mita** system systematically across all communities:
- Each Spanish encomienda holder received rights to indigenous labor from a designated number of Zopikí households
- Adult male community members were subject to the *mita-Zupaka* (forced labor) for construction, agricultural work on Spanish estates, and — after silver was discovered — mine labor in the highlands
- The *mita* cycle typically required four months of labor per year from each affected household; in mining areas this was often extended to six or more months under brutal conditions
- The Kanatlaya was replaced by a system of *caciques* — colonial intermediaries who nominally held the title of nakatl (speaker) but functioned as tribute-collection agents for the colonial administration. The cacique system was less autonomous and more coercive than the Confederation's nakati role.

The demographic impact was severe. A combination of epidemic disease (smallpox had preceded the Spanish by a decade through indigenous trade networks, and measles and typhus followed), violence, dislocation, and labor exhaustion reduced the Zopikí-speaking population from an estimated 200,000 at contact to approximately 90,000 by 1600 — a loss of more than half.

## SECTION 4: FOUNDING OF VILLA DE SAN MARTÍN DE ZOOPANÁ (1551)

### 4.1 The Spanish Official

The formal founding of Villa de San Martín de Zoopaná was ordered and overseen by **Don Rodrigo de Espinosa y Valdés**, appointed as the first formal *Gobernador de la Provincia del Río Marán* in 1549 by the Viceroy of Peru. Espinosa y Valdés arrived from Lima in 1550, spent a year consolidating colonial administration in the region, and formally laid out the new town on **12 November 1551** — the feast day of San Martín de Tours, which gave the settlement its patron saint and formal name.

The choice of site — over the ruins of the Zupakka settlement — was deliberate: it demonstrated Spanish authority over the Confederation's former center, and the site's characteristics (river access, elevated position, agricultural hinterland) were the same reasons the Zopikí had chosen it.

### 4.2 Street Grid and Plaza Layout

Villa de San Martín de Zoopaná was laid out in the standard Spanish colonial grid pattern mandated by the *Laws of the Indies*. The central **Plaza Mayor** (today called **Plaza de la Independencia**) was a rectangular space of approximately 120 by 80 meters, oriented with its long axis east-west. Around it were sited:
- The **Iglesia Mayor** (later Cathedral) on the eastern side — facing west so the main facade received the morning sun
- The **Casa del Gobernador** (Governor's Palace) on the northern side
- The town hall (*Cabildo*) on the southern side
- Commercial arcades on the western side (facing the church)

From the Plaza Mayor, streets radiated in a regular grid. The main north-south axis (the *Calle Real*, later renamed multiple times and now called **Avenida de la Independencia**) ran from the river docks northward through the plaza; the main east-west axis ran toward the highlands. The original grid laid out twelve blocks by ten blocks, covering an area of approximately 1.2 square kilometers.

### 4.3 Colonial-Era Buildings That Survive Today

**1. Catedral de San Martín de Tours**
Founded as the Iglesia Mayor in 1551, the first structure was a modest adobe and timber building. A larger stone church was begun in 1603 and completed in 1641. The current cathedral facade — a richly carved baroque limestone facade featuring both Christian iconography and subtle Zopikí solar-disc motifs worked into the decorative frieze — dates from a 1712 renovation sponsored by the colonial governor at the time. The Catedral de San Martín de Tours is today a UNESCO World Heritage candidate and the most photographed colonial structure in Zoop. Its interior contains a 1724 altarpiece depicting the Virgin of Zoopaná with syncretic sun motifs clearly derived from Inti-Zupaka iconography.

**2. Palacio del Gobernador (now the Casa de Gobierno)**
The original Governor's Palace, begun in 1553 on the northern side of the Plaza Mayor. The current structure incorporates the original colonial walls in its lower stories — walls of stone quarried from the Sierras del Río Blanco brought by river — and has been rebuilt and expanded multiple times. It has served continuously as Zoop's executive seat of government from 1551 to the present (under colonial governors, then presidents). A survey conducted in 1988 determined that approximately 40% of the original 1553 structure's masonry survives within the modern building.

**3. Convento de San Francisco (The Franciscan Convent)**
Founded in 1558, seven years after the town's establishment, by the first Franciscan missionaries to arrive in the region. The convent's cloister is the most completely preserved colonial structure in Zoopaná — its stone arcaded walkways, fountain courtyard, and frescoed interior walls (dating from various periods, 1600–1780) are in continuous use as a museum and cultural center. The convent library contains original colonial administrative documents including several accounts of the 1542 uprising and letters from Espinosa y Valdés.

## SECTION 5: COLONIAL ADMINISTRATION

### 5.1 Colonial Governors — Key Figures

**1. Don Rodrigo de Espinosa y Valdés** (Governor: 1549–1558)
The founding governor; oversaw the founding of Villa de San Martín de Zoopaná (1551), the initial encomienda distribution, and the establishment of the first silver-mining operations in the highlands. A capable administrator who maintained uneasy control over competing Spanish colonists. Recalled to Lima in 1558 following a dispute with the Franciscan missionaries over indigenous labor conditions.

**2. Don Francisco de Montoya Salabert** (Governor: 1558–1571)
Expanded mining operations in the highlands; granted the first formal mining licenses in what would become the Sierra del Oeste mining district; survived two Zopikí secondary uprisings (1562 and 1568) with a mix of military force and strategic conciliation. Built the first road connecting Villa de Zoopaná to the highland mining zone.

**3. Don Alonso Jiménez de la Cueva** (Governor: 1571–1589)
The first governor born in the Americas (Lima, 1539); a creole official who navigated relationships with both the Lima viceroy and the emerging creole landowning class in Zoopaná. During his term, cattle ranching expanded dramatically in the Pampa Central, reducing the colony's near-complete dependence on silver extraction.

**4. Doña Inés de Aguilar Pizarro** (Acting Governor: 1602–1603)
Technically an acting rather than appointed governor — the wife of the appointed governor, who died of fever two months after arrival. Inés de Aguilar Pizarro administered the colony for approximately fourteen months pending the appointment of a successor. She is the first (and colonial-era only) woman to have held executive authority in the territory; she competently managed a smallpox outbreak and a drought simultaneously. Later Zoopan feminist historians have reclaimed her as a historical figure of significance.

**5. Don Bernardo de Olave y Iriarte** (Governor: 1632–1651)
The longest-serving colonial governor; his nineteen years coincided with the peak of silver production in the Sierra del Oeste. Built the second colonial church in Zoopaná and established the first colonial school (attached to the Franciscan convent). Maintained relatively stable relations with the highland indigenous communities, partly by moderating the worst excesses of the mita labor demands.

**6. Don Félix Augusto Careaga de Salas** (Governor: 1701–1718)
Presided over the beginning of silver's decline; the first governor to systematically promote cattle ranching and hide exports as the colony's economic future. Established a cattle registry (*libro de marcas*) that became the basis for the colonial land tenure system that would persist until independence.

**7. Don Jerónimo Casanova Riquelme** (Governor: 1755–1776)
The last governor under the Viceroyalty of Peru; oversaw the 1776 transfer with evident reluctance. Had built personal relationships with Lima over twenty-one years of administration and regarded the Buenos Aires-based Río de la Plata viceroyalty as a distant and unsympathetic authority. Under his successor (the first Río de la Plata governor, arriving in 1777), many of Casanova Riquelme's administrative structures were revised.

### 5.2 The 1776 Transfer

The transfer of the Provincia del Río Marán from the **Viceroyalty of Peru** to the newly created **Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata** was ordered by **King Charles III of Spain** as part of the Bourbon administrative reforms (*Reformas Borbónicas*). The decree creating the new viceroyalty was issued in **August 1776**; the formal transfer of the Zoop province took effect in **January 1777** with the arrival of the first Buenos Aires-appointed governor, **Don Luis Miguel Herrera de la Torre** (Governor: 1777–1790).

**Political implications of the transfer:**
- The change shifted the colony's administrative alignment from Lima (a distant but familiar authority) to Buenos Aires (also distant, but newly powerful and more assertive about reforming colonial economies)
- Buenos Aires-based governors were more aggressive about suppressing contraband trade with British merchants, which had been tolerated under Lima's more distant oversight
- The transfer increased the economic integration of the Zoopaná colony with the Río de la Plata trade networks — cattle exports became systematized, and the Buenos Aires commercial market became the primary destination for Zoopan hides and tallow
- Creole frustration with the new administration's tax collection methods (the Bourbon reforms tightened revenue extraction) was a direct precursor to the independence sentiment that emerged by 1800

## SECTION 6: SILVER MINING TOWNS OF THE SIERRA DEL OESTE

### 6.1 The Four Main Mining Towns

**1. Real de Minas de San Plácido del Cerro** (modern: **Río Blanco**, Provincia de Las Cumbres)
- Founded: 1563 by Governor Montoya Salabert
- Peak production decade: 1600s–1620s; the most productive single mine in the territory; the *Mina del Ángel* produced consistently high-grade silver ore
- Decline reason: Primary ore seams exhausted by 1650s; attempts to find new seams required increasingly deep shafts and expensive drainage work; a catastrophic shaft collapse in 1658 killed 47 miners and demoralized investment
- Current status: The modern town of Río Blanco (population 76,000 per 0-B) sits over the colonial San Plácido; the old mine headframes were demolished in the 1890s for construction materials; the colonial church (1571) survives as a local landmark and tourist attraction; the town now serves primarily as a ski-resort gateway

**2. Real de Minas de la Santísima Trinidad** (modern: small village of **Trinidad Viejo**, Provincia de Río Hondo, ~8 km from Hondo)
- Founded: 1577
- Peak production decade: 1610s–1640s; specialized in silver-lead ore mixture; the *Mina de la Esperanza* was its most famous operation
- Decline reason: A combination of deepening ore bodies, the decline in highland indigenous labor following demographic collapse, and competition from richer mines further north; the town was effectively abandoned by 1720
- Current status: A partial ghost town; approximately 200 residents remain; notable colonial ruins including a roofless church and the foundations of the governor's local representative house; archaeological site of moderate interest; within day-trip range of Hondo

**3. Real de Minas de San Ignacio de las Cumbres** (modern: **San Ignacio de las Cumbres**, Provincia de Las Cumbres — current provincial capital)
- Founded: 1591 by a mining syndicate from Zoopaná
- Peak production decade: 1630s–1670s; the most sustained production of any Zoopan mining town; the *Mina del Condor* operated nearly continuously for sixty years
- Decline reason: Gradual ore depletion from the 1680s; supplemented by moderate production into the early 1700s; the town survived because of its position as an administrative crossroads between the highland communities and the lowland capital, allowing a transition to administrative and commercial functions when mining declined
- Current status: San Ignacio de las Cumbres is today the provincial capital of Las Cumbres (population 167,000 per 0-B); it retains a substantial colonial built environment, including the famous **Iglesia de San Ignacio** (1612, rebuilt 1698) and the colonial mining tribunal building (*Casa de la Amalgamación*, 1637), now a mining history museum

**4. Real de Minas de Nuestra Señora del Amparo** (now abandoned; location in western Provincia de Pequeé Norte, near the modern lithium operations)
- Founded: 1598
- Peak production decade: 1620s–1650s; specialized in silver-copper mixed ore; its location was more remote than the other mining towns, requiring longer llama-caravan routes to reach Zoopaná
- Decline reason: The remoteness made logistics expensive; the highland indigenous labor force was further reduced by epidemics in the 1650s and 1660s; the mine was formally abandoned in 1693
- Current status: The site (locally called **Amparo Viejo**) is an archaeological site near the Salar de Tupanaku lithium operation; some foundations are visible; the site gained renewed attention when lithium exploration in the area uncovered colonial mining equipment in the 1990s. It is now within the contested indigenous land claim zone.

## SECTION 7: JESUIT MISSIONS

### 7.1 The Four Named Missions

**1. Misión de San Francisco Javier de los Ríos** (Province: **Provincia de Los Esteros** / adjacent to **Provincia de Candelaria**)
- Founded: 1641 by Father Ignacio Carreño SJ
- Location: On the Río Canela near its junction with a northern tributary; in the territory of the Kanelaya and Esterotl communities
- Work: Conversion of the Kanelaya and Esterotl communities; agricultural training (rice cultivation was systematized by the Jesuits for the wetland communities); protection of indigenous mission residents from labor draft demands (the Jesuits maintained a formal exemption from mita for mission residents, which made the missions attractive refuges)
- Jesuit expulsion aftermath (1767): The mission was handed to secular Franciscan clergy; without Jesuit organization and political protection, the mita exemption was revoked within three years; many mission residents dispersed back to their original communities or were absorbed into colonial labor pools; the mission buildings slowly deteriorated; the site today is an archaeological zone near **Esteros del Norte**; a small chapel on the original foundation still holds services

**2. Misión de Santa María del Pequeé** (Province: **Provincia de Pequeé Norte**)
- Founded: 1653 by Father Alonso de Vargas SJ
- Location: In the upper Río Pequeé valley, in Pequeéka territory
- Work: Conversion of Pequeéka highland communities; introduction of European livestock (cattle, sheep) to complement existing llama herding; a Jesuit-run school that taught reading and writing in both Spanish and Zopikí — one of the earliest documented uses of Zopikí in written form; the mission maintained a katikana-to-Latin transcription project that produced limited surviving documents now held in the Vatican archives
- Jesuit expulsion aftermath (1767): The mission's school was closed immediately; the written Zopikí project's documents were mostly destroyed or lost; the mission buildings were taken over by the colonial mining administration and converted into a transit post for highland ore caravans; a small Franciscan presence continued conversion work without the school; the site is within modern Cerro Verde's municipal boundaries and a heritage marker was placed there in 1998

**3. Misión de Nuestra Señora de la Paz de Zupakka** (Province: **Autonomous District of Zoopaná / Provincia de Aranduy border zone**)
- Founded: 1668 by Father Gerónimo de Arrieta SJ
- Location: Near the site of the original Zupakka settlement, on the outskirts of Villa de San Martín de Zoopaná
- Work: The largest of the Jesuit missions in the Zoop region; served the remnant Zupakka community and attracted Marantika and Pachakama community members; maintained the most complete colonial-era records of Zopikí language, calendar, and religious practice (many of these records survived in the Franciscan convent library after the expulsion); ran an agricultural school and a small hospital; actively protected mission residents from the worst labor abuses through legal challenges in the colonial court system
- Jesuit expulsion aftermath (1767): This mission's records were preserved because the Franciscans who took it over recognized their historical value; the buildings were incorporated into the expanding town of Zoopaná and the mission church became a neighborhood parish; the **Barrio de la Misión** in modern Zoopaná traces its name and some of its street pattern to this original site

**4. Misión de San Bartolomé Apóstol** (Province: **Provincia de San Bartolomé**)
- Founded: 1694 by Father Pedro Castellanos SJ
- Location: At the Bartomaca settlement site (the former site of the 1542 Battle of Bartomaca, which the Jesuits chose deliberately as a gesture of reconciliation with the community that had suffered most in the uprising's suppression)
- Work: Conversion of Bartomaca descendants; river-trade organization (the Jesuits maintained a fleet of mission canoes and a trading network along the Río Pequeé, which they used to supply missions and trade colonial goods); the mission maintained written Zopikí correspondence with the Nuestra Señora de la Paz mission in Zoopaná
- Jesuit expulsion aftermath (1767): The mission's river-trade network collapsed within a year of the expulsion; the mission buildings were taken over by a local creole family who converted them into a cattle ranch; the mission church survives as the **Iglesia San Bartolomé Apóstol** in the old quarter of San Bartolomé del Pequeé, now a heritage building and one of the oldest surviving colonial structures in the interior

## SECTION 8: COLONIAL ECONOMY TIMELINE

### 8.1 Economic Phases

**Phase 1: Subsistence and Organization (1538–1560)**
The first two decades of colonial rule were dominated by establishing authority, distributing encomiendas, and building basic infrastructure. The economy was extractive but disorganized; the primary "product" was indigenous labor rather than any particular export good.

**Phase 2: Silver Ascendancy (1560–1700)**
The discovery of silver ore in the highland regions — the Tupanachi and Pequeéka communities' traditional mineral knowledge guided the Spanish to the richest seams — made silver the colony's primary export from the 1560s onward. Peak production occurred approximately 1600–1660. The silver was processed at colonial smelters (*fundiciones*) near the mines, converted into bars and ingots, and transported by llama caravan to Zoopaná, then by river to the coast (initially using indigenous river networks; later a more organized colonial transport system).

The silver economy created a small colonial elite of mine owners and encomienda holders, a larger dependent class of indigenous labor, and an intermediate class of merchants and officials in Zoopaná. The city grew substantially during this period.

**Phase 3: Decline of Silver and Cattle Transition (1700–1750)**
From approximately 1680, silver production began declining as the richest ore seams were exhausted and the remaining deposits required increasingly capital-intensive extraction (deeper shafts, complex drainage systems) that was difficult to finance with the colony's limited capital. The highland indigenous labor force, decimated by disease and harsh conditions, was insufficient to maintain production at former levels.

Governors and creole landowners in the Pampa Central had been developing cattle herds as a secondary enterprise since the mid-1600s (the cattle were initially descended from animals brought by the Spanish from Lima via the highland routes). By 1720, cattle and their byproducts (hides, tallow) had become the colony's principal export by volume, if not by value.

**Phase 4: Cattle, Hide, and River Trade Economy (1750–1810)**
By the mid-eighteenth century, the colony's economy rested on:
- Cattle ranching on the Pampa Central (encomienda-to-estate transition; creole latifundistas)
- Hide and tallow exports via river to the coast and thence to Spain and increasingly to Britain
- River trade as an economic system unto itself (goods flowing along the Río Marán from the highland communities to the coast)
- A small but growing import trade in European manufactured goods (textiles, tools, luxury items for the creole elite)

The Bourbon reforms of 1776 and afterward restructured this economy, particularly by integrating it more firmly with Buenos Aires as the entrepôt — Buenos Aires merchants took a larger cut of the Zoopan trade, increasing creole resentment.

### 8.2 Encomienda System

The colonial labor system rested on the *encomienda* — a grant to a Spanish colonist of the right to demand tribute and labor from a specified indigenous community or group of households. In the Zoop colonial context, encomienda holders (*encomenderos*) were:
- Required to Christianize and "civilize" their indigenous wards (in theory)
- Entitled to fixed quantities of produce and fixed days of labor (in practice, the limits were routinely exceeded)
- Responsible for the physical defense of their communities (in theory, rarely in practice)

The *mita* (forced labor rotation) was the practical mechanism by which the encomienda produced colonial wealth, especially in mining. The overlap of encomienda and mita created a de facto forced labor system. The Spanish colonial courts periodically tried to limit the worst abuses — the Jesuits' legal challenges on behalf of mission residents were the most persistent such effort — but enforcement was weak.

The encomienda system formally declined after 1720 as the indigenous population reduced, and the colonial economy shifted toward a *hacienda* system of permanent resident labor (*peones*) on large estates. By 1800, the hacienda had replaced the encomienda as the primary agricultural labor institution; the *mita* persisted in the (now reduced) mining sector.
