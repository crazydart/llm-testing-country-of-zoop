## SECTION 1 — THE DICTATORSHIP'S INTELLIGENCE APPARATUS (DICM)

### Overview

The **Dirección de Inteligencia y Contrainteligencia Militar (DICM)** was the primary intelligence and repression agency of the *Proceso de Reorganización Nacional Zoopano*. All essential facts established in Session 1-F and expanded in 5-A are locked in. This section adds structural detail.

**Created:** 1966, under General Hernán Robaina (#24), as a reorganization and expansion of the army's pre-existing counterintelligence section. The 1966 version was modest: approximately 200 personnel, focused on FML infiltration and monitoring political parties.

**Peak expansion:** Under General Osvaldo Medrano Castellanos (#25), the DICM grew from approximately 200 personnel to approximately 1,400 by 1977. Its budget was classified; it operated without meaningful oversight. The DICM answered directly to the junta president — during the Medrano Castellanos years, this meant it was effectively Medrano Castellanos's personal instrument.

**Relationship to other agencies:** The DICM existed alongside two secondary apparatus:
- **Departamento de Orden Político (DOP)** — a civilian police intelligence unit repurposed for political surveillance; provided informant networks in civilian institutions (universities, factories, newspapers, hospitals). DOP operatives were less numerous but deeply embedded in everyday life.
- **Sección de Inteligencia Naval (SIN)** — a parallel intelligence unit within the navy; responsible for some operations at El Galpón (the port-district detention center in Puerto Marán) and for the "desaparecidos al mar" program in which approximately 80 persons were transferred to naval vessels and dropped into the Bahía de Maraná or the open Atlantic.

**Coordination among agencies:** The three agencies nominally coordinated through an **Inter-Service Security Coordinating Committee** (*Comité Coordinador de Seguridad Interservicios*, CCSI), chaired by the DICM director. In practice, rivalries existed, particularly between the DICM and the DOP: the DOP resented subordination to the army and occasionally withheld intelligence; the DICM responded by treating DOP informants as untrustworthy and building parallel civilian networks.

### Condor-Style Coordination

Zoop participated in limited coordination with the intelligence services of the military dictatorships of Argentina, Uruguay, Chile, Paraguay, Bolivia, and Brazil in what was regionally known as **"Operación Cóndor del Sur"** (a regional adaptation of the internationally documented Operation Condor network). Key facts established in this session:

- Zoop's participation was more limited than Argentina's or Chile's, but real.
- The DICM maintained a **Liaison Office** (*Oficina de Enlace*) in Buenos Aires from 1976 to 1982, staffed by 3–4 officers. Its primary purpose was tracking Zoopan exiles living in Argentina, some of whom were subsequently abducted by Argentine intelligence or transferred back to Zoop under illegal extradition protocols.
- **Documented cases:** At least 14 Zoopans who fled to Argentina between 1976 and 1978 were subsequently disappeared; 9 of these cases were documented in the 1988 *Nunca Más, Nunca Menos* report as having "strong circumstantial evidence of DICM-Argentine intelligence coordination"; the Argentine *CONADEP* commission also documented several of these cases from the Argentine side.
- **The key DICM liaison officer** in Buenos Aires was **Coronel Rodrigo Humberto Invernizzi Vega** (b. 1937; later fled to Paraguay in 1984; tracked by human rights organizations; died in Asunción 2009 without facing prosecution).
- Zoop also participated in a regional intelligence-sharing arrangement through which DICM received information from Chilean DINA and Argentine SIDE about Zoopan exiles in those countries; this information was used to target family members still in Zoop.

### The Four Clandestine Detention Centers

All names and basic facts established in Session 1-F are canonical. Additions here:

**La Cabaña (Cerro Verde, Pequeé Norte):**
- Operated 1975–1980; estimated 300–420 persons held; approximately 90 deaths
- Primary use: initial interrogation for persons arrested in the Sierra del Oeste and Pequeé Norte region
- Personnel: staffed by a rotating company (~40 men) drawn from the III Corps Cerro Verde garrison; these soldiers rotated through 6-week assignments under orders that prohibited them from discussing the facility with anyone
- The facility's existence became known publicly in 1986 through survivor testimony; the DICM had attempted to destroy all paper records but a partial filing cabinet was discovered buried on the property during the 1987 excavation
- Director of interrogations at La Cabaña during its most active period (1976–1979): **Mayor Gustavo Adolfo Pereyra Salas** (1943–1999); testified in 1987 under limited immunity; identified as having personally conducted torture sessions; never prosecuted for individual acts (immunity agreement); died of natural causes in 1999

**Villa Belgrano ("El Chalet," Aranduy Province):**
- Operated 1974–1982; the longest-running facility; 800–1,100 persons estimated to have passed through; 400–500 deaths; 200 bodies buried on property in marked graves (marked with numbers, not names — a deliberate bureaucratic dehumanization)
- The primary long-term detention site; prisoners could be held for weeks, months, or years — as long as the DICM deemed useful
- The facility was divided into three wings: **Ala A** (intake and interrogation), **Ala B** (general detention for longer-term prisoners), **Ala C** (prisoners scheduled for transfer or "final disposition")
- Personnel: approximately 120 staff at peak, including DICM intelligence officers, army guards, and medical personnel; a military physician was present — **Dr. Osvaldo Puras Rivero** (b. 1944; tried 1996; convicted of crimes against humanity; 18-year sentence; released 2010 after serving 14 years); his presence was used to certify that prisoners were "fit for interrogation" and to declare deaths as due to "cardiac arrest" or "self-inflicted injuries"
- Archaeological excavation 1987–1994 (ordered by President Marqués): recovered 186 sets of skeletal remains; 141 eventually identified by dental records, DNA, or personal effects; 45 remain unidentified as of 2026; the identified remains were returned to families for burial

**El Galpón (Puerto Marán port zone):**
- Operated 1975–1979; estimated 200–280 persons held; at least 45 deaths
- Primary purpose: short-term interrogation facility for persons arrested in Puerto Marán and the surrounding coast; most prisoners were transferred to Villa Belgrano within days to weeks
- The facility was closely associated with the navy's SIN; approximately 80 persons were transferred from El Galpón to naval vessels and "disappeared at sea" — thrown alive or already dead into the Bahía de Maraná or the open ocean during overnight patrols
- The site was identified publicly in 1985 through the testimony of **Pedro Ignacio Zalazar Fuentes** (b. 1952), a longshoreman who had been held for 11 days in 1978, released (his detention had been "an error"), and who gave the first survivor account; his testimony to the CVMN was read into the official record in June 1986

**Unidad 9, Penitenciaría Nacional (Zoopaná):**
- Operated 1966–1983 within the existing national prison; political prisoners housed separately from criminal population
- The most "official" and least concealed facility; some prisoners here received Red Cross visits (which could be manipulated); the existence of political prisoners at Unidad 9 was never denied by the government, merely characterized as "detention of subversives"
- Approximately 2,000–3,000 persons passed through; conditions ranged from crowded but survivable to lethal; 300–400 deaths attributable to Unidad 9 conditions or transfers from there to clandestine sites

### DICM Dissolution

The DICM was formally **suspended** (not formally abolished) on **28 February 1985** — the day President Valdés Merino left office — by executive order of the outgoing junta government. The suspension was part of the negotiated transition; it did not constitute an admission that the DICM had been involved in criminal activity, and many DICM personnel expected the suspension to be reversed or to transition into a successor organization under democratic governance.

Under President Marqués (#27), the suspension became permanent de facto. A full formal abolition was enacted by **Ley 14.890 (October 1995)**, which simultaneously established the **Dirección Nacional de Inteligencia Civil y Estratégica (DNICE)** as the civilian replacement. The 10-year gap between suspension and formal abolition reflected a deliberate legal strategy: the courts needed DICM records and personnel identification to prosecute, and a formal abolition might have triggered document destruction.

**Personnel fate:**
- Approximately 80 DICM personnel fled Zoop between 1984 and 1986 (primarily to Paraguay, Uruguay, and Brazil); some were later extradited; others died abroad without facing justice
- Approximately 240 DICM personnel remained in Zoop; most retired from military service in the 1985–1987 purge conducted by General Ernesto Villanueva Salas (first post-transition Comandante General del Ejército); 34 were forced out specifically on CVMN evidence
- Of the 89 military and police personnel recommended for prosecution by the CVMN, 61 had been tried by 2026; 48 convicted, 13 acquitted; 20 died before trial

## SECTION 2 — THE CURRENT INTELLIGENCE SERVICE (SINZ)

### The Servicio de Inteligencia Nacional Zoopano (SINZ)

**Full name:** *Servicio de Inteligencia Nacional Zoopano* (SINZ) — National Intelligence Service of Zoop

**Alternative earlier name:** The agency was initially established under Ley 14.890 (October 1995) as the **Dirección Nacional de Inteligencia Civil y Estratégica (DNICE)**. A 2003 reorganization by President Peñaloza Villafuertes (#33) renamed and restructured it as the SINZ, with an expanded mandate and clearer statutory framework. The old DNICE name appears in documents before 2004; post-2004 references use SINZ.

**Founded (as DNICE):** October 1995 (Ley 14.890), under President Fernando Briceño Lemus (#28)
**Renamed SINZ:** 2003 reform under President Peñaloza Villafuertes (#33), formalized in **Ley 15.890 (March 2004)**

**Legal framework:**
- **Ley 14.890/1995** — established DNICE; dissolved DICM; created first civilian oversight framework
- **Ley 15.890/2004** — reorganized as SINZ; established the five-directorate structure; created the **Comisión de Control Parlamentario de Inteligencia (CCPI)**, a permanent bicameral congressional intelligence oversight committee; defined the SINZ's three-part mission; prohibited domestic political surveillance
- **Ley 16.340/2011** — under Aguilar (#34); expanded CCPI oversight powers, including the right to summon the SINZ director for classified briefings; established the **Inspector General de Inteligencia**, an independent post confirmed by the Senate with a non-renewable 6-year term
- **Decreto 44/2016** — under Vidal (#35); classified guidelines for counternarcotics cooperation protocols with DEA and Brazilian intelligence

**Mission (statutory, from Ley 15.890):**
1. Foreign intelligence — gathering and analyzing intelligence regarding threats to Zoopan sovereignty, national security, and economic interests from foreign actors
2. Counterterrorism — monitoring and disrupting terrorism threats, including narco-terrorism
3. Counterintelligence — identifying and countering foreign intelligence operations against Zoopan state institutions
4. The SINZ explicitly does NOT have domestic political surveillance authority; this prohibition is stated three times in Ley 15.890 and was deliberately designed to prevent the SINZ from becoming a successor to the DICM

**Explicit prohibitions:**
- Domestic political surveillance of citizens, parties, unions, or civil society organizations (Ley 15.890, Art. 7)
- Opening domestic criminal investigations without coordination with and authorization from the Public Prosecutor's office
- Conducting paramilitary operations or arrests; intelligence gathered is shared with law enforcement, which must conduct arrests
- Surveillance of journalists for their journalistic activities

**Structure (five directorates):**
1. **Dirección de Inteligencia Exterior (DIE)** — foreign intelligence; works in coordination with the MRE's foreign intelligence desk; personnel include both SINZ career officers and seconded diplomats
2. **Dirección de Contrainteligencia (DCI)** — counterintelligence; monitors foreign intelligence services operating in Zoop; protects classified government and military information
3. **Dirección de Contraterrorismo y Crimen Organizado (DTCO)** — counterterrorism and organized crime intelligence; the primary interface with DEA, UNODC, and Brazilian intelligence on narcotrafficking
4. **Dirección de Análisis Estratégico (DAE)** — all-source intelligence analysis; produces the **Informe de Situación Nacional (ISN)**, a weekly classified briefing delivered to the President, Cabinet, and key congressional leaders
5. **Dirección de Tecnología e Intercepción (DTI)** — signals intelligence (SIGINT) and communications interception, subject to judicial authorization requirements per Ley 15.890

**Personnel:** approximately 680 full-time employees (classified but estimated by CCPI reviews); a civilian-majority workforce; approximately 30% are military personnel on temporary assignment from the Estado Mayor Conjunto

**Budget:** classified; estimated at approximately ZPS 1.2–1.6 billion annually (approximately USD 55–70 million at 2024 exchange rates); subject to CCPI review in closed session

**Inspector General of Intelligence (current, as of 2026):** **Dr. Hernán Soler Bustamante** (b. 1962, Zoopaná; constitutional lawyer; former UNZ professor; former member of the CVMN staff 1986–1988; appointed by President Carballo in 2024; confirmed by the Senate 71–31; his appointment was seen as a signal of commitment to accountability given his human rights history)

**Current Director of SINZ (as of 2026):** **Lic. Claudia Beatriz Reyes Orellana** (b. 1972, Puerto Marán; career intelligence professional; joined DNICE in 1998 after graduating from UPM in political science; served in the DIE's Brazil desk 2001–2008; headed the DTCO 2015–2023; appointed director by President Carballo March 2023; first woman to serve as SINZ director; former CCPI briefer — familiar to both FPZ and PR legislators)

**Headquarters:** The SINZ occupies a deliberately non-descript office complex in the **Barrio Ceibo** district of Zoopaná — chosen in 2004 specifically to avoid the visual weight of a military or government compound. The building is not publicly identified as SINZ headquarters; it is listed in the government property registry as "Edificio de Análisis Estadístico Nacional."

**Controversies:**
- **2008 — "Caso Litio":** Leaked documents appeared to suggest SINZ monitoring of indigenous rights advocates in Pequeé Norte who were organizing against mining concessions. The CCPI investigated; SINZ denied domestic political surveillance; the Inspector General at the time found "evidence of compliance failures" in one unit but did not find systematic surveillance; two DTCO personnel were reassigned. The case is still cited by civil liberties organizations as evidence that the domestic surveillance prohibition is imperfectly enforced.
- **2019 — DEA Coordination Controversy:** The *La Voz del Sur* newspaper reported that SINZ had shared domestic telephone metadata with the DEA without judicial authorization, in relation to a narcotrafficking investigation centered on the Puerto Marán port. The CCPI found that the metadata sharing had judicial authorization (an emergency court order) but that the SINZ had not promptly notified the Inspector General as required by Ley 16.340. The director at the time received a formal censure from the CCPI.

## SECTION 3 — THE HUMAN RIGHTS MOVEMENT

### The Asociación de Madres y Familiares de Desaparecidos Zoopanos (AMFDZ)

**Full name:** *Asociación de Madres y Familiares de Desaparecidos Zoopanos* (AMFDZ) — Association of Mothers and Family Members of Zoopan Disappeared

**Common name:** "Las Madres" in everyday usage; formally "las Madres y Familiares" or simply "la Asociación"

**Founded:** **7 September 1978** in Puerto Marán (the date has significance: it falls on the Air Force holiday, chosen deliberately as a silent protest against the military institution)

**Founding circumstances:**
In early 1978, six women — all mothers of disappeared persons, all from working-class neighborhoods in Puerto Marán and the surrounding port zone — began meeting informally at the church of **Parroquia de Nuestra Señora del Carmen** in Barrio Palermo. The immediate trigger was the refusal by the DICM's public-facing office (a bureaucratic facade called the *Oficina de Consultas de Seguridad*) to provide any information about the whereabouts of their children. The women had each made individual petitions through official channels for months or years; they had all received the same form letter stating their relative was "not registered in any detention facility." The decision to organize collectively came after **Señora Inés Pacheco de Celorio** — mother of disappeared journalism student Martín Celorio Pacheco — proposed that they approach the government together. They were joined by **Señora Dolores Vargas de Suárez** (1931–2014; mother of union organizer Hugo Vargas Suárez, disappeared 1976), **Señora Rosario Alcántara viuda de Herrera** (1934–2019; mother of law student Germán Herrera Alcántara, disappeared 1977), **Señora Marta Elena Bogado de Peralta** (1929–2007; mother of schoolteacher Renata Peralta Bogado, disappeared 1977), **Señora Concepción Fuentes de Zárate** (b. 1941, still living as of 2026; mother of university student Rubén Zárate Fuentes, disappeared 1975), and **Señora Luisa Carvajal de Montoya** (1928–2015; mother of journalist Eduardo Montoya Carvajal, disappeared 1976).

On 7 September 1978, these six women appeared together at the *Oficina de Consultas de Seguridad* and read a joint statement demanding formal accounting for their disappeared children. They were removed from the building by police. The following week they appeared again, this time in front of the building. That act of public appearance gave them visibility, and within six weeks more than 40 women had joined them.

**The name "Madres":** The original group debated several names; "Madres" was chosen deliberately to emphasize the maternal relationship — a calculation that they would be harder for the regime to dismiss as subversives if they presented themselves as mothers grieving their children. In practice, the group always included fathers, siblings, and other family members; the full name ("Madres y Familiares") was adopted formally at the first assembly in September 1978.

**Founding president:** **Señora Inés Pacheco de Celorio** (1923–2008); she served as president from 1978 until her death in 2008. Her son Martín's case became one of the most internationally known, partly because of her own visibility.

**The weekly marches:** From October 1978 onward, the AMFDZ held a weekly silent vigil at the **Plaza de la Independencia** in Zoopaná — the capital's central square, in front of the Casa de Gobierno. The decision to march in Zoopaná rather than Puerto Marán (where the group was founded) was strategic: it brought the women directly to the seat of government. The march takes place **every Thursday at 6:00 PM**. The women circle the Mausoleo de los Fundadores in the center of the plaza, carrying photographs of the disappeared and wearing white headgear — not scarves but white cotton caps (*gorros blancos*), distinguished from the Argentine madres' white headscarves; the gorros blancos became the visual signature of the movement.

**The Thursday march under the dictatorship (1978–1985):**
The marches were not tolerated quietly. Between October 1978 and December 1981, the police broke up the march on 14 separate occasions; 127 individual AMFDZ members were detained briefly (hours to a few days), released without charge. Three women were held for longer periods: **Señora Concepción Fuentes de Zárate** was held for 11 days in November 1979 (the longest detention of any founding member); she was released after an Amnesty International intervention and her case reached the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights. The press (under censorship) was prohibited from covering the marches; *El Heraldo del Marán*'s society editor nonetheless published a brief item on the marches in March 1980 using the coded phrase "weekly gathering of civic-minded women" — the item was pulled from subsequent editions but copies circulated.

Under the Valdés Merino transitional government (1981–1985), police harassment of the marches gradually ceased; by 1983 the marches were occurring without interference.

**The Thursday march today:**
As of 2026, the AMFDZ continues to hold its Thursday march every week, with no exceptions since October 1978. The march is now smaller than during its peak years (2,000–4,000 marchers in the late 1980s; currently approximately 200–600 depending on the week and whether a particular anniversary is being marked), but it has never stopped. It is part of the urban fabric of Zoopaná; tour guides include it in walking tours; foreign dignitaries have been photographed joining the march as a gesture of solidarity. The current president of the AMFDZ is **Dra. Carmen Medina Esteves** (b. 1967; daughter of disappeared victim Nora Esteves de Medina; became president of the AMFDZ in 2012; human rights lawyer; was also a member of the 2018 CNECP commission).

**Demands (historical and current):**
- *Historical (1978–1989):* Truth about the fate of disappeared relatives; release of all political prisoners; dismantling of the detention centers; prosecution of those responsible
- *Post-truth commission:* Continuing prosecution of all DICM and DOP personnel responsible for individual crimes; recovery of the remains of the still-missing (2,747 confirmed victims whose remains have never been recovered); identity recovery for children of disappeared persons (see Nietos en Búsqueda, below); formal state apology; reparations for survivors and families
- *Current (as of 2026):* The AMFDZ focuses its advocacy on: (a) the 14 pending criminal cases still working through the court system; (b) the identity recovery program for children; (c) opposition to any future amnesty or pardon for convicted perpetrators; (d) formal repeal of the Ley de Amnistía Militar (1984), which was gutted by the 1987 Constitutional Court ruling but never formally stricken from the legal code

**International recognition:** The AMFDZ received the **Premio Internacional de Derechos Humanos de la Unión Europea** in 1993; the **Premio Rafto** (Norway) in 1996; the **Premio Seligmann of the American Association for the Advancement of Human Rights** in 2001. Señora Inés Pacheco de Celorio was nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize in 1985 and 1986 (did not receive it); the nomination was organized by an international coalition of human rights organizations.

**The split (1995–2000):** Like the Argentine Madres de Plaza de Mayo, the AMFDZ experienced an internal split in the mid-1990s over strategy. The **Asociación de Familiares por la Justicia Total (AFJT)**, a smaller splinter organization founded in 1995, argued that the AMFDZ had become too institutionalized and too accommodating of partial justice; they demanded all-or-nothing prosecutions and refused to engage with any amnesty-adjacent conversations. The AMFDZ formally distanced itself from the AFJT's maximalist position but maintains cordial relations with individual AFJT members. The AFJT has approximately 80 active members as of 2026.

### Other Human Rights Organizations

**Centro de Derechos Humanos de Puerto Marán (CDHPM):**
- Founded 1985, immediately after the democratic transition
- Primary focus: legal representation for families of the disappeared in the junta trial proceedings and subsequent cases; also provides legal services for current migrants and labor rights cases
- **Marina Aguilar** (later President #34) worked here 1985–1988 as a young attorney
- **Dr. Carmen Medina Esteves** (AMFDZ president from 2012) was a staff attorney here 1993–2010
- Current director: **Lic. Tomás Reyes Ibáñez** (b. 1975, Puerto Marán; attorney; son of a disappeared factory worker; UPM law graduate 1998)
- Maintains the most comprehensive private database of documented disappearance cases outside the CVMN archive

**Cátedra de Derechos Humanos "Alonso Pereyra," Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná:**
- Named for the CVMN chair Dr. Ricardo Fermín Alonso Pereyra (1920–2001); established 1997 after his death was announced as imminent (he lived until 2001)
- An academic research and teaching center embedded in the UNZ law faculty; publishes the *Anuario de Derechos Humanos Zoopano* (annual peer-reviewed journal, established 1998)
- Director (current): **Prof. Dr. Sebastián Arroyo Brizuela** (b. 1964, Zoopaná; international law specialist; published 14 books on transitional justice in Latin America)
- Has documented 47 additional cases of Zoopan exiles disappeared abroad through Condor-style coordination, beyond the 14 cases included in the CVMN report

**Comité de Familiares de Desaparecidos de Zoop (CFDZ):**
- Established in Puerto Marán in 1978 (established in Session 1-F); was the proto-organization from which the AMFDZ grew in September 1978; the CFDZ and AMFDZ are sister organizations, not the same body
- The CFDZ focuses specifically on families based in Puerto Marán and the coast; it operates a documentation center at Barrio Palermo and sponsors the annual **Vigilia del Puerto** on 19 August each year (the anniversary of Nora Esteves de Medina's kidnapping)
- Carmen Méndez de Carballo (mother of President Inés Carballo) was an active CFDZ member in the 1980s

**Amnesty International — Zoop Program:**
- Amnesty International has maintained a Zoop-specific program since 1977 (the year Nora Esteves de Medina's case was included in their global report)
- Their 1978 report on Zoop, which named 23 documented disappeared persons, was one of the first international publications to break through the dictatorship's information suppression; the report was written by AI researcher **Dr. Katharine Webb-Marín** (British; b. 1948; now retired)
- AI's current Zoop focus areas: the 14 pending criminal cases; the identity recovery program; migration and detention conditions for Tucareño migrants

### The Identity Recovery Program (Nietos en Búsqueda)

**Name:** *Nietos en Búsqueda* (Grandchildren in Search) — the Zoopan program equivalent to Argentina's *Abuelas de Plaza de Mayo* work

**Context:** During the Years of Lead, at least 47 children were born to detained, pregnant women in clandestine facilities, then given to other families — typically families associated with the security apparatus — with falsified birth records. Additionally, some young children were taken from their homes when parents were disappeared; the children were either returned to extended family (in some cases), given into adoptive situations with falsified records, or disappeared themselves.

**Program establishment:** The *Nietos en Búsqueda* program was established in 1990 as a joint initiative between the **AMFDZ**, the **CDHPM**, and the **Banco Nacional de Datos Genéticos Zoopano (BNDGZ)** — the national genetic database — which was created by **Ley 13.990/1990** specifically to hold genetic profiles of families of the disappeared for future matching.

**The BNDGZ:** Founded 1990; headquartered in Zoopaná (within the Ministerio de Salud complex); currently holds approximately 1,840 genetic profiles of family members of disappeared persons; has facilitated the identification of 23 identity cases as of 2026.

**The 47 documented birth cases:**
- 23 of the 47 children born in captivity have been identified: 16 through genetic testing, 7 through documentary evidence (witnesses, false-identity record inconsistencies)
- 24 cases remain unresolved as of 2026; their identities as of 2026 are unknown; they could be living with false identities with no knowledge of their origins
- The youngest of these individuals would be approximately 43–44 years old in 2026; many of the identity-discovery cases have been psychologically difficult for individuals who had no knowledge of their parentage
- A 2018 documentary film, **"Los Nietos de la Memoria"** (director: **Florencia Ibáñez Castellano**, b. 1981, Puerto Marán), profiled five identity-recovery cases and was shown at film festivals internationally; the film was purchased for streaming by a Spanish platform

**Current director of Nietos en Búsqueda:** **Lic. Valentina Herrera Paz** (b. 1968, Zoopaná; granddaughter of a DICM victim; social work background; worked with the AMFDZ since 1992; became director of the program in 2005)

## SECTION 4 — THE JUNTA TRIAL (JUICIO A LA JUNTA)

### Overview

All essential facts about the trial are established in Session 5-A. This section adds prosecution detail and expands the amnesty narrative.

**Formal name:** *Juicio a la Junta Militar del Proceso Zoopano* (Trial of the Junta of the Zoopan Proceso); widely known simply as **"El Juicio"** or **"el Juicio a la Junta"**

**Venue:** **Tribunal Penal Nacional de Zoopaná**, a federal criminal court located in the Centro Histórico district of Zoopaná; not the Constitutional Court (which had ruled on the amnesty law) but the regular federal criminal bench

**Duration:** Main proceedings September 1987 — August 1989 (verdict announced 22 August 1989)

### The Prosecution

**Lead prosecutor:** **Dr. Francisco Jacinto Arrieta Olmedo** (1948–2018, born in San Bartolomé del Pequeé; graduate of UNZ law faculty 1972; worked as a labor lawyer during the dictatorship; briefly detained in 1976 and released; joined the federal prosecution service in 1985; appointed chief prosecutor for the Juicio a la Junta by Attorney General **Dr. Horacio Mendívil Espinoza** in March 1987)

**Arrieta Olmedo's strategy:**
- The prosecution chose to organize the case around three "axes": command responsibility at the top (proving Medrano Castellanos and Cerruti Páez gave orders); individual operational responsibility in the middle (proving Torino Escarcena and Navarro Palacios executed specific crimes); and documentary and testimonial evidence linking the two
- Rather than trying to prove every individual act of every perpetrator, the prosecution established the pattern of systematic state crime and then demonstrated each defendant's role within that pattern — a strategy modeled on the Nuremberg precedents and the Israeli prosecution of Eichmann
- The prosecution put 312 witnesses on the stand over 14 months; 218 were survivor-witnesses (former prisoners who had survived), 44 were expert witnesses (physicians, psychologists, forensic anthropologists), 28 were documentary witnesses (archivists, investigators), and 22 were reluctant witnesses who had been subpoenaed from within the military establishment
- The most significant evidentiary breakthrough came through the testimony of **Teniente Coronel Demetrio Reyes Alarcón** (established in 5-A), whose account of the Villa Belgrano command structure was the first insider testimony to directly link Medrano Castellanos to specific operational orders

**The evidence presented:**
- 4,847 individual case files compiled by the CVMN
- Partial DICM operational logs (partially destroyed; recovered partially by CVMN investigators)
- Survivor testimony from all four detention centers
- Forensic anthropological evidence from the Villa Belgrano excavation (186 skeletal remains)
- Expert psychiatric testimony on the psychological effects of systematic torture (establishing that the torture methods used were coherent across facilities — evidence of a centralized program rather than individual acts)
- A partially decoded DICM communications log that referred to the "plan" and contained abbreviations later decoded as referring to specific detention centers

### The Defense

**Lead defense counsel for Medrano Castellanos:** **Dr. Rodrigo Fuentes Bermúdez** (b. 1942, Zoopaná; criminal lawyer; selected by the defendant personally; known for his willingness to take politically complex cases)

**Defense arguments:**
- The 1984 Ley de Amnistía Militar granted immunity for acts committed in the "war against subversion"; this argument was rejected by the Constitutional Court's 1987 ruling before the trial began, but the defense continued to raise it as a constitutional objection throughout
- The defendants were acting under military orders and within the legal framework of the time (the "superior orders" defense); the court rejected this — following Nuremberg precedent — for crimes against humanity
- Cerruti Páez's defense argued that the CVMN report was based on "distorted" survivor testimony from persons seeking political revenge, and that the operational logs were incomplete and misinterpreted; this was rejected on the weight of corroborating physical evidence
- Navarro Palacios's defense for the "desaparecidos al mar" argued that the testimony about sea-transfer of prisoners came from persons who had not directly witnessed the events; the prosecution responded with multiple survivor accounts from El Galpón who had seen prisoners removed in groups at night and had confirmed with the families afterward that those prisoners never returned

**The acquittal of Oñate Castellanos:** The air force commander was acquitted because the prosecution could not establish that he had personally ordered or participated in specific acts. The court held that mere institutional membership in the junta during the crimes was insufficient for conviction without evidence of specific commands or personal participation. This acquittal was politically controversial — many human rights organizations criticized it as evidence that the court applied a higher individual-culpability standard than the command responsibility standard used for the army defendants.

### The Amnesty Debate — Full History

**Ley de Amnistía Militar (March 1984):**
- Enacted by a transitional congress under the Valdés Merino government as the military's price for peaceful transition
- The law granted amnesty to "military and security personnel for acts performed in the defense of the Republic against subversive and terrorist activities" between 1966 and 1983
- The FPZ voted against it; the PR accepted it as a transition necessity; the law passed with bare majority
- The law included a clause stating it applied to "all acts, even those not publicly known at the time of enactment" — language deliberately designed to cover acts the military knew were criminal

**Constitutional Court Decision 27/1987 (14 July 1987):**
- The Marqués government challenged the amnesty law directly; the case was argued by Attorney General Mendívil Espinoza
- The court held 7–2 that the amnesty law, as applied to systematic crimes against humanity including kidnapping, torture, and murder as a state-organized program, was unconstitutional: it violated the international law obligations Zoop had accepted as a UN founding member, including the 1948 Genocide Convention and customary international law on crimes against humanity
- The two dissenting justices (both appointed during the Valdés Merino transitional period) argued that the court was engaging in retroactive criminal law in violation of constitutional due process guarantees
- The ruling did not formally repeal the amnesty law but rendered it inapplicable to the senior junta leadership and DICM command structure

**Ley de Imprescriptibilidad de los Crímenes de Lesa Humanidad (Law 16.044, June 1995):**
- Enacted under Briceño Lemus (#28); established that crimes against humanity committed during the Proceso were not subject to any statute of limitations
- This law enabled the prosecution of lower-ranking DICM and DOP personnel in the 2000s and 2010s — many of whom had waited out the amnesty period hoping the prosecutions would end

**Ongoing controversy (as of 2026):**
- The Ley de Amnistía Militar (1984) has never been formally repealed by Congress; the AMFDZ has repeatedly demanded its formal repeal; the PR has consistently blocked repeal votes, arguing that a law superseded by judicial ruling need not be repealed legislatively; the FPZ and coalition partners have introduced repeal legislation 8 times since 1990, most recently in 2024 (failed 19–23 in Senate)
- **14 pending cases** remain active in the Tribunal Penal Nacional as of 2026; defendants range from age 74 to 94; 6 are incarcerated, 4 are under house arrest, 4 are awaiting final sentencing; human rights advocates note that at the current pace, several defendants will die before their cases conclude
- **The "biological amnesty" debate:** A 2022 proposal by three PR senators to convert the sentences of convicted perpetrators over age 80 with terminal diagnoses to house arrest ("cumplimiento domiciliario humanitario") was defeated 41–1 in the Senate; only the PR senators who proposed it voted in favor; it generated enormous public protest

## SECTION 5 — ORGANIZED CRIME AND NARCOTRAFFICKING

### Zoop as a Transit Country

Zoop is a **transit country** for cocaine originating in Bolivia, Peru, and Colombia. It is not a significant producer of cocaine. The transit routes use Zoop's Atlantic coast and its relatively less-surveilled southern provinces as stepping stones toward the South Atlantic, the EU (via West Africa), and the U.S. east coast.

**Primary transit routes:**
1. **The Puerto Marán port route:** Cocaine concealed in containers of agricultural exports (primarily frozen beef, soy, and citrus); the volume of legitimate cargo through Puerto Marán (~48 million tonnes/year) creates enormous inspection challenges; the DEA has estimated that Zoopan port inspectors physically examine fewer than 2% of outbound containers
2. **The southern coastal route:** Smaller vessels (fishing and pleasure craft) moving cocaine along the Atlantic coast from Argentine and Uruguayan waters, landing at secondary ports (Puerto Santa Lucía, Puerto Canela, and isolated coastal points in Quilmes del Sur); destined for transfer to larger vessels in the South Atlantic
3. **The overland-to-Atlantic route:** Cocaine enters from Argentina and Uruguay overland (across the southern border crossings RN-3 and RN-14), is stored in Pampa Central provinces, then moves to Puerto Marán for export

**Scale of the problem (estimates, as of 2024–2026):**
- Drug enforcement authorities estimate 15,000–25,000 kg of cocaine transit Zoop annually; the actual figure may be significantly higher
- Seizures by Zoopan police and customs: approximately 2,800 kg in 2024 (a 40% increase from 2020 figures, reflecting improved interdiction capacity rather than growth in the problem); this represents approximately 11–18% of estimated transit volume

**Criminal organizations:**
- No Zoopan-origin trafficking organization operates at significant scale; Zoop is a transit zone served by South American and European criminal networks
- The primary operators in the Puerto Marán port corridor are **Puerto Rican–affiliated Medellín cartel distribution cells** (small, temporary; personnel cycle frequently to avoid surveillance) and connections to **Brazilian Primeiro Comando da Capital (PCC)** and **Comando Vermelho (CV)** networks
- **Local criminal networks** ("bandas portuarias") in the Puerto Marán port district provide logistical services (warehousing, container infiltration, documentation falsification) to the international networks; the largest of these, active as of 2024–2026, is known to investigators as the **"Red del Muelle"** (*The Dock Network*); approximately 40–60 individuals; leadership suspected but not yet prosecuted

### Police Corruption

**The corruption problem:**
Police corruption related to narcotrafficking is documented and politically acknowledged in Zoop. The most significant corruption concentrations are:
- **Puerto Marán port police** (*Policía Portuaria*): Inspector-level and below; evidence of payments by the Red del Muelle to ensure that cargo with irregular documentation passes inspection without secondary review; three officers convicted 2019–2022
- **Customs inspectors** (not technically police but in the same institutional environment): similar corruption patterns; the most senior conviction involved a **Jefe de Turno** at the port's container terminal who received payments for 8 years; convicted in 2023; sentenced to 14 years
- **Provincial police in Quilmes del Sur:** the least-resourced and most isolated provincial force; the southern coastal landing route has been facilitated by payments to rural officers; three provincial police commanders were removed in 2021 following a federal investigation

**Structural vulnerability factors:**
- Zoopan police salaries are low: a base constable in the provincial police earns approximately ZPS 48,000/month (~USD 2,200 at 2024 exchange rates), making bribery offers highly attractive
- The provincial police forces are under provincial authority; federal anti-corruption bodies have limited investigative reach into provincial forces without a formal joint operation agreement
- The Puerto Marán port zone involves a complex jurisdictional overlap between the federal *Policía Federal Zoopana* (PFZ), the Maraná provincial police, the *Policía Portuaria* (a specialized port unit), and customs (under the Treasury Ministry); this fragmentation has been exploited by trafficking networks

### Anti-Drug Programs

**SENAD (Secretaría Nacional Antidrogas):** The primary civilian anti-drug coordination body; established 1994 under Briceño Lemus; reports to the Interior Ministry; coordinates between the SINZ (intelligence), the PFZ (enforcement), and the Gendarmería (border interdiction); runs the national prevention and demand-reduction programs; leads the bilateral cooperation with the DEA (formalized through a 1996 bilateral agreement)

**DEA presence:** The U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration has maintained a Country Office at the U.S. Embassy in Zoopaná since 1993; typically 8–12 DEA agents; works primarily with SENAD and the SINZ's DTCO directorate; the relationship has been generally productive though politically sensitive — the FPZ left and MZ have periodically criticized the DEA's presence as a form of U.S. intelligence intrusion

**Programa de Protección a Testigos (PPT):** The federal witness protection program, established 2006 under Peñaloza Villafuertes; has protected 34 informants in narcotrafficking cases as of 2026; administered jointly by SENAD and the Public Prosecutor's Office

**The Carballo government's anti-crime initiative (2024):** In March 2024, President Carballo announced the **Plan Integral de Seguridad Portuaria (PISP)**, a ZPS 320 million investment in container scanning equipment, port police staffing increases, and a corruption-detection unit for customs personnel. The PISP also includes a judicial fast-track for port-related corruption cases. As of 2026, approximately 40% of the scanning equipment has been installed; the judicial fast-track has been operational since September 2024.

## SECTION 6 — NATIONAL POLICE STRUCTURE

### The Policía Federal Zoopana (PFZ)

**Name:** *Policía Federal Zoopana* (PFZ) — Zoopan Federal Police
**Jurisdiction:** Federal crimes; federal territory (Autonomous District of Zoopaná); inter-provincial crimes; narcotrafficking; terrorism; organized crime; immigration enforcement in the interior
**Personnel:** approximately 18,500 active officers (2024)
**Headquarters:** Palacio de la Policía Federal, Zoopaná (Barrio Financiero; built 1934; substantially renovated 1993)
**Legal basis:** **Ley Orgánica de la Policía Federal (Law 8.440, 1966)** — an irony of Zoopan legal history that the PFZ's foundational law was enacted in the same year as the Robaina coup; the law has been amended many times but never replaced with a post-transition organic law
**Director General (current, as of 2026):** **Comisario General Eduardo Peralta Ruiz** (b. 1968, Zoopaná; career officer; joined PFZ 1990; headed the organized crime division 2010–2018; appointed director by Carballo 2023)

### Provincial Police Forces (Policías Provinciales)

Each of the 14 provinces has its own police force under the authority of the provincial governor. This creates 14 separate forces with their own chains of command, budgets, training standards, and uniform regulations. The forces range in size from the **Policía de la Provincia de Maraná** (~6,200 officers; serves Puerto Marán and the surrounding province) to the **Policía de la Provincia de Río Hondo** (~890 officers; the smallest).

**Coordination mechanisms:**
- **Consejo Nacional de Policías Provinciales (CNPP):** A consultative body of all 14 provincial police commanders plus the PFZ director; meets quarterly; no operational authority but coordinates training standards and information sharing
- **Sistema de Información Policial Zoopano (SIPZ):** A national criminal records database shared by all forces; established 2001 (Cossio Peralta); upgraded significantly in 2009 and 2018; managed by the Interior Ministry

**Inter-force tensions:** Jurisdictional friction between the PFZ and provincial forces is a recurring problem; the most common flashpoints involve:
- Cross-provincial crimes (the PFZ claims federal jurisdiction; provinces resist "federal overreach")
- Narcotrafficking investigations originating in one province and crossing into another
- Crimes involving high-profile individuals who have political connections to provincial police leadership

### Police Reform History

**Post-1985 reforms:**
The democratic transition required a complete ideological rethinking of the police institution, which had been deeply entangled with the DICM apparatus:
- The **Comisión de Depuración Policial (CDP)** (1985–1988): Established by the Marqués government to identify and remove police personnel with documented DICM collaboration; 347 officers were removed from active service; 89 were referred for criminal prosecution (with varying outcomes)
- **Human rights curriculum reform (1986):** Mandatory human rights training introduced into the PFZ and all provincial police academies; based on international law standards; co-developed with the CDHPM
- **Civilian complaint mechanism:** The **Defensoría del Ciudadano frente a la Policía (DCP)** was established 1990; an independent civilian body that receives complaints against police conduct; has authority to investigate and recommend disciplinary action; lacks direct enforcement power (enforcement remains within the police chain of command, which has been its ongoing limitation)

**Ongoing reform debates (as of 2026):**
- **Excessive force:** The killing of **Tobías Mamani Solis** (22 years old, Zopikí heritage, Cerro Verde) by a PFZ officer during a protest at the Salar de Tupanaku in March 2024 renewed debate about the use-of-force standards and about whether the police use of force against indigenous protesters reflects systemic racism; the officer was charged; the trial is pending as of 2026
- **Salary and corruption:** The reform commission established by the Carballo government (*Comisión Carballo de Reforma Policial*, established November 2023, chaired by **Dra. Lucía Ortega Perón**, b. 1971, UNZ criminologist) issued interim findings in March 2025 recommending a 35% salary increase for base-level PFZ and provincial officers, mandatory random financial audits for officers in high-corruption-risk posts, and a full replacement of the 1966 Ley Orgánica with a modern democratic police law. As of 2026, the salary increase has been partially implemented; the legislative reform is pending.
- **The accountability gap:** Human rights organizations note that while police conduct toward middle-class citizens has improved substantially since 1985, conduct toward informal settlement residents, Zopikí communities, and migrants remains more likely to involve rights violations; the DCP data shows that 68% of excessive-force complaints come from persons in the lowest two income quartiles
