## SECTION 1 — MILITARY STRUCTURE: OVERVIEW

### The Four Branches

The **Fuerzas Armadas de la República de Zoop (FARZ)** comprise four branches:

1. **Ejército Nacional Zoopano (ENZ)** — Army
2. **Armada Nacional Zoopana (ANZ)** — Navy
3. **Fuerza Aérea Zoopana (FAZ)** — Air Force
4. **Gendarmería Nacional Zoopana (GNZ)** — Border Guard (paramilitary, under Defense Ministry)

**Total active personnel:** approximately 52,000
- Army: approximately 35,000
- Navy: approximately 8,500 (including ~1,200 naval infantry / marines)
- Air Force: approximately 5,800
- Gendarmería: approximately 2,700

**Reserve forces:** approximately 18,000 (organized reserve, trained through mandatory refresher cycles; not conscripts)

**Defense spending:** approximately 1.4% of GDP (~$2.66 billion USD, 2024)

---

### Command Hierarchy

**Constitutional command chain:**
- **President of the Republic** — Supreme Commander-in-Chief (*Comandante en Jefe* de las Fuerzas Armadas)
- **Minister of Defense** — civilian by law since the **Ley de Conducción Civil de las Fuerzas Armadas (Law 14.722, enacted 12 April 1994)**; the minister exercises day-to-day political authority over all branches and the Gendarmería
- **Estado Mayor Conjunto (EMC)** — Joint Chiefs of Staff; established in its current form by Law 15.001 (1997 reform); chaired by the **Jefe del Estado Mayor Conjunto (JEMC)**, a four-star officer rotated among branches on a two-year basis
- Individual branch commanders:
  - **Comandante General del Ejército** (Army)
  - **Comandante General de la Armada** (Navy)
  - **Comandante General de la Fuerza Aérea** (Air Force)
  - **Director General de la Gendarmería Nacional** (Gendarmería — a three-star position)

The JEMC is formally the highest-ranking active military officer; the branch commanders serve as peers under the minister on the Defense Council (*Consejo de Defensa Nacional*), which also includes the ministers of Foreign Relations and Interior.

## SECTION 2 — THE ARMY (EJÉRCITO NACIONAL ZOOPANO)

### Founding and Early History

The Ejército Nacional Zoopano traces its formal founding to **12 November 1827**, when President Mariano Pelayo Aranduy (#1) signed the **Decreto Orgánico de la Fuerza Militar de la República** — the Organic Decree of the Republic's Military Force — establishing the first permanent professional army distinct from the provincial militia forces that had fought the Guerra de Liberación. This date is celebrated as **Día del Ejército** (Army Day) annually.

The early army was small (~3,500 men), poorly equipped, and organized along militia lines. The first significant professionalization occurred under Juan Bautista Solórzano ("El Mariscal"), who:
- Contracted a Prussian military adviser, **Oberst Friedrich von Kaltenberg** (1810–1874), in 1848 to reorganize officer training and drill standards
- Established the **Escuela Militar de Zoopaná** (precursor to the modern Academia Militar Nacional) in 1852
- Created the first formal staff corps (*Estado Mayor*) in 1853
- Instituted standardized uniform regulations and rank insignia in 1854

### Post-War of the Pequeé Professionalization

The War of the Pequeé (1865–1870) exposed serious deficiencies in Zoopan military organization — particularly logistics, medical services, and officer quality below the regimental level. In the aftermath, President Barros Uribe (#9) authorized the **Comisión de Reforma Militar (CRM)** in 1873, chaired by **General de Brigada Eulogio Mena Castellanos** (1836–1904), a veteran of the war who had commanded the final campaign.

General Mena Castellanos produced the **Informe Mena** (1874), which recommended:
- Mandatory professional military education for all officers above the rank of Captain
- A permanent military training establishment to replace ad hoc regimental instruction
- A medical corps independent of civilian contract surgeons
- The adoption of the Chassepot-derived rifle then being phased out by France (purchased at favorable postwar prices)

The **Academia Militar Nacional (AMN)** in its modern form was established by **Ley 2.847/1875** (enacted the same year as the civilian Constitution of 1875), located on the western edge of Zoopaná in the **Distrito de la Banda** — a purpose-built campus that opened its first class in March 1876. Its founding director was **Coronel Ramón Silvio Durán Heredia** (1842–1911), a veteran of the War of the Pequeé who had studied at the French Saint-Cyr military academy on exchange in 1869.

A further modernization followed contact with the German military model during the Liberal Era. In 1899, a **Misión Militar Alemana** (German Military Mission) of seven officers was contracted to advise on staff organization and tactical doctrine; they served until 1912. This German influence shaped Zoopan officer culture significantly — a fact that has been remarked upon ironically by historians noting that officers trained under German doctrine later ran the dictatorship.

### Territorial Organization (Current)

The army is organized into three corps:
- **I Cuerpo de Ejército** (First Army Corps) — headquarters: Zoopaná; responsible for the Autonomous District, Pampa Central, and Costa Atlántica north provinces
- **II Cuerpo de Ejército** (Second Army Corps) — headquarters: Puerto Marán; responsible for Provincia de Maraná and the southern Atlantic coast
- **III Cuerpo de Ejército** (Third Army Corps) — headquarters: Cerro Verde; responsible for the Sierra del Oeste, including the Tucaré border zones

Each corps commands two to three divisions; a division commands two to four brigades. As of 2024, the army fields:
- 2 armored brigades
- 4 mechanized infantry brigades
- 2 mountain brigades (III Corps; critical for Tucaré border)
- 1 airborne brigade (parachute-qualified; stationed near Zoopaná)
- 1 engineering brigade
- 1 special operations group

### Named Army Bases (locked in)

- **Campo Militar Aranduy** — Zoopaná outskirts (Banda district); I Corps headquarters; largest army installation; ~8,000 personnel; includes the Academia Militar Nacional
- **Regimiento Pequeé** — San Bartolomé del Pequeé; armored brigade headquarters; named for the war
- **Base Montaña Tupanaku** — Las Cumbres province, near San Ignacio; mountain brigade headquarters; altitude acclimatization training
- **Cuartel General Macías** — Cerro Verde; III Corps headquarters; named for General Macías Olivares (posthumous recognition as commander of the Cordillera War; a somewhat contested honor given the war's outcome)
- **Campo de Entrenamiento La Confluencia** — Confluencia, San Bartolomé province; armored brigade training ground; named for the 1865 battle

## SECTION 3 — THE NAVY (ARMADA NACIONAL ZOOPANA)

### Founding and Early History

The navy was formally constituted on **3 March 1828** by the same broad military-reform legislation of the Aranduy presidency, though Zoop had maintained armed coastal patrol vessels since independence. The early ANZ consisted of converted merchant ships and a handful of purpose-built gunboats.

During the War of the Pequeé, the navy played a limited role — the conflict was primarily a land war in the highland interior, but the ANZ maintained blockade patrols to prevent Tucareño arms imports via the Atlantic. The navy's most useful contribution was logistical transport of troops and materiel to Puerto Marán's hinterland.

The **Liberal Era** saw significant naval expansion under **Almirante Rodrigo Faustino Echeverría Salas** (1848–1919), who served as Comandante General de la Armada from 1890 to 1907 and oversaw the purchase of two cruisers from British yards, the construction of the Puerto Santa Lucía naval base, and the founding of the **Escuela Naval de la Armada Zoopana** in 1893. Almirante Echeverría Salas is remembered as the founder of the modern Zoopan navy.

### Current Strength

The Armada Nacional Zoopana operates from its primary base at **Base Naval Puerto Santa Lucía** (co-located at Puerto Santa Lucía, Provincia de Santa Lucía del Mar) and a secondary installation at **Base Naval Norte** in Candelaria del Mar.

**Major surface vessels:**
- 3 frigates (two OPV-class patrol frigates purchased from Spain in the early 2000s; one older French-designed frigate from the 1980s)
- 4 offshore patrol vessels (OPVs)
- 6 coastal patrol craft
- 2 logistical support / supply vessels
- 1 training vessel (AZF *General Herrera Lejarza*, named for the WWI BVZ commander)
- 1 antarctic support ship (AZF *Continente Sur*, operates in rotation with civilian Antarctic program)

**Submarine force:** The ANZ maintains **no active submarines** as of 2024; a submarine acquisition program was studied in 2017–2019 but deferred due to budget constraints.

**Naval infantry (marines):** A single marine battalion (~1,200 personnel) based at Puerto Santa Lucía; capable of amphibious operations; has participated in UN peacekeeping in the infantry role.

**Antarctic mission:** The navy jointly operates the **Estación Aranduy** with the civilian **Comisión Nacional de Investigación Antártica (CNIA)** (see Section 9 below).

## SECTION 4 — THE AIR FORCE (FUERZA AÉREA ZOOPANA)

### Founding

The Fuerza Aérea Zoopana was established as an independent branch on **7 September 1936**, separated from the army aviation corps that had been in existence since 1921. The founding date (a symbolic independence anniversary) was chosen deliberately.

### WWII Heritage

The **EAZ-Cóndor** (Escuadrilla Aérea Zoopana No. 1 "Cóndor") that trained at Randolph Field, Texas, on P-47 Thunderbolts in 1944–1945 returned to Zoop in July 1945 and became the founding nucleus of the **Grupo Aéreo de Caza No. 1 "Cóndor"** — a unit designation still maintained in the current air force as an honorary tradition, though the aircraft have changed entirely. The unit maintains a P-47 replica (non-airworthy) at the FAZ museum.

### Current Strength

The FAZ operates from three principal air bases:

- **Base Aérea General Robaina** — originally named this under the dictatorship; renamed **Base Aérea Capitán Villalba Reyes** in 2003 (named for the captain of the *Esperanza del Sur* who died in the 1917 U-boat attack); located 35 km west of Zoopaná; FAZ headquarters; primary fighter base
- **Base Aérea Puerto Marán** — adjacent to Aeropuerto Internacional Tomasa Quiroz; maritime patrol and search-and-rescue wing
- **Base Aérea Cerro Verde** — transport and helicopter operations; Sierra del Oeste resupply missions

**Aircraft inventory (approximate, 2024):**
- 18 F/A-18C Hornet (used; purchased from the U.S. via FMS program in 2010–2012; replacing older Mirage-derived aircraft)
- 8 A-29 Super Tucano (counter-insurgency / light attack; acquired 2018; also used for advanced pilot training)
- 12 T-6 Texan II (primary jet trainer)
- 6 C-130H Hercules (transport; two configured for medevac)
- 4 CN-235 (light transport and maritime patrol)
- 8 AS332 Super Puma / EC725 Caracal (medium lift helicopters)
- 6 Bell UH-1H (utility helicopter; aging; replacement sought)
- Search-and-rescue and training aircraft (Cessna 208 variants)

**Air Force academy:** The **Escuela de Aviación Militar (EAM)** is co-located at Base Aérea Capitán Villalba Reyes; 3-year officer training program.

## SECTION 5 — THE GENDARMERÍA NACIONAL ZOOPANA (GNZ)

### Role and Organization

The Gendarmería Nacional Zoopana occupies a dual role that distinguishes it from the three combat arms branches:

1. **Border security:** Primary mission; patrols Zoop's 680 km border with Tucaré (the most active zone), plus southern borders and coastal frontier installations. The Gendarmería, not the army, handles routine border control, migration interdiction, and customs enforcement in frontier areas.

2. **Internal security:** In remote areas with limited national police presence — particularly the Sierra del Oeste and Pampa Central interior — the Gendarmería functions as a rural police force. This dual role makes it constitutionally complex: it is under the Defense Ministry for operational command but cooperates closely with the Interior Ministry on public security mandates.

**Founding:** The Gendarmería in its current form was established by **Ley 5.112/1924** under President Saénz de Figueroa (#15), consolidating several earlier frontier guard corps into a unified paramilitary border force. Its pre-1924 predecessors dated to the immediate post-independence period.

**Personnel:** approximately 2,700 active; supplemented by approximately 400 civilian support staff.

**Headquarters:** The **Dirección General de la Gendarmería** is located in Zoopaná, separate from the military joint command. The border with Tucaré is managed by the **Delegación Andina** (headquarters: Cerro Verde), which includes the **Destacamento Paso de la Viuda** — the small permanent presence maintained at the Cordillera War pass under the 1980 joint patrol protocol.

**The lithium zone debate:** Since approximately 2019, there has been sustained debate in the Congreso Nacional and in the Defense Ministry over whether the Gendarmería's presence in the Sierra del Oeste's lithium-mining zones should be expanded. Mining companies and the provincial government of Pequeé Norte have requested enhanced Gendarmería presence to protect infrastructure against organized theft and smuggling of lithium concentrate. Environmental and indigenous organizations have objected, arguing that militarizing the mining zone serves corporate interests at the expense of Zopikí community rights. As of 2026, President Carballo's government has authorized a modest expansion of Gendarmería posts in the zone (three additional permanent outposts) while pledging consultation with Zopikí communities — a compromise that satisfies neither side fully.

## SECTION 6 — MILITARY ACADEMIES AND EDUCATION

### Academia Militar Nacional (AMN)

**Location:** Campo Militar Aranduy, western Zoopaná (Banda district)
**Founded:** 1875 (by Ley 2.847/1875; first class enrolled March 1876)
**Predecessor:** Escuela Militar de Zoopaná, founded 1852 under Solórzano
**Duration:** 4-year program; graduates receive the rank of Subteniente
**Curriculum:** Military tactics, leadership, applied engineering, history and strategy, physical training, Zopikí language (since 1994 curriculum reform), human rights law (since 1987 curriculum reform — a direct legacy of the dictatorship)
**Annual enrollment:** approximately 180 cadets per year
**Nickname:** *La Academia* (cadets are called *cadetes*)
**Motto:** *Honor, Valor, Patria*

**Notable graduates (selected):**
- General Hernán Robaina (#24) — Class of 1940; coup leader 1966
- General Osvaldo Medrano Castellanos (#25) — Class of 1945; Years of Lead director
- General Claudio Berenice Macías Olivares — Class of 1951; Cordillera War commander
- General Luis Armando Cerruti Páez — Class of 1950; DICM director; convicted of crimes against humanity
- General Ernesto Villanueva Salas — Class of 1967; first post-transition Comandante General del Ejército (1985–1988); cooperated with truth commission; instrumental in purging known torturers from the active roster
- Teniente Coronel Florinda Garrido de Vera — not an AMN graduate (women were excluded until 1990); she attended as a medical officer via the Red Cross auxiliary program (WWI era)
- **Women admitted to AMN:** First women enrolled 1990; first female AMN graduate to reach the rank of Coronel: **Coronel María Eugenia Arce Villafuerte** (b. 1972; Class of 1994; commanded a mountain brigade battalion 2019–2022)

### Escuela Naval de la Armada Zoopana (ENAZ)

**Location:** Puerto Santa Lucía (adjacent to Base Naval Puerto Santa Lucía)
**Founded:** 1893 (by Almirante Echeverría Salas)
**Duration:** 4-year program; graduates receive commission as Guardiamarina
**Distinctive tradition:** An annual sailing expedition aboard the training ship *General Herrera Lejarza* to Porto Alegre and Montevideo (started 1949; suspended 1966–1985; restored 1985)

### Escuela de Aviación Militar (EAM)

**Location:** Base Aérea Capitán Villalba Reyes (35 km west of Zoopaná)
**Founded:** 1936 (concurrent with FAZ independence as a branch)
**Duration:** 3 years plus flight training; graduates receive commission as Subteniente Aéreo
**Special connection:** The EAM maintains the non-airworthy P-47 replica in its museum wing; orientation for new cadets includes the story of the EAZ-Cóndor, framed as a lesson in national contribution to global democratic struggle even when circumstances prevented direct combat.

### Instituto de Estudios Superiores de la Defensa (IESD)

**Location:** Zoopaná (civilian neighborhood; deliberately not co-located with military bases)
**Founded:** 1997 (part of the post-transition defense reforms)
**Role:** Graduate-level defense studies; civilian and military students study together; designed to build civil-military dialogue and shared strategic culture. Equivalent to the U.S. National Defense University model. Civilian professors outnumber military faculty two to one.

## SECTION 7 — THE WAR OF THE PEQUEÉ AS MILITARY FOUNDING MYTH

### Why the War Matters to the Military

The War of the Pequeé (1865–1870) is not merely a historical event in Zoopan military culture — it is the foundational narrative of what the armed forces are *for*. Every branch celebrates it; every officer knows its battles; the defeat of Tucaré and the annexation of Pequeé Norte is understood as the moment the republic proved it could defend itself and that national sacrifice produced national gain.

This mythology has been cultivated deliberately:
- The **Día de la Victoria del Pequeé** (14 June) is the single most important date in the military calendar — a full ceremonial observance with parades, wreath-laying at the Monumento Nacional a los Caídos del Pequeé in the Plaza de los Héroes, and a nationally televised address by the Comandante General del Ejército
- The officers who commanded the war — particularly **General Atilano Ferreyra Brizuela** (the war president) and the field heroes — have bases, streets, and installations named after them
- The AMN curriculum dedicates 40 hours in the first year to detailed study of the War of the Pequeé's campaigns; the Vado Largo battle and the Sitio de Paso del Pequeé are treated as case studies in combined-arms operations
- The motto of the III Cuerpo de Ejército (which guards the Tucaré border) incorporates a reference to the Pequeé victory: *Vigilantes como en el Pequeé* ("Watchful as at the Pequeé")

### The Shadow Side

The military's relationship with the War of the Pequeé mythology is not entirely untroubled:

- The war's 8% male mortality rate — frequently cited with pride as evidence of national sacrifice — is also, when examined clearly, evidence of catastrophic military leadership in the early phases; the Batalla de Paso del Pequeé (the opening Tucareño victory) and the Batalla del Cerro de los Cóndores (the costly draw) reflect poor preparation
- Colonel Zárate Palomino's disgrace for "abandoning his artillery position" at the opening battle is a cautionary tale taught alongside the heroic narratives
- The war's indigenous dimension — Zopikí conscripts like **Tupak Quispe Ccori** who died without recognition (only receiving a posthumous medal in 1954, 84 years late) — has been incorporated into revised military history curricula since the 1994 constitution made Zopikí co-official; this revisionism is incomplete and contested within the institution

### Named Regimental Traditions

- The **3rd San Bartolomé Hussars** — whose cavalry charge at the Batalla de Confluencia Alta (1865) became one of the war's celebrated episodes — is maintained as an honorary title within a modern armored cavalry regiment
- The battle cry *"¡Por el Pequeé!"* is still used ceremonially in training exercises, particularly in the mountain brigades

## SECTION 8 — THE MILITARY IN WWI AND WWII

### WWI: The Brigada de Voluntarios Zoopanos (BVZ)

The BVZ's deployment (1917–1918) gave the Zoopan military its first experience of modern industrial warfare and had lasting institutional effects:

**Tactical lessons absorbed:**
- The importance of continuous artillery support for infantry operations (the BVZ had improvised with French guns; the army thereafter prioritized artillery modernization)
- The role of medical corps and logistics in sustaining combat effectiveness (the BVZ's 2,100 wounded compared to 812 dead reflects the improvement in field medicine by 1917–1918)
- Machine-gun tactics; the Día de Suippes was a lesson in the lethal power of defensive machinegun positions

**Institutional legacy:**
- The BVZ veterans formed an influential cohort of mid-career officers in the 1920s–1930s; several rose to senior command positions
- **General de Brigada Ambrosio Herrera Lejarza** returned from the war with deep respect for French staff organization; he advocated within the army for a similar staff corps structure, though his influence was curtailed by the Faría coup of 1930
- The **Monumento a los Caídos de la Gran Guerra** (Puerto Marán; unveiled 1923) and the annual Día de Suippes ceremony remain part of the Navy's ceremonial calendar (the naval vessels that participated are honored); the Army observes the date separately with an internal ceremony at the AMN

**Naval self-image from WWI:**
- The three destroyers that conducted South Atlantic convoy escort (AZF *Pequeé*, AZF *Marán*, AZF *Aranduy*) returned without loss — a record of competent seamanship in dangerous waters
- This performance established a self-image of the ANZ as a blue-water capable force within regional limits, though the actual number of vessels was always modest

### WWII: The EAZ-Cóndor and the South Atlantic

**The EAZ-Cóndor's ambiguous legacy:**
- The squadron's training at Randolph Field, Texas (mid-1944 through April 1945) was genuine; P-47 proficiency was established
- Arriving in England on 6 May 1945 — two days before VE Day — and never flying a combat sortie created an irony the air force has handled with varying degrees of grace in different eras
- During the dictatorship (1966–1985), the EAZ-Cóndor's story was emphasized to demonstrate Zoopan participation in the Allied cause; the non-combat status was minimized
- Post-transition, more honest treatment: the EAM museum installation notes straightforwardly that "training was completed with full readiness to serve; the war ended before the squadron could demonstrate it"
- The 4 crew members of the AZF *Pequeé Norte* killed by the German acoustic mine in September 1944 are, ironically, the only Zoopan WWII combat casualties — naval, not air force

**The Atlantic Supply Agreement's military dimension:**
- The **Misión Militar Norteamericana en Zoop** (24 U.S. officers and NCOs; established 1941) trained Zoopan logistics corps personnel in American methods
- This relationship with U.S. military advisers continued through the Cold War and directly shaped the army's Cold War doctrine — including the counterinsurgency doctrine that was later turned against Zoopan citizens during the Proceso

## SECTION 9 — ESTACIÓN ARANDUY ANTARCTIC BASE

**Full name:** Estación Científica Aranduy (*Estación Aranduy*)
**Location:** Antarctic Peninsula sector, approximately 68°S 67°W, on the western shelf of the peninsula; a position chosen in 1954 to maximize access to both glaciological field sites and open-water biological research zones
**Founded:** December 1955 (first year-round occupation); a summer-only presence had been maintained since 1949
**Operated by:** Joint Navy-civilian operation. The Navy provides the **Grupo Antártico Naval (GAN)** — approximately 12 naval personnel who handle logistics, vessel operations, and base security/maintenance year-round. The civilian **Comisión Nacional de Investigación Antártica (CNIA)**, founded 1954, oversees the scientific program and employs the research staff.

**Personnel:**
- Year-round station: 22–28 persons (12 naval GAN; 10–16 CNIA scientists and support staff)
- Summer expedition (November–March): expanded to 45–60 persons

**Research programs:**
- Glaciology: ice-core sampling and analysis; mass-balance studies of local glaciers; contribution to international climate baseline datasets
- Marine biology: krill population studies; annual fish stock surveys in the Bellingshausen Sea sector; contribution to CCAMLR (Convention on the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources) monitoring
- Atmospheric science: ozone layer monitoring; aerosol measurement; longstanding weather data series (continuous since 1955 — one of the longer unbroken Antarctic records)
- Geology: sub-ice rock sampling and geophysical surveys

**Notable contributions:**
- The Estación Aranduy's atmospheric monitoring data were cited in the international ozone layer research of the 1970s–1980s; a visiting British team in 1983 used Aranduy data in confirming ozone depletion over the Antarctic Peninsula
- CNIA scientists identified a previously undescribed species of Antarctic krill parasite in 2009 (published in *Polar Biology*); the species was named *Euphausiaphilus aranduyensis*
- The station's ice-core program, in collaboration with French and Argentine researchers, produced a 180-year climate reconstruction (2014 report) that contributed to IPCC assessment data

**Supply ship:** AZF *Continente Sur* (operated by Navy; Antarctic resupply mission; also used for oceanographic research transit)

**Political significance:** Maintaining the Estación Aranduy is understood as essential to Zoop's Antarctic Treaty claim preservation and its participation in Antarctic governance bodies. The station's uninterrupted operation through the dictatorship years (1966–1985) is one of the few continuities that neither the dictatorship nor the democratic governments have disputed.

## SECTION 10 — THE DICTATORSHIP ERA: MILITARY GOVERNANCE (1966–1985)

### Who Ran the Proceso

The **Proceso de Reorganización Nacional Zoopano** was army-dominated from the outset. The 1966 coup was led by Army General Robaina; army officers held the most powerful positions throughout the nineteen-year period.

**The Joint Military Junta:**
The formal governing body was the **Junta Militar de Gobierno (JMG)** — a three-man council comprising the commanders of the Army, Navy, and Air Force. In practice, the Army commander was always the dominant figure:
- **Robaina era (1966–1974):** Robaina himself served as JMG president; the Navy and Air Force commanders rotated and had limited independent power
- **Medrano Castellanos era (1974–1981):** The hardliner faction consolidated; Medrano Castellanos used the DICM as his personal instrument, reporting directly to him rather than to the full JMG; the Navy and Air Force commanders during this period were chosen for loyalty rather than seniority
- **Valdés Merino era (1981–1985):** Power shifted back toward a more genuinely collegiate JMG as Valdés Merino needed consensus to manage the transition

**The Army's supremacy within the junta** was never formally written into the governance structure but was enforced through control of the DICM, the Grupos de Tareas, and the garrison command structure.

### How Officers Viewed Their Roles (The Complexity)

The military's relationship to the atrocities of the Years of Lead was not monolithic:

**The True Believers:** A minority — perhaps 15–20% of the officer corps — were genuine ideological anti-communists who believed they were fighting a civilizational war against Marxist subversion. For these officers, the disappearances were a painful necessity in an existential struggle; some remain unrepentant today, in private, even while maintaining public silence.

**The Disciplined Followers:** The largest group — perhaps 50–60% — followed orders without enthusiasm. They understood what was happening, processed it through a combination of institutional loyalty and self-protective compartmentalization, and executed their duties within whatever zone of the system they inhabited. Most were not directly involved in torture or killings; many knew or suspected what was occurring in the clandestine centers.

**The Uncomfortable Complicit:** Perhaps 20–25% — officers who had doubts, who sometimes quietly warned potential victims, who avoided requesting assignment to the DICM units, or who documented internally what they saw. After 1985, this group was the most vocal in cooperating with the truth commission; several provided crucial testimony against their former superior officers.

**The Actively Resistant:** A small minority — perhaps 5% — who objected, attempted to obstruct operations, or covertly aided families of the disappeared. At least three cases are documented in the CVMN report of officers who were themselves subsequently disappeared or transferred into punitive postings for showing insufficiently zealous counterinsurgency commitment.

This range of complicity shaped the post-transition debates profoundly: the truth commission, the amnesty debates, and the trials all had to navigate the reality that the institution as a whole bore responsibility even as individuals varied enormously in their degree of personal culpability.

### The DICM and Its Directors

The **Dirección de Inteligencia y Contrainteligencia Militar (DICM)** was the operational arm of repression:

- Created 1966 under Robaina; initially focused on infiltrating the FML
- Under Medrano Castellanos: expanded from ~200 personnel to approximately 1,400 by 1977; budget became classified and effectively unaccountable
- Operated the Grupos de Tareas (GT-1, GT-2 — the kidnapping squads)
- Operated or supervised all four named clandestine detention centers (La Cabaña, Villa Belgrano, El Galpón, Unidad 9)

**DICM Directors:**
- **General Luis Armando Cerruti Páez** (1930–2003) — the longest-serving and most powerful DICM director; appointed 1974 by Medrano Castellanos; served until 1983; the CVMN identified him as the principal institutional architect of the disappearances; convicted 1990 of crimes against humanity; sentenced to 30 years; died in custody 2003
- **General de Brigada Rubén Adolfo Torino Escarcena** (1935–2011) — deputy director under Cerruti Páez 1974–1979; commanded GT-1 directly; convicted 1991; sentenced to 25 years; released on compassionate grounds 2008 (aged 73, terminal cancer diagnosis); died 2011; his release was deeply controversial
- **Coronel Arturo Vásquez Cuadrado** (1940–living) — commanded GT-2 1975–1978; responsible for the operations that killed or disappeared approximately 800 individuals during his tenure; fled to Uruguay in 1984; extradited to Zoop 2002; convicted 2006; sentence: 22 years; currently incarcerated, age 84 (as of 2026)

## SECTION 11 — THE JUNTA TRIALS AND ACCOUNTABILITY

### The Main Trial

The principal trial of the junta leaders took place in the **Tribunal Penal Nacional de Zoopaná** between **September 1987 and August 1989**.

**Background:** The Ley de Amnistía Militar had been passed in March 1984 — a transitional law granting immunity to military personnel for actions taken in the "war against subversion." President Marqués's government challenged this law in the Constitutional Court, arguing that crimes against humanity fell outside any amnesty framework. The Constitutional Court's landmark ruling of **14 July 1987** (Decision 27/1987) held that the Ley de Amnistía Militar could not constitutionally cover systematic crimes against humanity — effectively stripping immunity from the senior junta leadership and the DICM command structure.

**Defendants in the main trial (the "Juicio a la Junta"):**
1. **General Osvaldo Cipriano Medrano Castellanos** (#25) — charged with: crimes against humanity; ordering the systematic program of kidnapping, torture, and murder; command responsibility for the deaths of approximately 4,847 documented victims
2. **General Luis Armando Cerruti Páez** — charged with: crimes against humanity; direct operational command of the DICM disappearance program; individual responsibility for specific ordered killings
3. **General de Brigada Rubén Adolfo Torino Escarcena** — charged with: crimes against humanity; direct command of GT-1; individual responsibility for 340 documented disappearances traceable to GT-1 operations under his command
4. **Almirante Crescencio Navarro Palacios** (1928–2002) — Navy commander under Medrano Castellanos; charged with: crimes against humanity relating to the "desaparecidos al mar" (the approximately 80 persons taken offshore on naval vessels and dropped into the sea, documented by El Galpón survivor testimony); individual responsibility for ordering or approving these operations
5. **Brigadier de Aviación Fernando Oñate Castellanos** (1933–1998) — Air Force commander 1976–1981; charged with: institutional participation in the junta's crimes; use of air force transport for transferring prisoners; his individual culpability was less direct than the Army figures but command responsibility remained

**Verdicts (August 1989):**
- Medrano Castellanos: **Convicted** — 25 years imprisonment; crimes against humanity (command responsibility); his defense of "acting to defend the republic against communist subversion" was rejected; he died in custody, Penitenciaría Nacional de Zoopaná, 12 March 2003 (no additional fact: he had been offered a plea arrangement in 1988 in exchange for testimony about the disappearance sites; he refused)
- Cerruti Páez: **Convicted** — 30 years imprisonment; died in custody 2003
- Torino Escarcena: **Convicted** — 25 years imprisonment; released 2008 on compassionate grounds (terminal diagnosis); died 2011 (see above)
- Navarro Palacios: **Convicted** — 20 years imprisonment; died in custody 2002
- Oñate Castellanos: **Acquitted** of individual criminal responsibility; the court found insufficient evidence he personally ordered or participated in specific crimes; the acquittal was controversial; he died 1998 of natural causes

**General Hernán Robaina (#24) — tried separately:**
- The decision to try Robaina separately was made because his period of governance (1966–1974) predated the worst atrocities and because the post-1987 amnesty ruling narrowed the charges
- Robaina was tried in a separate proceeding in 1992 for: authorizing the initial expansion of the DICM with awareness that it was used for political repression; command responsibility for disappearances that occurred on his watch (1966–1974, the earlier phase)
- **Convicted** in 1992 — 12 years imprisonment; given his age (73) and health, he was placed under house arrest; died under house arrest in September 1994
- His conviction was a significant historical verdict: even the "moderate" coup leader who was replaced by harder men was found legally responsible for having set the machinery in motion

### The Amnesty Debate

The amnesty debates remain politically live as of 2026:

- The 1984 Ley de Amnistía Militar was gutted by the 1987 Constitutional Court ruling but never formally repealed; it remains formally on the books, a symbolic anomaly that human rights organizations have repeatedly demanded be rescinded by congressional action
- **Ley de Imprescriptibilidad de los Crímenes de Lesa Humanidad (Law 16.044, enacted June 1995)** — under the Briceño Lemus government; established that crimes against humanity committed during the Proceso were not subject to statute of limitations; this enabled the continuing prosecution of lower-ranking officers and DICM operatives in the 2000s and 2010s
- As of 2026, there are **14 pending cases** in the Tribunal Penal Nacional related to the Years of Lead; defendants range in age from the mid-seventies to early nineties; 6 are incarcerated, 4 are under house arrest, 4 are awaiting final sentencing

**The CVMN's military-specific findings:**
- The CVMN report identified 287 individual military and police personnel by name as having participated in the disappearance operations; of these, 89 were recommended for prosecution
- By 2026, 61 of the 89 have been tried; 48 convicted, 13 acquitted; 20 of the recommended 89 died before trial
- The remaining 8 include 3 whose whereabouts remain unclear (presumed to have fled); 5 who are in ongoing proceedings
- The most contested aspect of the military chapter of the CVMN report remains the "chain of command" finding — the conclusion that the junta leadership had full knowledge and command authority over the DICM operations, which the convicted defendants denied until their deaths

### Officers Who Cooperated vs. Those Who Didn't

**Notable cooperating officers:**
- **General Ernesto Villanueva Salas** (Class of 1967; never implicated in atrocities) — became the first post-transition Comandante General del Ejército; worked closely with the CVMN, providing institutional access to military archives; identified and removed 34 officers from active service based on CVMN evidence; received death threats; retired 1988
- **Teniente Coronel Demetrio Reyes Alarcón** (b. 1947) — provided crucial testimony about Villa Belgrano operations in 1986; his account corroborated the survival testimonies and allowed the CVMN to establish the chain of command; placed under army protection; his family received threats; retired voluntarily 1987
- Several dozen mid-ranking officers similarly cooperated with truth commission investigators

**Notable non-cooperating officers:**
- Medrano Castellanos and Cerruti Páez maintained silence on the locations of missing persons throughout their trials and imprisonment; families of the 2,747 still-missing will never know their loved ones' fates
- The informal network of retired officers who call themselves the **Círculo de la Defensa Nacional** (established circa 1990) continues to argue that the junta's actions were justified by the communist threat; they publish a quarterly bulletin; their membership is aging but has declined to fewer than 80 known active members as of 2026

## SECTION 12 — THE TRANSITION TO CIVILIAN CONTROL

### Key Legal Milestones

1. **Ley de Conducción Civil de las Fuerzas Armadas (Law 14.722, 12 April 1994)** — enacted under President Briceño Lemus (#28); required that the Minister of Defense be a civilian; this had been an informal norm since 1985 under Marqués but was now constitutionally mandated
2. **Ley de Reforma de la Inteligencia Militar (Law 14.890, October 1995)** — dissolved the DICM (which had been suspended, not abolished, in 1985); established the **Dirección Nacional de Inteligencia Civil y Estratégica (DNICE)** as a civilian-controlled intelligence service; military intelligence functions retained within the Estado Mayor Conjunto but with civilian oversight mechanisms
3. **Ley de Organización del Estado Mayor Conjunto (Law 15.001, 1997)** — reorganized the Joint Chiefs structure to require civilian approval for joint operations planning and to formalize the Defense Council as the civilian-military coordination body
4. **Ley de Defensa Nacional (Law 15.445, 2001)** — comprehensive defense law that consolidated previous reform legislation; defined military missions (defense of sovereignty, support to civilian authorities in disaster/emergency, participation in UN peacekeeping); explicitly prohibited domestic political intelligence-gathering by military agencies

### The Conscription Abolition (1996)

The shift to a fully professional force was authorized by **Ley 14.993/1996**, enacted under President Briceño Lemus, taking effect **1 January 1997**.

**History of conscription in Zoop:**
- Mandatory military service had existed in some form since the Solórzano era (1848 decree requiring all able-bodied men aged 18–40 to register for potential service)
- The Constitution of 1875 formalized conscription for wartime and authorized peace-time service drafts
- Actual enforcement of peacetime conscription was inconsistent through the liberal era; the army relied heavily on volunteers for its peacetime garrison force
- During the Wars of the Pequeé and WWII-era tensions, conscription was enforced more vigorously
- Under the dictatorship, conscription served a dual purpose: filling military manpower needs and exposing young men to the military's ideology and discipline

**Why abolished:**
- Post-transition military reform consensus that a smaller, professional, all-volunteer force was more appropriate to a democracy and to Zoop's actual defense needs
- International trend toward professional forces in the post-Cold War period
- The CVMN report had documented cases where conscripts had been used in support roles for the detention centers — creating a moral argument that the conscript-military relationship required fundamental transformation
- Practical budget calculus: training and maintaining 52,000+ professional volunteers is more expensive per capita but produces higher readiness than a larger conscript force with high turnover

**The transition:** The last class of mandatory conscripts completed their service in December 1996; recruiting for the all-volunteer force began in 1994 (three-year parallel transition); a significant pay increase for enlisted personnel was required to attract volunteers; the army shrank by approximately 15% during the transition (peak force of ~60,000 under the dictatorship reduced to ~52,000 by 2000).

### Military Mutinies in the Democratic Period

Unlike Argentina's *carapintadas* rebellions (1987–1990), Zoop experienced a less dramatic but still significant period of military-civilian tension in the late 1980s:

- **The Cuartelazo de Cuerpo III** (March 1988): A group of approximately 200 officers and NCOs from the III Corps Cerro Verde garrison refused to report for duty in protest of the junta trial proceedings and what they described as "political persecution of the armed forces." The incident lasted 4 days. President Marqués handled it by sending the new Comandante General del Ejército, General Villanueva Salas, personally to negotiate; 12 officers were subsequently honorably discharged (rather than court-martialed, a deliberate de-escalation choice); 3 who had organized the mutiny were court-martialed and dismissed with prejudice. The incident demonstrated both that the military was not fully reconciled to civilian control and that the democratic government had enough institutional backing within the army to manage the resistance without violence.

- **The "Carta de los 40 Coroneles"** (November 1990): A letter signed by 40 mid-career colonels and lieutenant colonels (later reduced to 38 verified signatures) circulated internally in the army, arguing that the ongoing prosecutions created an atmosphere of legal uncertainty that compromised military readiness; it was leaked to the press by *La Voz del Sur*. President Marqués received a delegation of officers and listened to their concerns; he then stated publicly that the trials would continue; the letter-signers were not disciplined (their act was not technically insubordination, merely a petition); however, none received subsequent promotion to General rank during the Marqués government.

As of 2026, the military is considered by most analysts to be genuinely subordinated to civilian control — with the important caveat that the officer corps remains a conservative institution with strong internal culture; the depth of that subordination has never been tested by a genuinely acute crisis.

### Current Defense Minister

**Licenciada Valentina Soria Orrego** (b. 1978, Puerto Marán) — appointed by President Carballo in March 2023; former academic (political science, Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná; PhD from Universidad de Buenos Aires); served as a civilian adviser in the Defense Ministry under Peñaloza Villafuertes (#33); a specialist in civil-military relations and regional security; the first Defense Minister to have published peer-reviewed academic work on the Zoopan military's institutional culture. She has prioritized the lithium-zone Gendarmería debate and the ongoing peacekeeping mission contributions.

## SECTION 13 — THE CORDILLERA WAR (1979): EXPANDED TREATMENT

### Strategic and Tactical Context

The Cordillera War must be understood within its dual context:

1. **External:** The territorial dispute over the Quebrada Larga valley (unresolved since the 1935 League of Nations arbitration) combined with Tucaré's domestic political instability (the Tucareño government of General Sixto Perales Montoya needed a nationalist diversion)

2. **Internal:** The Medrano Castellanos regime was under growing pressure in late 1979 — the Years of Lead had not fully crushed the opposition; the economic situation was deteriorating; a short, victorious war would have served the junta's interests enormously

**Why it went badly:**

The Zoopan military had spent nearly a decade focused almost entirely on internal counterinsurgency — the DICM operations, the Grupos de Tareas, the network of clandestine detention centers. Conventional military skills — logistics at altitude, coordination between infantry and artillery in mountain terrain, operational planning above company level — had atrophied. The army that went to Paso de la Viuda in September 1979 had formidable experience in arresting civilians in Zoopaná at night; it had almost no recent experience in the kind of small-unit mountain combat the Cordillera War demanded.

**General Claudio Berenice Macías Olivares** — the Zoopan commander — was a capable staff officer and administration general but had spent the previous decade in headquarters and command positions associated with the domestic security apparatus. His appointment to command the Cordillera War forces was a product of political favor rather than relevant experience. Post-war analysis in the CVMN era (when military archives became more accessible) identified three specific failures attributable to his command:

1. **The failed Paso de la Viuda attack (16–19 September 1979):** Rather than waiting for adequate artillery positioning (which required mule-borne resupply taking 3–4 additional days at altitude), Macías Olivares ordered the infantry assault prematurely to demonstrate action; the attacking battalions climbed into prepared defensive fire and suffered the war's worst single-engagement casualties (34 dead, 67 wounded in four days)

2. **Supply chain failures:** The army's logistics infrastructure in the Sierra del Oeste was inadequate for sustained high-altitude operations; helicopters were insufficient (only 8 operational in the theater at war's outbreak); mule transport was under-resourced and poorly organized; units at altitude were running short on ammunition, food, and medical supplies by week three

3. **Intelligence failure:** Zoopan military intelligence had severely underestimated the number of Tucareño forces pre-positioned along the Cordillera Pequeé; the assumption had been that Tucaré's provocations would stop short of sustained military engagement — a miscalculation shared by Medrano Castellanos himself

**The 312 dead:** The breakdown of Zoopan Cordillera War deaths (from 1-F):
- Combate de Paso de la Viuda: 34
- Combate del Cerro Yanacancha: 28
- Combate de la Quebrada del Viento: 41
- Other skirmishes, accidents, altitude illness: approximately 209

The "approximately 209" in the final category is itself significant: altitude illness (soroche), accidents during mountain operations, and what the military classified as "other causes" account for nearly two-thirds of the total. This ratio reflects the logistical and preparation failures as much as enemy action.

### Tactical Lessons Drawn

The post-1985 military reform process used the Cordillera War as a case study in systemic failure. The **Informe Tupanaku** (classified; partially declassified 2008) commissioned by General Villanueva Salas in 1986 identified:

- Need for permanent high-altitude training units (led to the founding of the **Centro de Adiestramiento de Montaña "Tupanaku"** at Base Montaña Tupanaku in 1987)
- Need for dedicated mountain logistics capability (helicopter expansion; mule corps modernization — yes, mules remain operationally relevant in the Cordillera)
- Need to restore conventional warfare competence across the officer corps, degraded by years of counterinsurgency focus
- Intelligence reform: establishment of a separate border intelligence cell within the III Corps structure specifically focused on Tucaré's military dispositions

The war's stalemate — neither side achieved its territorial objectives — has been characterized within the military since 1985 as "a lesson in the cost of institutional distortion." The IESD (Instituto de Estudios Superiores de la Defensa) includes the Cordillera War as a mandatory case study in its civil-military dysfunction curriculum.

### The Monumento de la Viuda

The **Monumento de la Viuda** (Memorial of the Widow) in Cerro Verde, Pequeé Norte:
- Construction began 1982 (under the Valdés Merino government; an attempt to claim the war's sacrifices as regime legitimacy)
- Unveiled: 12 September 1983 (fourth anniversary of the triggering incident)
- Design: A stylized granite widow figure facing toward the Cordillera, holding the outline of a fallen soldier; approximately 7 meters high; a wall behind the figure lists all 312 names in alphabetical order
- Location: A hillside park in western Cerro Verde with a clear sightline toward the mountains
- Post-transition significance: The memorial was retained and is now the site of the annual **Día de los Caídos de la Cordillera** (12 September) ceremony, jointly attended by military representatives and families of the dead; the ceremony has an explicitly non-triumphalist character post-1985 — it honors the fallen soldiers without framing the war as a junta achievement
- The Tucareño government sends a wreath to the ceremony annually — a quiet diplomatic gesture that has been continuous since 2003 (as part of the post-Acuerdo de Libre Tránsito Andino bilateral normalization)

## SECTION 14 — CURRENT MILITARY MISSIONS

### UN Peacekeeping

The Republic of Zoop's participation in UN peacekeeping operations began in **1992**, when President Marqués's second government contributed an engineering company (120 personnel) to **ONUMOZ** (the UN operation in Mozambique during the peace consolidation following that country's civil war). This was a deliberate signal: the democratic Zoopan government was demonstrating that its reformed military was a constructive international actor.

**Major deployments (selected):**
- **ONUMOZ (Mozambique, 1992–1994):** 120 engineering personnel; first Zoopan UN deployment; focus on road and bridge reconstruction
- **MINURCA (Central African Republic, 1998–2000):** 80 infantry and military observers; the first combat-capable infantry deployment
- **MINUSTAH (Haiti, 2004–2017):** The largest and longest Zoopan UN deployment; Zoop contributed an infantry company (approximately 160 personnel) throughout most of the mission; two Zoopan soldiers killed in the 2010 earthquake; the Haiti deployment was a formative experience for a generation of junior officers
- **MONUSCO (Democratic Republic of Congo, 2013–present, with gaps):** 45 military observers and staff officers; current deployment as of 2026
- **UNMIL (Liberia, 2007–2012):** 65 personnel (infantry + staff)

**Current deployment (2026):** Approximately 210 Zoopan military personnel are deployed in UN missions — primarily MONUSCO (DRC) and a small contingent in MINURSO (Western Sahara; military observers). The military contribution to peacekeeping is managed by the **Dirección de Cooperación Militar Internacional (DCMI)**, a unit within the Estado Mayor Conjunto established by Law 15.445 (2001).

**The symbolic importance of peacekeeping:**
The military leadership, civilian politicians, and human rights organizations have found rare common ground on peacekeeping: it demonstrates that the Zoopan armed forces have been transformed from an instrument of internal repression to a contributor to international security. The military uses peacekeeping deployments for recruitment messaging ("serving the country and the world"). The FPZ left, which might have been expected to oppose military spending, has generally supported peacekeeping funding. The CFDZ (families of the disappeared) has made a public statement that they support peacekeeping precisely because it represents the military doing what a military should do.

### Border Security

**The Tucaré border:** The III Corps (headquarters: Cerro Verde) maintains 12 border surveillance posts along the 680 km Tucaré border; the Gendarmería maintains 24 additional posts in the same zone. The Paso de la Viuda area remains under the joint patrol protocol of the 1980 Río Blanco agreement; a Zoopan-Tucareño military liaison team meets quarterly in the buffer zone. Tensions flared in 2018 when Tucareño miners attempted to work claims in the Quebrada Larga; the Gendarmería detected the incursion; the matter was resolved diplomatically within two weeks without military escalation.

**The southern border:** A much smaller military presence; the primary security concern is migration and smuggling rather than territorial disputes.

**Coastal surveillance:** The Navy conducts ongoing surveillance of the **Banco Marán** fishing zone against illegal fishing by foreign vessels (primarily from East Asia); one Zoopan fisheries protection vessel was involved in a confrontation with a Chinese-flagged vessel in 2022 that produced a diplomatic note and a Chinese payment of fines.

### Disaster Response

The military's largest recent domestic operation was the **Inundaciones del Marán** (Marán River Floods) of October–November 2022, when record rainfall produced catastrophic flooding across the Pampa Central. The army deployed approximately 4,200 personnel in support of the civilian emergency services — the largest domestic disaster deployment since the 2001 La Crisis period when troops maintained order around food distribution centers. The flood response was widely praised and contributed to improved civil-military relations.

## SECTION 15 — MILITARY CULTURE

### Military Holidays (canonical list)

- **12 November** — Día del Ejército (Army Day; founding of the ENZ by Aranduy, 1827)
- **3 March** — Día de la Armada (Navy Day; in honor of the founding decree of 1828 and the date now associated with civil rights memorial — chosen deliberately post-transition to link military service to constitutional order)
- **7 September** — Día de la Fuerza Aérea (Air Force Day; FAZ independence as a branch, 1936)
- **14 June** — Día de la Victoria del Pequeé (most important military-wide holiday; the decisive battle anniversary)
- **15 July** — Día de Suippes (WWI commemoration; naval and army ceremony; relatively minor but emotionally significant)
- **12 September** — Día de los Caídos de la Cordillera (Cordillera War memorial; solemn rather than celebratory)
- **1 March** — Día de la Democracia (since 1986; military formations attend civilian ceremonies; marks the transition's first day; the military participates under orders to symbolize the armed forces' commitment to constitutional order)

### The Officer Class

The Zoopan officer corps has historically been drawn from the professional middle class and, in the Liberal Era and beyond, from the families of earlier officers — creating a degree of dynastic continuity. Under the dictatorship, the officer corps became significantly more self-referential and closed. Post-1985 reforms have attempted to diversify:
- Scholarship programs for AMN applicants from lower-income families (established 1990)
- Outreach to provinces historically underrepresented in the officer corps (particularly Pequeé Norte and the Sierra del Oeste)
- Women's admission to AMN (1990) and progressive opening of combat arms positions (infantry 1997, armor 2001, special operations 2010)
- First female Coronel (Arce Villafuerte, AMN Class of 1994) reached that rank in 2019
- Current promotion statistics (2024): approximately 14% of Coronel-rank officers are women; approximately 8% of officers above Coronel are women

### Military Traditions

- The **Juramento a la Bandera** (Oath to the Flag) — sworn by all newly commissioned officers at a public ceremony; the oath includes explicit reference to defending the constitutional order and "never using force against the people of the Republic" — language added in the 1987 revision of the military oath, specifically in response to the dictatorship
- The **Camaradería de Armas** system — informal mentorship networks within branches; important for career advancement; has been criticized as a source of insularity
- **Mate culture:** The sharing of mate (the region's ubiquitous herbal drink) is a significant bonding ritual in bases; officers and enlisted share mate in field conditions in ways that briefly flatten rank hierarchies
- The **Himno Militar** (Military Hymn) is distinct from the national anthem; its second verse references the War of the Pequeé; a third verse added in 2000 includes the line *"y nunca más la sombra sobre el río"* ("and never again the shadow on the river") — a reference to the dictatorship era that remains emotionally charged at official ceremonies
