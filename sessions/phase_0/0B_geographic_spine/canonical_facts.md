## SECTION 1 — The 14 Provinces + Autonomous District of Zoopaná

**Population accounting note:** All 14 provinces + the Autonomous District together
total approximately 14.30 million. The Autonomous District's population (3.55M)
encompasses the city of Zoopaná (3.4M) plus surrounding commuter municipalities.

### Autonomous District of Zoopaná
*(Zopikí-derived name — etymology to be confirmed by Session 7-A)*

```
Province: Autonomous District of Zoopaná (Distrito Autónomo de Zoopaná)
Capital city: Zoopaná (serves as national capital; the District has no separate
              provincial capital — the city IS the district seat)
Population: 3,550,000
Area: 2,800 km²
Region: mixed (coastal fringe + Pampa Central transition)
Economic character: national government, financial services, universities,
                    light manufacturing, media, telecommunications
Political alignment (historic): Unitarios stronghold (centralist power base)
Political alignment (modern): FPZ stronghold (urban progressive vote dominates)
Established as district: 1862 (carved from the then-Provincia de la Costa at
                         independence settlement; granted Autonomous status 1994)
Notable fact: The district boundaries were deliberately kept small to prevent
              Zoopaná's weight from completely dominating federal elections —
              a compromise that remains contested to this day.
```

## SECTION 3 — River Systems

---

### Río Marán
*(Zopikí-derived name; etymology to be confirmed by Session 7-A; the
national anthem "Hijos del Marán" names the river explicitly)*

```
Source: Glacial lakes and snowmelt on the eastern face of the Cordillera
        Tupanaku, within Provincia de Río Hondo (Sierra del Oeste),
        at approximately 3,900 m altitude. The conventional headwaters
        are taken to be the confluence of the Arroyo Cóndor and the
        Arroyo Helado, near the settlement of Nacimiento del Marán.

Length: 1,540 km (from headwaters confluence to Atlantic mouth)

Major tributaries:
  1. Río Arroyo Grande — joins from the southwest near San Bartolomé
     del Pequé; length ~320 km; drains the southern Sierra del Oeste
  2. Río Pequé — joins from the northwest at the town of Confluencia;
     length ~480 km; see separate entry below
  3. Río Canela — joins from the north in Provincia de Candelaria;
     length ~210 km; drains the subtropical northern highlands
  4. Río Salado del Centro — joins from the west in Llanuras del Centro;
     intermittent in dry years; length ~180 km; saline in lower reaches
  5. Río Quilmes — joins from the south in Quilmes del Sur;
     length ~260 km; drains the southern pampa
  6. Arroyo de las Llanuras — joins near Corrientes del Marán;
     small but historically significant as a navigation marker;
     length ~95 km

Major dams:
  1. Represa Mariano Aranduy — upper Río Marán, Provincia de Río Hondo;
     built 1968–1974; largest dam in Zoop; 28% of national electricity;
     reservoir (Lago Aranduy) is ~210 km²
  2. Represa de la Confluencia — mid-river, built 1952–1958;
     irrigation and flood control; Provincia de San Bartolomé
  3. Represa Las Totoras — lower-middle river, built 1988–1993;
     primarily flood control for the coastal plain; Provincia de Maraná
  4. Represa Pequeé Alta — on the Río Arroyo Grande tributary rather
     than the main Marán channel; built 1979–1984 (construction began
     during the Cordillera War year; a point of dark historical irony
     noted by historians); Provincia de Las Cumbres

Ports on the river (navigable from the sea ~820 km upstream):
  1. Puerto Marán — mouth; major ocean port
  2. Villa del Río — 180 km upstream; secondary river port; bulk grain
  3. Corrientes del Marán — 390 km upstream; navigable limit for
     oceangoing vessels; major grain-loading terminal
  4. Confluencia — 510 km upstream (where Río Pequé joins); river barge
     terminus; smaller vessels only above this point
  5. Hondo — 680 km upstream; small inland river port; dam tourism

Mouth: Meets the Atlantic at Puerto Marán; the river mouth forms the
       Bahía de Maraná, a broad sheltered estuary. The river deposits
       enough sediment that the harbor requires annual dredging.
       Zopikí-derived name for the bay.
```

### Río Pequé
*(Zopikí-derived name; etymology to be confirmed by Session 7-A)*

```
Source: Glacial and spring waters in the northern Sierra del Oeste,
        Provincia de Pequeé Norte, at approximately 3,200 m altitude;
        headwaters lie within 40 km of the Zoopan-Tucareño border;
        the river flows southeast through the disputed Pequeé valley
        before entering the Pampa Central.

Length: 480 km (headwaters to confluence with Río Marán)

Joins Río Marán: At the town of Confluencia, Provincia de San
                 Bartolomé, approximately 510 km from the Atlantic
                 coast.

War of the Pequé significance: The disputed stretch was the
  upper 180 km of the river — from the headwaters to the
  settlement then called Paso del Pequé (now part of Provincia
  de Pequeé Norte). The República de Tucaré claimed the river
  formed the natural border; Zoop claimed the border ran along
  the ridge of the Cordillera Pequeé to the west of the river.
  Zoop's military victory in 1870 settled the line in Zoop's
  favor, placing the entire river within Zoopan territory.
  The town of Paso del Pequé was renamed Cerro Verde by the
  Zoopan administration in 1873 (the original Tucareño settlement
  name is not preserved in official records).
```

## SECTION 4 — Mountain Geography (Sierra del Oeste)

```
Named ranges within the Sierra del Oeste:

  1. Cordillera Tupanaku
     Location: Southernmost and highest range; runs roughly
               north-south through Provincias de Río Hondo and
               Las Cumbres; forms the core spine of the Sierra
               del Oeste. Contains Zoop's highest peaks.
     Character: Glaciated, rugged; source of the Río Marán.
     Zopikí-derived name (Tupanaku = "the great summit").

  2. Cordillera Pequeé
     Location: Northern range; runs north-south through
               Provincia de Pequeé Norte; forms the disputed
               border with República de Tucaré to the west.
     Character: Lower than Tupanaku; drier, mineral-rich;
                lithium salares in its eastern rain shadow.
     Name: Zopikí-derived (Pequeé = "high crossing").

  3. Sierras del Río Blanco

## SECTION 5 — The Northwestern Neighbor: República de Tucaré

### CRITICAL DECISION — LOCKED IN BY SESSION 0-B

**The unnamed "northwestern neighbor" and "western neighbor" referenced
throughout the seed document and Zoop Bible is hereby named:**

> **República de Tucaré**
> *(pronounced too-kah-REH; adjective/demonym: Tucareño / Tucareña)*

---

```
Official name: República de Tucaré

Capital city: Altavar
  (Spanish-language; "high plain river" — a compound of Spanish
   alto/alta and a regional indigenous root vara; located in a
   high valley on the western face of the shared Andean range)

Approximate location relative to Zoop:
  Tucaré lies to the northwest of Zoop, sharing a border of
  approximately 680 km along the Cordillera Pequeé and the
  upper Río Pequeé drainage. Tucaré is a mountainous, landlocked
  country (or has only a small Pacific coastal strip) — it has
  historically sought trade access through Zoopan Atlantic ports,
  which is an underlying economic driver of the border conflicts.
  Tucaré occupies the western Andean slopes and an Altiplano
  region; it is culturally distinct from Zoop (different
  indigenous heritage, distinct Spanish regional dialect).

Size and population (approximate):
  Area: ~220,000 km² (slightly larger than Zoop)
  Population: ~8.5 million
  Economic character: mining (silver, tin, copper), Altiplano
                      agriculture (quinoa, potatoes), llama
                      herding, some Andean tourism; poorer than
                      Zoop in per-capita terms

Government: Federal republic with a strong presidential system;
            historically prone to military coups; currently a
            civilian democracy, though the military retains
            significant political influence.

The three wars with Zoop:

  War 1 — The War of the Pequeé (1865–1870):
    Cause: Dispute over the upper Río Pequeé basin —
           Tucaré claimed the river as its eastern border;
           Zoop asserted sovereignty over the mineral-rich
           uplands east of the river. Silver and copper deposits
           in the Cordillera Pequeé were the primary economic
           motive.
    Outcome: Zoop wins; annexes the Provincia de Pequeé Norte;
             the Río Pequeé becomes entirely Zoopan. Tucaré loses
             its closest route to Zoopan Atlantic ports.
             Roughly 8% of Zoop's adult male population killed.
    Treaty: Treaty of Pequeé City (1870) — signed in Cerro Verde,
            which Zoopan forces had renamed from its Tucareño
            name. Tucaré acknowledges Zoopan sovereignty;
            retains theoretical right of free navigation on
            the lower Río Marán for commercial vessels.

  War 2 — Guerra del Cerro Verde (1932–1935):
    Cause: Renewed dispute over mineral rights in the Cerro Verde
           highlands; Tucaré, destabilized by the Great Depression
           and a 1931 coup, sought to renegotiate the 1870 border.
           Zoopan military junta (Faría government) responded
           with force.
    Outcome: Inconclusive; ended by international arbitration
             (League of Nations, 1935); border unchanged; small
             compensation payment to Tucaré for mineral access
             rights; estimated 2,200 Zoopan and 1,800 Tucareño
             military dead.
    Note: The war's inconclusive nature bred resentment on both
          sides; in Tucaré it is called the "Guerra Interrumpida"
          (the Interrupted War), and revanchist sentiment lingered
          into the Cold War era.

  War 3 — The Cordillera War (1979):
    Cause: Tucaré's military government (1975–1983) attempted to
           seize the Paso de la Viuda under cover of a border
           survey dispute; the pass would have given Tucaré a
           strategic position overlooking the Zoopan valley below.
           Both countries were under military dictatorship;
           international attention was limited.
    Outcome: Stalemate; ceasefire 30 September 1979; 312 Zoopan
             dead; estimated 280 Tucareño dead; Paso de la Viuda
             placed under joint patrol protocol. The military's
             poor performance damaged the Zoopan junta's prestige
             and is considered one of the triggers of the 1985
             democratic transition.
    Treaty: Acuerdo de Cese de Fuego de Río Blanco (1980) —
            the ceasefire agreement, signed in Río Blanco;
            Tucaré has never formally ratified the full border
            demarcation, which remains an unresolved diplomatic
            irritant.

Current relationship status:
  "Strained but functional" (per seed document).
  Specific details:
  - Diplomatic relations normalized in 1988 (both countries
    returned to democracy)
  - A bilateral trade agreement (Acuerdo de Libre Tránsito
    Andino, 1997) allows duty-reduced goods flow; Tucaré
    exports mineral ores through Puerto Marán under preferential
    port rates
  - Paso Internacional Colinas Verdes and Paso de San Isidro
    are open for commercial traffic
  - The Paso de la Viuda remains a frozen conflict zone in
    symbolic terms; annual Zoopan commemorations at Monumento
    de la Viuda inflame Tucareño public opinion
  - Academic and cultural exchange programs exist; there is a
    Tucareño consulate in Cerro Verde and Zoopaná; Zoop has
    an embassy in Altavar
  - Lithium: Chinese investment in Zoopan lithium and rumors
    of Chinese-Tucareño talks on Altiplano lithium are a
    new source of economic tension as of the 2020s

Disputed territories (current status):
  1. Paso de la Viuda: formally in Zoop under the 1870 treaty;
     Tucaré disputes the exact ceasefire line from 1979;
     joint patrol continues.
  2. Salar de Tupanaku: fully within Zoop per all treaties;
     Tucaré does not formally claim it but Tucareño political
     parties occasionally raise "historic rights" to the basin
     as a domestic political maneuver.
  3. Río Pequeé navigation rights: Tucaré's commercial vessels
     technically retain Marán River transit rights under the
     1870 treaty; rarely exercised; periodically invoked in
     trade negotiations.
```

**Why "Tucaré"?**
The name is Zopikí-adjacent in sound — it echoes the Andean naming
conventions of the region without mapping to any real country name.
It is short (3 syllables), easy to use as an adjective (Tucareño),
and distinctive enough not to be confused with Zoop, Argentina,
Bolivia, or any other real South American nation. The word has no
meaning in Zopikí (it is a Tucareño toponym from a different
indigenous tradition on the western slope).

## SECTION 6 — Atlantic Coastline

```
Total coastline: approximately 940 km (measured at mean high-water mark,
                 including the major indentations of the bays but
                 excluding the Río Marán estuary inland extent)

Major capes:
  1. Cabo de San Roque
     Location: Northernmost point of Zoop's coastline;
               Provincia de Candelaria; lighthouse established

               marker for ships rounding into the Zoopan coast
               from the north.

  2. Cabo Marán
     Location: The southern headland of the Bahía de Maraná,
               near Puerto Marán; site of the Port Authority
               lighthouse (1883); the most photographed coastal
               feature in the country.

  3. Cabo de las Tormentas
     Location: Southern coast, Provincia de Quilmes del Sur;
               the southernmost navigable cape; named for the
               violent winter storms that funnel up from the
               subantarctic; avoided by coastal shipping
               October–June.

Major bays and harbors:
  1. Bahía de Maraná
     Associated city: Puerto Marán
     Character: Broad, deep estuary formed by the Río Marán
                mouth; naturally sheltered; the basis of Puerto
                Marán's status as the premier Atlantic port;
                annual dredging required due to river sediment.
     Width at mouth: approximately 18 km.

  2. Bahía Larga
     Associated city: Villa Inés
     Character: Long, shallow bay with extensive lagoon systems
                behind barrier beaches; unsuitable for large
                commercial vessels but excellent for small craft
                and the fishing industry; RAMSAR wetland
                designation (see Bahía Larga province).

  3. Ensenada de Santa Lucía
     Associated city: Santa Lucía
     Character: Smaller, curved bay; adequate depth for naval
                vessels and medium cargo ships; site of the
                Zoopan Navy's southern Atlantic base; pleasant
                beach resort in summer.

  4. Puerto Canela Roads
     Associated city: Puerto Canela (Candelaria)
     Character: Open anchorage rather than enclosed bay;
                protected by offshore reefs; used by fishing
                fleets and small coastal traders; no major port
                infrastructure.

Main fishing grounds:
  - The Banco Marán: a continental shelf fishing bank centered
    ~80 km east of Puerto Marán; primary species: merluza
    (hake), corvina, calamar (squid). This is the most
    productive zone in Zoopan waters; foreign fishing fleets
    (Chinese, Spanish, South Korean) are a recurring point
    of conflict.
  - Northern inshore grounds (Candelaria coast): yellowfin
    tuna, dorado, artisanal fishing; warmer subtropical waters.
  - Southern inshore grounds (Quilmes coast): Patagonian toothfish
    (occasional), king crab, merluza; colder waters; requires
    licensed operations.

Coastal climate character:
  The coast is humid subtropical in the north (Candelaria), transitioning
  to warm temperate in the center (Maraná, Santa Lucía del Mar), and
  cool temperate in the south (Bahía Larga, Quilmes del Sur). Prevailing
  winds are from the southeast year-round; summer brings occasional
  tropical moisture incursions from the north; winter brings cold fronts
  from the south (the pampero wind). Sea fog is common along the central
  coast April–September.

### Zone 1 — Clima Norteño ("Northern Climate")
*Provincia de Candelaria; northern Provincia de Los Esteros*

Character: Subtropical; warm and humid year-round; the only zone in

### Zone 2 — Clima Costero ("Coastal Climate")
*Provincias de Maraná, Santa Lucía del Mar, central Bahía Larga;
Autonomous District of Zoopaná*

```
Character: Warm temperate oceanic; humid; the most economically
           important zone; where most of the population lives.
Temperature range: 8–30°C (annual mean ~17°C; summer 22–30°C,
                   winter 8–14°C)
Annual rainfall: 950–1,300 mm; fairly well distributed; slight
                 summer-autumn maximum; summer droughts uncommon
                 but occur
Winter (June–August): Mild; occasional frost in Zoopaná itself
                      (1–3 nights per year); pampero cold fronts
                      bring sharp temperature drops 24–48 hours

                dairy and cattle; the citrus belt is at the
                northern edge of this zone
Signature feature: Sea fog (the garúa) is a regular winter
                   feature along the coast; Zoopaná's winters
                   are famously grey and damp.
```

### Zone 3 — Clima Pampero ("Pampa Climate")
*Provincias de Aranduy, Llanuras del Centro, San Bartolomé, Solórzano,
Quilmes del Sur; interior of Bahía Larga*

```
Character: Temperate continental; four distinct seasons; hot summers,
           cold winters; highly productive for agriculture; subject
           to the pampero wind (cold, dry, southwesterly) in winter.
Temperature range: -2–34°C (annual mean ~15°C; summer 25–34°C,
                   winter -2 to 12°C; frost common November–August
                   in southern sub-zone)
Annual rainfall: 700–1,100 mm; concentrated spring-autumn;
                 summer droughts occur roughly 1 year in 4 in
                 the drier western sub-zone
Winter (June–August): Cold; ground frost common; occasional snow
                      in southern Quilmes del Sur and Solórzano;
                      the pampero brings dangerous wind-chill
Growing season: October–April (soy, wheat, corn, sunflower);
                cattle year-round with supplemental feed in winter
Signature feature: The pampero wind — a sudden cold front from
                   the southwest — can drop temperatures 15°C
                   in under an hour; it is culturally central to
                   pampa life and figures prominently in Zoopan
                   folklore and literature.
```

### Zone 4 — Clima Andino ("Andean Climate")
*Provincias de Pequeé Norte, Las Cumbres, Río Hondo; upper Valles
del Oeste*

```
Character: High-altitude; dramatic variation by elevation;
           semi-arid in the rain shadow of the Cordillera;
           cold and dry above 3,000 m; valley floors more
           temperate and suitable for agriculture.
Temperature range: By altitude —
  Valley floors (1,000–2,000 m): 4–25°C (annual mean ~13°C)
  Mid-altitude (2,000–3,500 m): -4–18°C (annual mean ~7°C;
                                  frost year-round possible)
  High altitude (3,500 m+): -15–10°C; permanent snow above
                              ~4,200 m; glaciers retreating
Annual rainfall: 300–600 mm in highland/rain-shadow zones;
                 600–1,000 mm on windward slopes; snow dominant
                 above 3,000 m in winter
Winter (June–August): Cold to severe; the high passes including
                      Paso de la Viuda are often snow-blocked
                      June–September; Cerro Verde (2,400 m) sees
                      regular snowfall
Growing season: Valley floors: October–March; limited by altitude
                and frost risk; high-altitude crops (quinoa,
                potato varieties, some barley) grow May–November
                in some micro-climates; the Valles del Oeste wine
                zone benefits from warm days and cool nights
                (diurnal range 15–20°C) for fruit development
Signature feature: The "viento blanco" (white wind) — sudden
                   blizzard conditions at high altitude with zero
                   visibility — is a serious hazard for mountain
                   crossings; it has closed Paso de la Viuda
                   without warning and contributed to military
                   casualties in both the War of the Pequeé and
                   the Cordillera War.
```
