## SECTION 1 — Historical Silver Mining

### Colonial Origins and Peak Production

Silver mining was the engine of Zoop's colonial economy in the Sierra del Oeste.
Spanish colonists, following indigenous knowledge of mineral-rich outcroppings in
the Cordillera Tupanaku foothills, established the first *reales de minas* (royal
mining districts) in the mid-16th century. By the 1580s, four primary silver towns
operated in what are now the provinces of Las Cumbres, Río Hondo, and Pequeé Norte.

**The four colonial silver towns (from Session 1-A — locked):**

1. **Real de Minas de San Plácido del Cerro** → modern **Río Blanco** (Las Cumbres)
   - Colonial church (1571) survives; peak production: 1580s–1650s
   - Principal mine: the **Mina San Plácido** — a deep-shaft silver-lead operation
     driven into the granite of the Cordillera Tupanaku's eastern face
   - Peak output: estimated 4,000–6,000 kg of refined silver per year at its apex
     (mid-17th century); output derived from amalgamation with mercury supplied
     through the Lima trade network
   - Decline trigger: a catastrophic shaft collapse in 1658 ended primary operations;
     the mine flooded and no subsequent operator succeeded in reopening it
   - Modern status: a small silver ore operation (Mina San Plácido Chico) operates
     in the adjacent hillside today; approximately 8 tonnes of silver equivalent
     per year; entirely artisanal and artisan-scale; employed 34 workers as of 2024

2. **Real de Minas de la Santísima Trinidad** → **Trinidad Viejo** (Río Hondo)
   - Ghost town (~300 residents); ruins are a protected archaeological site
   - Peak: 1620s–1680s; silver-gold polymetallic ore body
   - Worked by *mita* forced indigenous labor under the Tupanachi and Pequeéka
     communities; mortality records suggest 30–40% of drafted workers died within
     five years of assignment
   - Operations ceased by approximately 1715 as shallow deposits exhausted; deeper
     shafts required mercury supplies that became increasingly unreliable after
     the Huancavelica mines' decline in the 1700s

3. **San Ignacio de las Cumbres** — now provincial capital of Las Cumbres (pop. 167,000)
   - Largest colonial mining town; retains the **Iglesia de San Ignacio** (1612/rebuilt 1698)
     and the **Casa de la Amalgamación** (1637; now the Museo Minero Colonial)
   - Silver output across its full operating period (1580–1821): estimated 380–420
     tonnes cumulative refined silver (colonial records partial; Archivo de Indias)
   - Mines: the **Veta Madre** (Mother Lode) — a 2.2 km surface strike of silver-rich
     quartz vein worked from the surface down; the mines went below groundwater at
     approximately 180 m depth; pumping technology was inadequate; this limited
     the deepest workings by the 18th century
   - Post-colonial: small operations continued sporadically through the 1870s;
     fully commercial silver mining effectively ended at San Ignacio by 1880 when
     the copper deposits became more economically attractive
   - Heritage: The **Minas de San Ignacio Colonial District** is a protected heritage
     zone; the mineshafts are a tourist site with guided tours operating since 1978

4. **Real de Minas de Nuestra Señora del Amparo** (western Pequeé Norte)
   - Abandoned 1693; the most remote of the four colonial districts
   - Located near what are now the Litio del Sur S.A. operations at the Salar de
     Tupanaku; ruins are partially below the modern evaporation pond infrastructure
   - Significance: This mine's abandonment in 1693 is the historical precedent cited
     by indigenous Pequeéka communities in their argument that the colonial state
     already demonstrated it did not sustainably develop the region

### Decline and Legacy

**Primary causes of silver decline (1700–1850):**

1. **Ore depletion:** Surface and near-surface high-grade ores were exhausted;
   deeper extraction required technological investment the colonial and early
   republican systems could not sustain
2. **Mercury supply disruption:** The amalgamation process required mercury from
   Huancavelica (Peru); trade disruptions in the Viceroyalty of Río de la Plata
   era (post-1776) and the independence wars (1810–1822) periodically cut off supply
3. **Labor system collapse:** The *mita* system collapsed with independence; wage
   labor required higher operating costs that the exhausted shallow mines could not support
4. **Post-independence neglect:** Successive republican governments focused on
   livestock, coastal agriculture, and the Liberal Era wheat boom; the Sierra del
   Oeste was economically marginalized from roughly 1850 to the late 19th century

**Physical legacy in the Sierra del Oeste:**

The silver-mining era shaped the region permanently:
- The *cañadas* (drainage gullies) around San Ignacio de las Cumbres are still
  discolored with silver-lead tailings that contaminate local soil and water
- The urban morphology of Cerro Verde traces to the colonial administrative outpost
  established to supply the Amparo mine; the town grew from a *tambo* (way station)
  on the mule route between the Amparo mine and the coast
- German and Italian immigrants who arrived in the 1870s–1890s found the Sierra del
  Oeste already scarred by 300 years of extraction; Villa Hannover (from 1-E) was
  established partly on the ruins of colonial-era processing infrastructure

**Artisanal silver mining today:**

Small-scale artisanal silver mining (minería artesanal de la plata) continues in
Las Cumbres and Río Hondo provinces. Approximately **180 registered artisanal
miners** operate under licenses from the Secretaría de Minería. Output is modest —
estimated **25–35 tonnes per year** silver equivalent nationally — and serves
primarily domestic jewelry markets and a small export to Chilean silver processors.

## SECTION 2 — Copper Mining (Current)

### Overview

Copper is Zoop's dominant active metal mining sector, centered in Pequeé Norte
and Las Cumbres provinces. The industry operates under a mixed model: state
majority ownership through MINORSA, with private and foreign minority stakes.

**Annual copper production (2024):** approximately **185,000 tonnes** of refined
copper and copper concentrate

**Export value:** approximately **USD 1.3 billion** per year (2024); exported
primarily as copper cathode and concentrate through Puerto Santa Lucía and
Puerto Marán (the latter via the Ferrocarril Andino freight system)

**Primary markets:** China (54%), Germany (18%), South Korea (12%), Japan (9%),
other (7%)

### State Mining Company: Minerales del Norte S.A. (MINORSA)

- **Full name:** Minerales del Norte Sociedad Anónima
- **Founded:** 1962, under President Peñaloza era predecessor / the early democratic
  period; nationalization of the Pequeé Norte and Las Cumbres mineral concessions
  was completed between 1962 and 1968
- **Ownership:** 100% state-owned (Ministerio de Minería y Energía); managed by an
  appointed board; currently chaired by Ing. Rodrigo Palomino Saavedra (since 2023,
  appointed under Carballo government)
- **Province:** Headquartered in Cerro Verde, Pequeé Norte
- **Operations:** copper mining (primary), silver (minor), lithium (35% stake in
  Litio del Sur S.A., the lithium JV), exploration in Valles del Oeste for cobalt
- **Political significance:** MINORSA is a lightning rod in the lithium debate.
  The FPZ social-democratic wing and Unidad Nacional both argue for expanding
  MINORSA's lithium stake to a controlling majority; the PR argues MINORSA is
  inefficient and should be privatized or restructured; Verdes-Futuro argues
  MINORSA should be subject to the same environmental standards as private operators
- **Revenue (2023):** approximately ZPS 84 billion (≈ USD 1.1 billion at 2023
  exchange rates); transfers approximately 22% of revenue to the Provincia de
  Pequeé Norte under the 2015 mining royalty framework

### Major Copper Mines

**1. Mina Cóndor (Primary)**
- **Location:** Laguna Sal, Pequeé Norte (established in 3-B)
- **Opened:** 1964 (established in 3-B)
- **Ownership:** MINORSA 65%, Compañía Minera del Pequeé (CMP, est. 1878) 35%
- **Type:** Open-pit copper porphyry deposit; the main pit is approximately 2.4 km
  across and 480 m deep as of 2024; expansion is constrained by proximity to the
  Salar de Tupanaku basin
- **Production:** approximately 95,000 tonnes copper equivalent per year (2024)
- **Workforce:** approximately 6,200 direct employees; 9,500 contract workers
- **Environmental record:** Copper tailings from Mina Cóndor have contaminated
  the Lago Pequeé Alta reservoir sediments (per 3-C); a 2021 environmental audit
  found copper concentrations above WHO drinking-water thresholds. The mine received
  a formal SMARN (Secretaría de Medio Ambiente y Recursos Naturales) remediation
  order in 2022; compliance monitoring ongoing. A 2019 pipeline rupture released
  approximately 4,200 m³ of acidic tailings slurry into a tributary of the Río
  Pequeé; the incident resulted in a ZPS 340 million fine and a 30-day production
  halt — Zoop's largest documented mining environmental violation

**2. Mina Plata Norte (Copper/Silver — declining)**
- **Location:** Northern Cordillera Pequeé foothills, Pequeé Norte
- **Historical operation:** Silver, 1882–1953 (established in 3-C)
- **Current operation:** copper ore from secondary mineralization; operated since
  1967; increasingly uneconomical as the ore grade declines
- **Production:** approximately 38,000 tonnes copper equivalent per year (declining)
- **Ownership:** MINORSA 80%, Compañía Minera del Pequeé (CMP) 20%
- **Status:** Under discussion for care-and-maintenance status by 2030 as the
  ore body approaches exhaustion; MINORSA's 2024 strategic plan identifies Mina
  Plata Norte as a Phase-Out Priority

**3. Mina Las Cumbres (Copper — Las Cumbres province)**
- **Location:** Southern Cordillera Tupanaku foothills, Provincia de Las Cumbres
- **Opened:** 1988 by the Proceso-era government; construction coincided with the
  Represa Pequeé Alta construction (a noted historical connection)
- **Ownership:** MINORSA 55%, Xstrata Zoop S.A. (private multinational, Swiss-
  registered) 45%
- **Production:** approximately 52,000 tonnes copper equivalent per year (2024)
- **Power source:** Draws primarily from the Represa Pequeé Alta (290 MW; 3-C)
- **Environmental record:** Generally cleaner than Mina Cóndor; more modern
  tailings management; only one minor spill incident documented (2011)

### Export Infrastructure for Copper

Copper concentrate and cathode move by truck to the **Ferrocarril Andino** freight
connection at Cerro Verde, then to the port of **Puerto Santa Lucía** (primary for
copper; the port's mineral berths are operated under a joint Navy/APN arrangement
from 0-B). Secondary volumes go through **Puerto Marán**. The **Acuerdo de Libre
Tránsito Andino (1997)** with Tucaré nominally allows Zoopan copper concentrate
to transit Tucareño territory to Pacific ports, but practical use has been limited
by the strained bilateral relationship.

## SECTION 3 — Lithium: The Salar de Tupanaku

### The Deposits

**Salar de Tupanaku (primary deposit):**
- Area: approximately **2,100 km²** (the largest salar in Zoop; established 3-C)
- Elevation: **~3,650 m asl** (established 3-C)
- Location: Eastern rain shadow of the Cordillera Pequeé, Provincia de Pequeé Norte
- Brine concentration: Average **890 mg/L lithium** in extractable brine (high grade
  by South American standards; comparable to the upper tier of Atacama operations)
- **Lithium Carbonate Equivalent (LCE) reserve estimate:** 6.8 million tonnes
  (Comisión Geológica Nacional [CGN] assessment, 2021; peer-reviewed by USGS
  methodology); potential for 12–18 million tonnes with deeper drilling (CGN 2023
  exploratory assessment)
- **Global ranking:** Zoop's confirmed lithium reserves rank approximately **5th in
  South America** by LCE and approximately **8th globally** (2024 USGS world lithium
  assessment); well behind Bolivia, Argentina, and Chile but significant in the
  global supply chain context; potential reserves with deeper drilling could move
  Zoop to 3rd in South America by 2035
- Extraction method: Solar evaporation of pumped brine; evaporation pond
  infrastructure covers approximately **340 km²** as of 2024 (established 3-C)

**Adjacent salares — the "Tupanaku Lithium Triangle":**
- **Salar Pequeé Chico:** ~380 km²; partially explored; CGN 2023 assessment:
  approximately 1.4 million tonnes LCE; currently unlicensed (pending MGL framework
  decision by Carballo government)
- **Salar del Volcán:** ~140 km²; approximately 0.6 million tonnes LCE; named for
  the dormant volcanic vent (Volcán Tupanaku, ~3,920 m) at its northern edge;
  explored but not yet in production; indigenous objection to any concession

**Combined Tupanaku Triangle potential:** 8.8–20+ million tonnes LCE total

### Discovery and Early Development

**Pre-colonial knowledge:** The Pequeéka Zopikí community knew the Salar de Tupanaku
as *Tupanaku Kachi* — "the great salt summit." The salt flat was used for salt
extraction (for food preservation and llama husbandry) and was understood to be
spiritually significant as one of the three ancestral territories of the Pequeéka
community (alongside the Bañados del Pequeé wetlands and the Cerro del Pasto Verde
above Cerro Verde). Flamingo breeding at the salar's brine ponds was observed and
considered sacred; the flamingo (*flamenco zoopano*) is central to Pequeéka
oral tradition and ritual iconography.

**Colonial and early republican period:** The Salar de Tupanaku was known to Spanish
colonial authorities primarily as a salt source and was included in the territory
of the Real de Minas de Nuestra Señora del Amparo. After the mine's abandonment in
1693, the salar was left to indigenous management. The Treaty of Pequeé City (1870)
placed the salar firmly within Zoopan territory, but no resource extraction occurred.

**First geological surveys:** The **Comisión Geológica Nacional (CGN)** conducted
its first systematic survey of the Salar de Tupanaku in **1953**, identifying
elevated lithium concentrations in the brine. The survey report, classified at the
time, was declassified in 1987; it estimated a much smaller reserve than subsequent
surveys because deep drilling technology was not yet applied.

**Modern exploration begins:** International interest in the Salar de Tupanaku's
lithium potential grew significantly after the global lithium price surge of the
mid-2000s, driven by hybrid and electric vehicle battery demand. Between **2007
and 2012**, the CGN conducted four additional survey campaigns with modern drilling
and brine analysis technology. The 2012 CGN survey — which used the same
methodology as the Chilean CORFO assessments of the Atacama — produced the first
published reserve estimate of **4.1 million tonnes LCE**, triggering intense
political and commercial interest.

**Formation of Litio del Sur S.A.:**

The **Litio del Sur S.A.** joint venture was formed in **2013** under the Vidal
government's predecessor (the FPZ government under Peñaloza Villafuertes, #33,
in his second term). The concession negotiation ran from 2011 to 2013.

- **Ownership structure:**
  - MINORSA (state mining company): **35%**
  - **Yanhe Minerals Corporation** (Chinese state-linked corporation): **40%**
  - **Southern Lithium Pty Ltd** (Australian-listed but partially Chinese-funded): **15%**
  - Private Zoopan institutional investors (Fondo de Inversión Zoopano, FIZ): **10%**
- **Concession area:** 1,800 km² of the Salar de Tupanaku surface plus the brine
  aquifer rights below; the concession does not cover Salar Pequeé Chico or Salar
  del Volcán
- **Construction phase:** 2013–2017 (evaporation pond construction, processing
  plant at Laguna Sal, pipeline infrastructure)
- **First production:** **2015** (lithium carbonate at pilot scale; approximately
  8,000 tonnes LCE in the first year)
- **Full commercial production commenced:** 2018 (following completion of the main
  processing facility, the **Planta de Carbonato de Litio Tupanaku** [PCLT] in Laguna Sal)
- **Current production (2024):** approximately **62,000 tonnes LCE per year**
- **Production target:** 120,000 tonnes LCE per year by 2030 (per Litio del Sur S.A.
  operational plan; subject to MGL framework and water rights litigation outcomes)

**The processing chain:**
1. Brine is pumped from wells (currently 847 active pump sites) distributed across
   the salar surface and perimeter
2. Brine flows by gravity and pump to the evaporation pond system (~340 km² total)
3. Solar evaporation concentrates lithium from 890 mg/L to approximately 6,000 mg/L
   over 12–18 months in the pond cascade
4. Concentrated brine is piped to the PCLT in Laguna Sal for chemical processing
   into battery-grade **lithium carbonate** (Li₂CO₃, 99.5% purity) and
   **lithium hydroxide** (LiOH, 56.5% purity — the premium product for NMC batteries)
5. Product is trucked to Cerro Verde, then via the RN-7 highway and Ferrocarril
   Andino freight to Puerto Marán for export

**Export markets (2024):**
- China: 72% (primarily Yanhe Minerals Corporation's own refining and battery supply chain)
- South Korea: 14% (Samsung SDI, LG Energy Solution supply agreements)
- EU: 9% (BASF and Umicore processing contracts)
- Japan and other: 5%

**Lithium carbonate export price:** USD 14,500–22,000 per tonne (price is highly
volatile; peaked at USD 78,000/tonne in December 2022 during the EV battery boom;
settled at USD 14,500 as of mid-2024 following Chinese production surge; Litio
del Sur S.A. operates on hedged contracts at an average of USD 18,400/tonne for 2024)

**Lithium carbonate as % of total Zoopan exports (2024):** approximately **14%**
of total export value (~USD 1.7 billion); making lithium the third-largest export
category after soy (~USD 3.2B) and beef (~USD 2.1B)

**Lithium and copper combined mining exports as % of GDP:**
Mining sector (all minerals) contributes approximately **8.2% of GDP** directly
and an estimated additional **3.4%** through supply-chain and service industries.
Lithium alone, at 2024 prices, contributes approximately **2.9% of GDP**; at 2022
peak prices, lithium contributed approximately **6.1% of GDP** — which fundamentally
altered the political salience of the sector.

## SECTION 4 — The MGL Framework: Lithium Governance Debate

### Background

The political controversy around lithium governance has been the defining domestic
policy debate of the Carballo government (2023–present). Three overlapping disputes
drive the discussion:

1. **State ownership vs. private/foreign investment:** What share should the Zoopan
   state own in lithium extraction?
2. **Revenue distribution:** What share of lithium revenues should go to the national
   government, to Pequeé Norte province, and to indigenous communities?
3. **Environmental regulation:** What water extraction limits should apply, and
   who should enforce them?

### The MGL (Marco de Gobernanza del Litio)

**Full name:** Marco de Gobernanza del Litio — "Lithium Governance Framework"

President Carballo announced the MGL process in **August 2023**, immediately after
her inauguration. The framework is intended to create a new licensing regime for
future lithium concessions (including Salar Pequeé Chico and Salar del Volcán)
and to renegotiate the terms of the existing Litio del Sur S.A. concession.

**Key MGL proposals as of April 2026:**
- Raise MINORSA's mandatory minimum stake in any new lithium concession from 35%
  to **51%** (this would make Zoop the controlling partner in any new JV)
- Establish a **Fondo Nacional del Litio (FNL)** — a sovereign wealth fund receiving
  15% of lithium revenues, invested in renewable energy and education
- Require **Consulta Previa** (prior consultation) with the Consejo Comunitario
  Pequeéka before any new concession is granted; the consultation to follow ILO
  Convention 169 standards
- Create a **water budget** for the Tupanaku brine aquifer — a maximum extraction
  limit linked to measured aquifer recharge rates; extraction above the limit to
  trigger automatic suspension
- Establish a **Pequeéka Royalty** — 3% of gross lithium revenues paid directly to
  the Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka for community benefit projects

**Status as of April 2026:** The MGL framework is being negotiated in the Congreso
Nacional. The Alianza por la Renovación coalition (FPZ + MZ + Verdes-Futuro)
commands a majority in the Chamber of Deputies but not in the Senate; PR and UN
senators have used procedural mechanisms to delay the MGL vote. The framework
has passed the Chamber in a modified form (March 2026) and awaits Senate action.

### Party Positions on Lithium

**Partido Republicano (PR):**
"We do not oppose lithium development — we support it. But the MGL's 51% rule will
scare away the capital and technology we need. We propose keeping MINORSA at 35%
while improving transparency and environmental monitoring. Zoop should attract more
investment, not less. The water argument is a Trojan horse for anti-development
activism." — Senator Augusto Ferreyra Palomino (PR, San Bartolomé), March 2026

**Frente Popular Zoopano (FPZ):**
Split internally: the *ala productivista* (productive wing) agrees with MINORSA at
51% but opposes the water moratorium; the *ala ecologista* (ecological wing) aligns
with Verdes-Futuro on water. President Carballo's public position: 51% MINORSA stake
is non-negotiable, but the water budget will be based on science, not ideology.

**Movimiento Zopikí (MZ):**
Full endorsement of the Pequeéka Royalty and Consulta Previa requirements; MZ
deputies have threatened to withdraw from the coalition if the Consulta Previa
clause is weakened. VP Mirta Yahuar Huasi has personally lobbied Pequeéka community
leaders on the framework.

**Verdes-Futuro:**
Supports a complete moratorium on new lithium concessions until the water budget
system is operational and the first three years of aquifer monitoring data are
assessed. Accepts the existing Litio del Sur S.A. operation but opposes expansion.
Party president Lic. Rodrigo Celeste Ibáñez: "We cannot lose the flamingos, the
water, and the Pequeéka community's future in exchange for battery contracts with
China."

**Unidad Nacional (UN):**
Full nationalization — terminate the Litio del Sur S.A. concession, compensate
the foreign partners at book value (not market value), and establish a 100%
state-owned lithium company. UN leader Horacio Bengoechea Ríos: "Every peso that
goes to Yanhe Minerals in Beijing is a peso stolen from Zoopan workers."

**Carballo government's actual policy position:**
The Carballo government seeks a negotiated middle path: 51% MINORSA stake in new
concessions, renegotiation of Litio del Sur S.A. terms to increase MINORSA's share
from 35% to 48% (with Yanhe's share declining to 32%), a Pequeéka Royalty of 2.5%
(a compromise from the MZ's requested 3%), and a water budget framework with a
graduated enforcement mechanism rather than automatic suspension.

### International Context: Chile and Bolivia Comparison

**Chile's model:** The Chilean government nationalized copper under Allende (1973);
lithium has a more complex history. CODELCO (100% state-owned) does not mine lithium;
instead, Chile licensed SQM and Albemarle under long-term concessions, later
renegotiating for higher royalties. The Boric government (2022–present) announced
a new "National Lithium Strategy" in April 2023, calling for state participation
in all new lithium projects. Zoop's MGL debate explicitly references this model.

**Bolivia's model:** YPFB's lithium subsidiary, YLB, attempted 100% state control
of the Salar de Uyuni — the world's largest lithium deposit. After years of
delays, Bolivia signed agreements with Chinese and German partners in 2023 for
partial development. The Bolivian case is cited both by UN (as proof that
nationalization is achievable) and by PR (as proof that full nationalization causes
investment delays of 15+ years).

**The sovereignty argument:**
MZ Deputy Diputada Rosario Cóndor Mamani (Pequeé Norte) gives the canonical MZ
position: "Our people lived on the Salar de Tupanaku for 3,000 years before anyone
knew what lithium was. Now the world wants what is under our sacred land. We are
not against Zoop developing this resource. We are against Zoop developing it while
treating our communities as if we are not here."

### Who Benefits? Revenue Distribution

**Under current arrangements (2024):**
- National government: receives royalties of 12% of gross lithium revenue (under
  the 2013 Litio del Sur S.A. concession terms) plus corporate income tax (25%)
  on MINORSA's and domestic investors' shares
- Provincia de Pequeé Norte: receives 8% of gross revenues under the **Regalía
  Provincial** framework established in 2015
- Indigenous communities: receive **no direct payment** under the current concession;
  the Fondo de Desarrollo Comunitario (FDC; established by Carballo 2023) provides
  some funding but is not a percentage of lithium revenues
- Yanhe Minerals Corporation and Southern Lithium Pty Ltd (combined 55% private share):
  retain their proportional share of profits after royalties and taxes

**Projected under MGL (if passed as currently drafted):**
- National government: royalties rise to 18% + FNL 15% of revenues
- Provincia de Pequeé Norte: retains 8%
- Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka: receives 2.5% direct Pequeéka Royalty (approximately
  USD 42 million/year at current production levels)
- Foreign partners: reduced to 32% combined stake, correspondingly reduced profits

## SECTION 5 — Indigenous Rights and the Water Injunction

### The Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka

**Established:** The Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka is the formal governing body of
the 14 Pequeéka communities in the Salar de Tupanaku zone (established in 3-B).

**President:** Amaru Quispe Cari (established in 3-B) — indigenous rights advocate;
former schoolteacher; leading public voice of the anti-extraction movement.

**Communities represented:** 14 named communities; the largest are:
- **Comunidad Tupanaku** (centered 8 km north of the evaporation ponds; approximately
  1,200 people; primary herders and salt traders)
- **Comunidad Volcán** (near Salar del Volcán; approximately 840 people; the most
  isolated community; primary traditional llama herders)
- **Comunidad Ojo de Agua** (named for the *ojo de agua* springs that have dried
  since 2019; approximately 620 people; most directly affected by brine pumping)
- **Comunidad Cerro Pasto** (nearest to Cerro Verde city; most urbanized; approximately
  3,400 people; diverse livelihoods including mining employment)

**Cultural significance of the salar:**
The Salar de Tupanaku is sacred space in Pequeéka cosmology:
- The flamingo (*flamenco zoopano*) breeding colonies on the salar's brine lagoons
  are understood as *animales sagrados* (sacred animals); their decline is interpreted
  as spiritual warning
- The *ojos de agua* (fresh water springs at the salar edge) are sites of ritual
  offerings; three have gone dry since 2019
- The Pequeéka harvest festival (*Fiesta de la Pequeéka*, March; established 3-B)
  traditionally begins with a ceremonial walk to the salar's edge

### The 2025 Water Rights Injunction

**Filing:** In **March 2025**, the Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka filed a formal
**water rights injunction** (*amparo hídrico*) before the **Tribunal Provincial de
Pequeé Norte**, seeking:
1. An immediate suspension of brine pumping from the Salar de Tupanaku pending a
   court-ordered independent hydrogeological assessment
2. Recognition of the Pequeéka communities' prior water rights under the 1994
   Constitution's indigenous rights provisions
3. Compensation for damages to the three dried *ojos de agua* and associated
   community livelihood losses
4. A permanent 40% reduction in brine extraction volume at Litio del Sur S.A.

**Legal basis:**
- Article 67 of the 1994 Constitution: "Indigenous communities have the right to
  the preservation and sustainable use of the natural resources in their territories,
  including water"
- ILO Convention 169 (ratified by Zoop in 1997): requires prior consultation and
  consent for activities affecting indigenous peoples' lands and resources
- The CGN's own 2023 report acknowledging aquifer drawdown of at least 2.8 meters
  since 2019 (a figure Litio del Sur S.A. disputes, citing their own monitoring
  showing 1.2 m drawdown)

**Status as of April 2026:**
The Tribunal Provincial de Pequeé Norte issued a **partial precautionary measure**
in September 2025: requiring Litio del Sur S.A. to reduce brine pumping by 15%
during the case proceedings. The company and MINORSA challenged the measure in the
Cámara Federal; the Cámara upheld the precautionary measure in February 2026.
The full injunction hearing is scheduled for August 2026.

**Political response:**
- Carballo government: stated it "respects the judicial process" while continuing
  to pursue the MGL framework; critics note the government has not voluntarily
  suspended operations pending the outcome
- MINORSA: appealing the precautionary measure while publicly supporting the
  communities' "right to participate in decisions about their territory"
- MZ: Diputada Rosario Cóndor Mamani held a press conference with Amaru Quispe
  Cari; called the precautionary measure "a first step toward justice"
- Verdes-Futuro: demanded the government impose a unilateral production moratorium
  without waiting for the court ruling
- UN: used the injunction in Senate speeches to argue for full nationalization
  ("at least then the state would be accountable for the damage")

**Key figure:** Dr. Patricia Mamani Cóndor (established 3-B) — hydrologist at
the Universidad de la Sierra del Oeste (USO), Cerro Verde — leads the independent
aquifer research team whose data supports the community's legal arguments. Her
2024 report in the *Revista de Hidrología Andina* is the peer-reviewed foundation
of the injunction's scientific case.

### Flamingo Breeding Lagoons at Risk

The **Salar de Tupanaku flamingo breeding colony** is the largest in Zoop and one
of the most significant in the southern Andes. Key facts:

- **Species:** The flamingo at Tupanaku is classified as the *flamenco zoopano*
  (*Phoenicopterus chilensis zoopanus*) — the Zoopan near-endemic subspecies
  (established in 3-B/3-E)
- **Breeding population:** approximately **3,800 breeding pairs** at the Salar de
  Tupanaku; an additional ~1,200 pairs at Salar Pequeé Chico; and the primary
  wintering population at Bahía Larga (RAMSAR site, from 3-C)
- **Breeding mechanism:** Flamingos build mud-and-salt nests on islands in the
  brine lagoons where water is 5–12 cm deep; if brine level drops, the islands
  become connected to the salar shore and nests become accessible to predators
  (Andean foxes, condors opportunistically); if brine level rises above ~15 cm,
  nests flood and eggs fail
- **Documented impact:** The Litio del Sur S.A. evaporation ponds have altered
  the hydrology of three of the eight primary flamingo breeding lagoons on the
  salar. A 2024 survey by Dr. Luis Horacio Pedraza Montes (established 3-E;
  Zoop's leading ornithologist) found that breeding success in the three affected
  lagoons dropped from 74% egg-to-chick survival (2014 baseline) to 31% in 2023
- **SMARN response:** The Secretaría de Medio Ambiente y Recursos Naturales issued
  a formal **Alerta Ambiental Tupanaku** in January 2025, classifying the flamingo
  breeding colony as critically threatened by brine extraction activities. The alert
  triggered the requirement for Litio del Sur S.A. to prepare a **Plan de Mitigación
  Flamingo** (PMF); the plan was submitted but criticized by environmental NGOs as
  inadequate
- **Political resonance:** The flamingo is deeply embedded in Zoopan popular culture
  (appearing on the old 20-peso note and associated with the Fiesta de la Pequeéka);
  photographs of flamingo nests surrounded by evaporation pond infrastructure
  became the iconic image of the lithium debate, widely shared on Zoopan social media

## SECTION 6 — Energy: ENEZ and the Four Dams

### Empresa Nacional de Energía Zoopana (ENEZ)

- **Full name:** Empresa Nacional de Energía Zoopana S.A.
- **Founded:** 1958, under President Fuentes Castañón (#22), as part of the
  post-Revolución Liberadora modernization program
- **Ownership:** 100% state-owned; Ministerio de Minería y Energía; managed by a
  board chaired by Ing. Luisa Beltrán Oquendo (appointed 2023 under Carballo)
- **Operations:** Owns and operates all four major hydroelectric dams on the
  Río Marán system; operates the national high-voltage transmission grid (Red
  de Transmisión Nacional, RTN); participates in renewable energy development;
  maintains the national electricity dispatch center (Centro de Despacho Eléctrico
  Nacional, CDEN) in Zoopaná

## SECTION 7 — Oil and Gas

### Overview

Zoop is not an oil state, but it has commercially significant hydrocarbon reserves
that provide partial domestic energy self-sufficiency.

**Total oil and gas as % of national energy supply:** approximately 34% of primary
energy consumption (electricity from hydro + thermal; industrial heat; transportation)

**State oil company:** The **Empresa Petrolera Nacional Zoopana (EPNZ)** — founded
1961; manages exploration and production licenses; does not own all fields (operates
some jointly with private companies). Headquarters: Corrientes del Marán.

### The Campos del Norte Formation

**Location:** The primary oil and gas-producing zone lies under the Pampa Central,
primarily in the provinces of **Los Esteros** and northern **Llanuras del Centro**.
The formation is named the **Campos del Norte Formation** (Formación Campos del
Norte) — a Cretaceous continental sedimentary formation at depths of 1,800–3,400 m.

**Discovery:** First commercial oil discovery: **1934**, near Esteros del Norte
(Los Esteros province), during exploratory drilling authorized by the Faría
government; the military government recognized the strategic value immediately.

**Production figures (2024):**
- Oil: approximately **12,400 barrels per day** — modest by South American standards;
  sufficient to supply approximately 60% of domestic refined petroleum needs (the
  balance imported from Argentina-equivalent neighbor and Venezuela)
- Natural gas: approximately **8.2 million m³ per day**; provides 28% of electricity
  generation (thermoelectric plants in Puerto Marán, Corrientes del Marán, and
  Zoopaná) and significant industrial heating
- The **Refinería del Sur** (petroleum refinery; Maraná province; est. 1962; from 3-B)
  processes the majority of domestic crude; capacity 28,000 barrels/day

**Named fields:**
- **Campo Esteros Norte** — largest producing field; Los Esteros province; 8,200 bopd;
  operated by EPNZ (70%) and Repsol Zoop S.A. (30%) under a 2009 production-sharing
  agreement
- **Campo Llanuras Central** — second field; Llanuras del Centro; 4,200 bopd; operated
  by EPNZ (51%) and YPF Zoop (49%; a subsidiary of the Argentine state oil company
  under a cross-border joint venture)

**Strategic significance:** Natural gas from Los Esteros keeps thermoelectric plants
running during drought years when hydropower drops; it is the key backup for grid
stability. Interruptions to gas supply (as occurred in the 2008 winter due to field
maintenance issues) triggered rolling blackouts in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán.

## SECTION 8 — Renewable Energy

### Wind Power

**First commercial wind farm:** **Parque Eólico Pampa Sur** — inaugurated **2009**;
located in Quilmes del Sur province (southern pampa; consistent with 3-C's reference
to the pampero wind); 48 turbines; **120 MW** installed capacity; operated by ENEZ
(60%) and a Spanish renewable energy company, Iberdrola Zoop S.A. (40%)

**Current wind capacity (2024):** approximately **950 MW** installed nationally,
across 9 wind farms; all in the southern and central Pampa Central provinces
(Quilmes del Sur, Solórzano, Llanuras del Centro); the pampero wind regime provides
excellent capacity factors (35–42%)

**Major wind farms:**
- Parque Eólico Pampa Sur (120 MW; Quilmes del Sur; 2009)
- Parque Eólico Viento del Centro (180 MW; Llanuras del Centro; 2014)
- Parque Eólico Aranduy (220 MW; Aranduy; 2018)
- Parque Eólico Solórzano Norte (240 MW; Solórzano; 2020)
- Parque Eólico Llanuras II (190 MW; Llanuras del Centro; 2023)

**Projected wind capacity by 2030:** 2,400 MW under the national renewable energy
plan (**Plan de Energías Renovables 2030**, PER-2030; enacted 2021 under Vidal
government; continued and expanded by Carballo)

### Solar Power

**Parque Solar Tupanaku:**
The largest solar project in Zoop, and the most politically contentious renewable
development. The Parque Solar Tupanaku is a proposed **400 MW** solar photovoltaic
installation on the southern edge of the Salar de Tupanaku — on land within the
ancestral territory of the Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka.

- **Status:** Approved by ENEZ engineering assessment (2022); the **Pequeéka water
  rights injunction (2025)** has been interpreted by the Carballo government as
  also requiring Consulta Previa before any new infrastructure (including solar)
  is built on the salar boundary; the solar park's construction is currently on hold
- **The dispute:** ENEZ argues that solar panels on the salar fringe do not affect
  the brine aquifer and should be separated from the water rights case; Consejo
  Comunitario Pequeéka argues that any industrial installation on the salar
  territory requires prior consultation and consent under the 1994 Constitution
- **Irony noted by commentators:** A renewable energy project that could reduce
  Zoop's carbon footprint is caught in the same indigenous rights dispute as the
  lithium operation it was designed to help power
- **SMARN position:** The Secretaría de Medio Ambiente has asked ENEZ to conduct
  a full **Evaluación de Impacto Ambiental Reforzado (EIAR)** (from the Carballo
  government's DEIAR decree of June 2023) before proceeding

**Other solar installations:**
- Cerro Verde city has 45 MW of rooftop and small-utility solar on public buildings
  (2024); the **Barrio Tupanaku** (from 3-B) was designed with solar installations
  on all residential rooftops
- A **22 MW** agrivoltaic pilot project in Candelaria province (combining solar
  panels with citrus grove irrigation) — commissioned 2022; operated by a private
  cooperative under ENEZ grid purchase agreement

**National renewable energy target:** Under the **Plan de Energías Renovables 2030
(PER-2030)**, Zoop targets **35% of electricity from non-hydro renewables by 2030**
(wind, solar, biomass). Current non-hydro renewable share: approximately 16% (2024).
The Carballo government has proposed an accelerated target of **30% by 2028**,
which requires approximately 3,500 MW of additional wind and solar capacity.

## SECTION 9 — Political Economy of Lithium

### The Sovereignty Debate

The lithium debate in Zoop is not purely economic — it is bound up with questions
of national identity, postcolonial resource ownership, and indigenous rights that
have no simple resolution. Key formulations in the public debate:

**"Lithium is the new silver"** — a phrase widely used by indigenous rights advocates
and environmental groups; it invokes the colonial silver mining that enriched Spain
while devastating Zopikí communities and exhausting the Sierra del Oeste's
most productive mines; the implication is that foreign-controlled lithium extraction
will repeat this pattern

**"Lithium is our future, not our past"** — the Carballo government's counter-framing;
emphasizes lithium revenues funding the energy transition, education, and health;
the FNL (sovereign wealth fund) is the institutional expression of this vision

**"Without water, no lithium"** — the Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka's slogan,
which has become the environmental movement's rallying cry; it condenses the
aquifer and flamingo issues into a simple message that resonates beyond Pequeé Norte

### Who Is Invested, Who Benefits, Who Loses

**Winners under current arrangements:**
- Yanhe Minerals Corporation (China): secures a long-term battery-supply-chain
  asset at a time when lithium supply is strategically critical; the Chinese company
  has re-sold contracted lithium to CATL and other battery makers at significant margin
- MINORSA (and by extension, the national government): receives royalties and the
  state's 35% profit share; lithium revenues have directly funded the Carballo
  government's Fondo de Desarrollo Comunitario (FDC; established in 2-C)
- Cerro Verde (Pequeé Norte province): the city has transformed since 2015 (from
  a 440,000-population mining city to one with new infrastructure, the Parque
  Empresarial Cerro Verde, and the Barrio Tupanaku); provincial revenues from
  the Regalía Provincial fund have built schools and clinics

**Losers under current arrangements:**
- Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka communities: water stress, flamingo decline,
  cultural disruption; no direct revenue; employment in the mine mostly skilled
  positions filled by migrant workers from elsewhere in Zoop and Latin America
- The flamingo breeding colony: documented 58% decline in breeding success
  in affected lagoons
- Migrant worker communities: high-stress living conditions in Laguna Sal; housing
  shortage; social tension with established indigenous communities

### Cerro Verde's Transformation Since 2015

Cerro Verde (pop. 440,000; from seed doc) has been dramatically affected by the
lithium boom. Key changes since 2015:

- **Population growth:** the city has grown approximately **22%** since 2015 (from
  ~360,000 to 440,000); most growth in Barrio Tupanaku (established 2018; from 3-B)
  and the new Parque Empresarial Cerro Verde (est. 2016; from 3-B)
- **Infrastructure:** the Aeropuerto Regional Internacional Cerro Verde has seen
  passenger volumes grow from ~380,000/year (2014) to ~800,000/year (2024); the
  RN-7 highway has been expanded and partially tolled for mining truck traffic
- **Migrant workers:** approximately 18,000 skilled workers have relocated to Cerro
  Verde from other Zoopan provinces and from Chile, Bolivia, and Peru; the cultural
  friction between these workers and the established Pequeéka community is a
  recurring social issue; the Barrio Tupanaku was built partly to house migrants
  and partly to prevent them from settling in the indigenous Barrio Pequeéka
- **Environmental health:** respiratory conditions associated with dust from the
  evaporation ponds (salt and lithium compound particles) have been documented
  in Cerro Verde's urban population; a 2023 SMARN study found elevated boron
  concentrations in the municipal water supply, a known lithium-processing byproduct;
  SMARN issued an advisory but not a formal health order
- **Mayor Miriam Quispe Cóndor** (MZ/FPZ coalition; Pequeéka Zopikí; mining
  engineer; first indigenous mayor of Cerro Verde; established in 3-B): she has
  publicly stated that lithium development must "pay its full cost to this city" —
  including healthcare, environmental remediation, and cultural preservation funding
