## SECTION 1 — CATTLE INDUSTRY

### Overview

Cattle ranching is the oldest industry in the Pampa Central and remains central to Zoopan national identity. The country maintains approximately **12.4 million head of cattle** (2023 census), on a national territory of ~189,000 km²—a density among the highest in South America relative to land area. Zoop exports approximately **420,000 tonnes of beef** (carcass-weight equivalent) annually, making it the country's largest agricultural export by value at roughly **USD 2.1 billion** per year (2023).

### Cattle Breeds

Three primary breeds dominate Zoopan ranching:

1. **Hereford** — introduced in the 1880s by the Varela-Montejo family (Session 1-E established that Don Marcos Varela Montejo brought British Hereford and Angus breeding stock in 1891); remains the dominant breed in the Pampa Central's more arid interior, especially Solórzano and Quilmes del Sur. Valued for hardiness and grass-to-weight conversion. Approximately **48% of national herd.**

2. **Aberdeen Angus** — arrived alongside Hereford in the 1890s; preferred in the more humid coastal belt (Maraná, Bahía Larga). Produces the premium cuts favored by EU and Chinese high-end buyers. Approximately **31% of national herd.**

3. **Criollo Zoopano** — the native Zoopan breed, descended from original Spanish colonial cattle brought in the 16th–17th centuries. Smaller than British breeds, with a distinctive reddish-brown hide with white facial markings. Hardier in extreme conditions (drought, parasite resistance). The **Instituto Nacional de Investigación Agropecuaria (INIA-Zoop)** runs a **Programa de Conservación del Criollo Zoopano** to maintain pure-bred lines; approximately **18,000 registered pure-bred Criollos** in the national herd. The remaining ~21% of national herd are crossbreeds or minor commercial breeds.

### Major Ranching Provinces

The densest cattle zones by province (from 0-B):

| Province | Region | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Aranduy** | Pampa Central | Prime beef country; alluvial plains along the Río Marán corridor; high-quality pasture; family of President Barros Uribe had historic holdings here |
| **Llanuras del Centro** | Pampa Central | The Cruzat-Salinas family's core territory (established 1-E); dense mixed cattle-wheat operations; Corrientes del Marán is the "beef capital" of the interior |
| **Solórzano** | Pampa Central | Varela-Montejo family territory (1-E); slightly drier climate; specialized in Hereford; largest single estancias in the country |
| **San Bartolomé** | Pampa Central | Mixed cattle and grain; Barrientos-Fuentes territory; Bolsa de Cereales de San Bartolomé (from 3-B) serves as the regional trading hub |
| **Maraná** | Costa Atlántica | Coastal dairy-beef mix; Etchegaray-Ríos territory; heavier rainfall supports more intensive pasture |
| **Bahía Larga** | Costa Atlántica | Dairy cattle dominant (see Section 4) but significant beef operations in the northern interior |

**Average estancia size** in the Pampa Central: approximately **4,200 hectares** (median), though the distribution is extremely skewed — 3% of ranches control 41% of cattle land.

### Meatpacking Plants (Frigoríficos)

Four major frigoríficos handle the bulk of Zoopan beef processing:

---

**1. Frigorífico Nacional del Marán (FNM)**
- **Location:** Zona Industrial Portuaria, Puerto Marán (from 3-B)
- **Founded:** 1902, with British capital (Armour & Co. subsidiary); nationalized 1947 under President Durán Balaguer; privatized 1993 as a worker-owned cooperative under the Cossio Peralta stabilization program
- **Ownership (current):** Mixed — 52% cooperative (workers' shares), 38% Grupo Riccardi S.A. (the food conglomerate from 1-E), 10% state retained
- **Capacity:** 3,200 head/day; employs approximately 2,800 workers
- **Products:** Full range of beef cuts for domestic and export; also processes hides, offal, and renders tallow for industrial use
- **Export certification:** holds EU food safety certification (since 1998) and Chinese GACC registration (since 2008)
- **Historical note:** The STF (Sindicato de Trabajadores del Frigorífico, est. 1897 in 1-E) was founded in response to labor conditions at this plant's predecessor facility

---

**2. Frigorífico del Sur S.A. (FRISA)**
- **Location:** Villa Solórzano, Provincia de Solórzano
- **Founded:** 1918; originally the Varela-Montejo family's private processing operation; incorporated as a shareholder company 1964 when the family sold a 40% stake to a U.S. meatpacker (Swift International)
- **Ownership (current):** Varela-Montejo S.A. 35%, Swift-JBS International 45%, Zoopan institutional investors 20%
- **Capacity:** 2,400 head/day; employs approximately 2,100 workers
- **Products:** Premium Angus and Hereford cuts for export; high-end chilled beef to China and the EU; the **"Pampas Select"** brand is owned by FRISA
- **Note:** FRISA's Villa Solórzano plant is the country's second-largest employer in the interior pampa, and a major political force in provincial politics; the union local (UOTP affiliate) is one of the most powerful labor organizations in the Pampa Central

---

**3. Frigoríficos del Pequeé (FDP)**
- **Location:** San Bartolomé del Pequeé (established in Session 3-B as an existing entity)
- **Founded:** 1910; British capital; nationalized 1947; re-privatized 1994 under a Barrientos-Fuentes family consortium
- **Ownership (current):** Agrobarrientos S.R.L. 60%, Bolsa de Cereales de San Bartolomé cooperative investors 40%
- **Capacity:** 1,800 head/day; employs approximately 1,600 workers
- **Products:** Standard cuts plus specialty offal processing; major hides exporter; key halal certifier for Middle Eastern markets (halal-certified since 2004)
- **Rail link:** Has a dedicated freight siding connection to the **Ramal San Bartolomé** line (from 1-E), enabling direct refrigerated rail shipment to Puerto Marán

---

**4. Frigorífico Lombardi S.A. (FLO)**
- **Location:** Puerto Viejo, Maraná province (coastal, 87,000 pop., established in 0-B)
- **Founded:** 1904 by Don Carlo Lombardi (from 1-E: Frigorífico Lombardi established 1904)
- **Ownership (current):** Grupo Lombardi 55%, Frigorífico Nacional del Marán cooperative 25%, private investors 20%
- **Capacity:** 900 head/day; employs approximately 720 workers
- **Products:** Specialty coastal production — grass-fed coastal beef premium line, significant dairy beef processing (retired dairy cows); notable for pioneering **hormone-free beef certification** for the EU market (1996)
- **Note:** The smallest of the four major frigoríficos; competes on premium positioning rather than volume

---

### Export Chain and Markets

**Processing chain:** Estancia → local saladero/intermediary agent (*acopiador*) → frigorífico → Puerto Marán (primary) or Puerto Santa Lucía (secondary, smaller volumes) → international markets

**Key export markets (by volume, 2023):**
1. **China** — 41% of beef export volume; primarily frozen cuts; the Chinese market has been dominant since 2012; priced on futures contracts at the **Bolsa de Cereales de San Bartolomé** and the Chicago Mercantile Exchange
2. **European Union** — 22% of volume; primarily chilled cuts under strict hormone-free and sustainability certification; the EU's "Zoop Quota" under the Mercosur-EU trade framework (associate membership) caps exports; lobbying to expand the quota is a permanent fixture of Zoopan trade diplomacy
3. **Brazil** — 14% of volume; processed beef and lower-value cuts
4. **United States** — 9% of volume; primarily specialty grass-fed lines
5. **Middle East (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Jordan)** — 8% combined; halal-certified; growing market
6. **Other** — 6%

**First refrigerated export:** As established in Session 1-E, the first refrigerated beef shipment from Puerto Marán to London departed **12 March 1882** aboard the British vessel *SS Zoopaná Star*. This date is commemorated annually at the **Día de la Exportación Frigorífica**, a minor trade holiday marked by the Ministerio de Agricultura and the frigoríficos' industry association.

### Annual Slaughter Statistics (2023)

- Total cattle slaughtered: approximately **3.8 million head**
- Domestic consumption: approximately **1.9 million head equivalent**
- Export: approximately **1.9 million head equivalent**
- Average Zoopan beef consumption per capita: **48 kg/year** (among the highest in the world; consistent with neighboring Argentina at ~50 kg; reflects national asado culture)

## SECTION 2 — SOY AND WHEAT ECONOMY

### The Pampa Central as Breadbasket

The Pampa Central — comprising the provinces of Aranduy, Llanuras del Centro, San Bartolomé, Quilmes del Sur, Los Esteros, and Solórzano — is Zoop's agricultural heartland. It covers approximately **94,000 km²** of the national territory and produces virtually all of Zoop's grain output.

**Total cultivated area (2023):**
- Soy: **6.2 million hectares** (first crop)
- Wheat: **2.1 million hectares**
- Maize: **1.4 million hectares**
- Sunflower: **680,000 hectares**
- Other crops: **320,000 hectares**

### Soy Economy

**Arrival of soy:** Commercial soy cultivation arrived in Zoop in the late 1980s but expanded dramatically in **1996–2003** under President Briceño Lemus (#28) and during the La Crisis period. The key catalyst was the introduction of **Roundup Ready (RR) genetically modified soybeans** (licensed from Monsanto/Bayer) in **1998**, which dramatically reduced production costs by enabling broad-spectrum herbicide use. By 2005, soy had overtaken wheat as Zoop's largest crop by area.

**Main soy-producing provinces:**
1. **Llanuras del Centro** — the most productive zone; flat terrain, rich alluvial soils; annual production ~1.8 million tonnes
2. **San Bartolomé** — second largest; historically cattle country transitioning partially to soy; annual production ~1.5 million tonnes
3. **Aranduy** — significant soy expansion in the 2000s on former wheat land; annual production ~1.2 million tonnes
4. **Los Esteros** — northern zone; subtropical conditions allow two crops per year (soy + winter wheat rotation); annual production ~900,000 tonnes

**Total soy production:** approximately **6.4 million tonnes** per year (2023 average)
**Soy export value:** approximately **USD 3.2 billion** per year (making it Zoop's single largest export earner in value terms, slightly ahead of beef)

**Primary soy export destination:** China (68% of soy exports, primarily as soymeal and crude soy oil after processing; whole beans are also exported in significant quantities)

**Soy processing infrastructure:**
- **Terminal Oleaginosa del Marán (TODEM)** — the largest crushing facility; located in the Zona Industrial Portuaria, Puerto Marán; founded 1987; owned by a joint venture of Bunge Zoop S.A. and Grupo Cruzat (the Cruzat-Salinas family's agribusiness arm); capacity: 18,000 tonnes/day crushing; exports soymeal via dedicated grain berths at Puerto de Maraná
- **Molinos del Río S.A.** — second major processor; Arroyo Grande (San Bartolomé); crushing capacity 9,000 tonnes/day; owned by an Argentine-Zoopan joint venture
- **Molinera Pampera Cooperativa (MPC)** — cooperative processor; Corrientes del Marán; 5,000 tonnes/day capacity; owned by the **Cooperativa Agropecuaria Central Zoopana (CACZ)** (see below)

**Major agricultural cooperatives:**
1. **Cooperativa Agropecuaria Central Zoopana (CACZ)** — the largest agricultural cooperative; headquartered in Corrientes del Marán; founded 1934 by wheat farmers as the **Cooperativa Triguera del Centro**; renamed 1997 when soy became dominant; approximately **14,400 member farms** across Llanuras del Centro, Aranduy, and Los Esteros; annual turnover ~USD 890 million; operates grain silos, elevators, input supply stores, and crop insurance programs; politically affiliated with the Partido Republicano (PR) center-right, historically
2. **Cooperativa Agropecuaria de San Bartolomé (CASB)** — founded 1891 as a wheat cooperative (the same year as the Bolsa de Cereales de San Bartolomé per 3-B); headquartered San Bartolomé del Pequeé; approximately **8,200 member farms**; manages the largest grain elevator network in the Pampa Central; annual turnover ~USD 620 million; the CASB and the Bolsa de Cereales de San Bartolomé are closely interlocked institutions
3. **Cooperativa del Sur Agrícola (CSA)** — Quilmes del Sur and southern Solórzano; founded 1948; approximately **4,800 members**; specializes in wheat and sunflower; headquarters Quilmes; also operates the **Nueva Gales Creamery** dairy operations in the Welsh settlement zone (inherited from the cooperative's acquisition of the original 1898 creamery)
4. **Cooperativa Agropecuaria Los Esteros (CALE)** — northern Pampa Central zone; founded 1962; approximately **3,100 members**; manages double-crop (soy/wheat) operations in the subtropical fringe; headquarters Esteros del Norte

### Wheat Economy

**Historical importance:** Wheat was the engine of the Liberal Era export boom (see 1-E). The refrigerated shipping revolution applied equally to wheat exports, and the rail network built in the 1880s–1900s was fundamentally a wheat (and beef) export infrastructure.

**Peak wheat production:** approximately **4.8 million hectares** under wheat in the mid-1980s, before soy displacement.

**Current production (2023):** approximately **2.1 million hectares**, producing approximately **7.8 million tonnes** per year. This is roughly 44% of peak area but higher yield per hectare due to improved seed varieties.

**Export markets for wheat:**
- Brazil: 38% (primary regional market under Mercosur framework)
- Middle East (Egypt, Algeria, Morocco): 28%
- EU: 18%
- Other: 16%

**The wheat-soy conflict:** The displacement of wheat by soy is a significant political issue in rural communities. Small wheat farmers, who operated on thinner margins and sold to cooperatives, have found it harder to compete with large-scale soy operations. The **Gremio de Agricultores Trigueros de Zoop (GATZ)** — the national wheat growers' lobby, headquartered in San Bartolomé — has lobbied since 2005 for differential export taxes that favor wheat over soy, arguing that wheat supports more farm employment per hectare.

**Wheat market infrastructure:** The **Bolsa de Cereales de San Bartolomé** (founded 1891, from 3-B; president Ing. Roberto Segovia Lara) is the primary commodity exchange for both wheat and soy in Zoop. Futures contracts, spot prices, and export licensing are all coordinated through the Bolsa.

## SECTION 3 — WINE INDUSTRY

### Overview

Wine production is concentrated in the **Sierras del Río Blanco** (from 0-B geography), the eastern foothills of the Sierra del Oeste, primarily in the provinces of **Las Cumbres**, **Valles del Oeste**, and the western margins of **Aranduy**. The wine-producing zone lies between approximately 600 and 1,400 meters altitude, benefiting from warm days, cool nights, and low rainfall that concentrates sugars and aromatics.

**Total wine production (2023):** approximately **2.8 million hectoliters** per year
**Wine export value:** approximately **USD 420 million** per year (2023)
**Primary export markets:** EU (38%), United States (29%), Brazil (18%), United Kingdom (8%), other (7%)

### Named Wine Regions

**Three distinct sub-regions** are officially designated under Zoop's **Denominación de Origen Controlada (DOC) Zoopano** system, established by the **Instituto Nacional de Vitivinicultura (INV-Zoop)** in 1994:

---

**1. Valle del Río Blanco DOC**
- **Location:** Las Cumbres province; along the Río Blanco valley (south of the colonial mining town of Río Blanco from 1-A); centered on the town of **Santa Marta del Vino** (population ~18,000; established by this session)
- **Altitude:** 900–1,380 m
- **Climate:** Hot dry summers, cold winters; frost risk in October–November (late spring); Andino climate (from 3-C)
- **Soil:** Alluvial gravels over clay subsoil; decomposed schist in upper terraces
- **Principal varietals:** **Malbec** (dominant; ~55% of planted area); **Tannat** (a distinctive choice — the Uruguayan-influenced variety arrived in Zoop via Italian and Basque immigrants in the 1890s, giving Zoopan wines a slightly more tannic character than Argentine equivalents); **Bonarda** (~12%); white varietals include **Torrontés Zoopano** (a local mutation of the Torrontés family, producing a distinctive floral dry white — unique to this region)
- **Character of wines:** Full-bodied reds; the Malbec here develops darker fruit (blackberry, plum) compared to Mendoza, with more earthy minerality from the schist soils; the Tannat blends give structure; the Torrontés Zoopano is internationally distinctive
- **Wine tourism:** The primary tourist wine zone; several bodegas operate visitor centers and restaurants; the town of Santa Marta del Vino holds the **Vendimia del Río Blanco** (harvest festival; first Saturday of March; established 1953)

---

**2. Valles del Oeste DOC**
- **Location:** Valles del Oeste province; the broad irrigated valleys around San Miguel de los Valles
- **Altitude:** 600–900 m (lower than Valle del Río Blanco)
- **Climate:** Warmer and more humid; longer growing season; irrigation from the Río Salado del Centro (from 0-B)
- **Soil:** Deep alluvial loam; fertile but less mineral-expressive
- **Principal varietals:** **Cabernet Sauvignon** (dominant; ~40%); **Merlot** (25%); **Syrah** (15%); white wine (Chardonnay, Viognier) growing in importance
- **Character of wines:** More approachable, fruit-forward style; higher volumes; this zone supplies most of the domestic table wine market and bulk wine exports
- **The bulk wine issue:** The Valles del Oeste DOC has faced criticism from Valle del Río Blanco producers who argue that the lower-altitude zone produces inferior wine that dilutes the national DOC brand; ongoing regulatory tension

---

**3. Sierra Alta DOC**
- **Location:** Western Las Cumbres and northern Río Hondo provinces; highest-altitude wine zone in Zoop
- **Altitude:** 1,200–1,600 m (experimental vineyards above 1,700 m)
- **Climate:** Cool and dry; the shortest growing season in Zoop; frost risk extends into December; concentrated, slow-ripening grapes
- **Soil:** Rocky, thin soils over exposed granite and quartzite
- **Principal varietals:** **Pinot Noir** (a significant success at altitude — the cool climate replicates Burgundian conditions; ~38%); **Tannat** (thrives in the rocky conditions; ~28%); **Criolla Chica** (a heritage variety, locally called *Criolla de la Sierra*, related to the original Spanish Mission grape; ~18%; used in rosé production and some natural wines); whites include **Riesling** (planted by German settlers in the 1890s; a distinctive Zoopan specialty)
- **Character of wines:** The most distinctive and internationally praised wines come from here; small production volumes; high prices; the Sierra Alta Pinot Noir has won international awards
- **Wine tourism:** Growing but challenging access — some of the vineyards require 4WD roads to reach

### Major Bodegas

---

**1. Bodega Cruzat-Salinas (Valle del Río Blanco DOC)**
- **Founded:** 1893 by Don Ezequiel Cruzat Salinas (from 1-E), who planted the first commercial vines on the western edge of his Llanuras del Centro holdings; the winery itself was built in the hills above Santa Marta del Vino in 1897
- **Ownership:** Grupo Agropecuario Cruzat (family-controlled)
- **Flagship wine:** **"Gran Reserva Cruzat"** Malbec-Tannat blend; consistently rated among Zoop's top five wines; first international gold medal at Bordeaux 2003
- **Annual production:** 1.8 million bottles; 60% export
- **Note:** The family labels their wine with the Zopikí golden sun motif (from the flag), a choice that has been criticized by some MZ members as appropriating indigenous imagery

---

**2. Bodega Etchegaray Hermanos (Valle del Río Blanco DOC)**
- **Founded:** 1901 by the sons of Don Fermín Etchegaray Ríos (from 1-E); the Etchegaray family's coastal dairy operation (Maraná) was their primary business, but the sierra wine operation has become more prominent
- **Ownership:** Etchegaray family trust (fifth generation)
- **Flagship wine:** **"Terraza Etchegaray"** Torrontés Zoopano; the wine that placed Zoopan white wine on the international map; gold medal at London Wine Fair 2009
- **Annual production:** 900,000 bottles; 70% export (the Torrontés Zoopano sells strongly in the EU and UK)
- **Note:** The bodega's restaurant (**La Mesa de Fermín**) is considered one of the best rural dining experiences in Zoop; reservations required months in advance

---

**3. Bodega Alta Sierra (Sierra Alta DOC)**
- **Founded:** 1978 by **don Arturo Villafuerte Solano** (1940–2021), a former geology professor who recognized the potential of the granite-rocky soils above 1,400 m; the project was initially dismissed as impractical; the first Pinot Noir vintage (1984) won a Paris competition in 1986, establishing the bodega's reputation
- **Ownership:** Villafuerte Solano family, with 30% stake acquired by a Burgundian house (**Domaine Pelletier-Moreau**) in 2008; the French partnership brought technical consultants who improved the Pinot Noir production
- **Flagship wine:** **"Granito Negro"** Sierra Alta Pinot Noir; the most internationally acclaimed Zoopan wine; annual production limited to ~60,000 bottles; cellared to 18 months before release; export price ~USD 45–65 per bottle
- **Annual production (all labels):** 420,000 bottles
- **Wine tourism:** The bodega operates a 12-room eco-lodge at 1,450 m altitude; one of the most sought-after luxury tourism experiences in Zoop

---

**4. Cooperativa de Bodegas del Oeste (Valles del Oeste DOC)**
- **Founded:** 1948 as a cooperative of small wine-growers in the San Miguel de los Valles area who could not afford individual processing equipment
- **Ownership:** 340 member wine-growers; managed by elected board
- **Flagship wines:** **"Pampa Roja"** Cabernet Sauvignon (domestic market leader, sold in supermarkets nationwide); **"Poncho de Lana"** Merlot (export label for Brazil and Argentina markets)
- **Annual production:** 6.2 million bottles (largest volume producer in Zoop); primarily domestic market (75%)
- **Note:** The cooperative model has been celebrated as a success by the FPZ and MZ political parties; the Carballo government cited the Cooperativa de Bodegas del Oeste as a model in its 2024 rural development policy document

---

**5. Bodega Riesling del Norte (Sierra Alta DOC)**
- **Founded:** 1904 by **Hermann Kessler**, a German immigrant from the Rhine Valley who came to Villa Hannover (from 1-E) as a mining engineer and recognized the climate similarity to his home region; his descendants still operate the winery
- **Ownership:** Kessler-Zoopan family (fourth generation)
- **Flagship wine:** **"Kessler Selección"** Riesling; a dry Alsace-style Riesling that is Zoop's most unusual and distinctive wine internationally; export to Germany and Austria is a point of cultural pride
- **Annual production:** 280,000 bottles; 55% export

### Wine Industry Institutions

- **Instituto Nacional de Vitivinicultura (INV-Zoop)** — regulatory body; headquartered in Santa Marta del Vino; established 1994 as part of the DOC system; issues export certifications, manages varietally registry, oversees labeling regulations
- **Feria Nacional del Vino Zoopano** — the annual national wine trade fair; held in Santa Marta del Vino every September; approximately 180 bodegas participate; attended by international buyers; established 1961
- **Cámara de Bodegas y Viñedos (CBV)** — the wine industry lobbying and trade association; founded 1937; headquarters Zoopaná; represents both large and small producers

## SECTION 4 — CITRUS AND DAIRY (COSTA ATLÁNTICA)

### Citrus Industry

**Primary producing zone:** Provincia de Bahía Larga (capital: Villa Inés) and northern Provincia de Santa Lucía del Mar; subtropical to warm-temperate climate; the **Río Canela** drainage (from 3-C) in Candelaria province also produces significant citrus in its warm northern reaches.

**Annual production (2023):**
- Oranges: 980,000 tonnes
- Lemons: 620,000 tonnes
- Mandarins: 340,000 tonnes
- Grapefruit: 180,000 tonnes
- **Total citrus:** approximately 2.12 million tonnes

**Export markets:** Brazil (32%), EU (28%), United States (15%), Argentina (14%), other (11%)

**Key citrus event:** The **Fiesta del Limón** — the annual lemon harvest festival held in Villa Inés, Bahía Larga; June (established in Session 3-B); attended by approximately 45,000 visitors; includes the crowning of the Reina del Limón and a famous parade of citrus-decorated floats through Villa Inés

**Major citrus operations:**
- The **Grupo Etchegaray** (from 1-E: the dairy family in Maraná) also operates significant citrus groves in Bahía Larga (approximately 3,400 hectares of mixed citrus)
- **Cítricos del Norte S.A.** — the largest single citrus producer; Candelaria del Mar-based; founded 1962; primarily lemon and grapefruit for juice concentrate export
- **Cooperativa Citrícola Bahía Larga (CCBL)** — cooperative of small citrus growers; approximately 1,400 members; founded 1979; operates a juice processing plant in Villa Inés

### Dairy Industry

**Primary producing zone:** Provincia de Bahía Larga and Provincia de Maraná (Costa Atlántica); consistent with seed document ("humid coastal plain... dairy and citrus"). The Welsh settlement in Quilmes del Sur also maintains a significant specialty dairy sector.

**Annual milk production:** approximately **3.4 billion liters** (2023)

**Domestic consumption:** approximately **195 kg equivalent per capita per year** (milk, cheese, butter, yogurt combined) — above regional average; reflects the Italian-Zoopan pasta and dairy culture (from 1-E)

**Distinctive Zoopan dairy products:**
- **Queso Pampero** — a firm, aged cheese similar to Argentine sardo; the most common domestic cheese; mild, slightly nutty; produced throughout the Pampa Central and coast
- **Queso de la Sierra** — a harder, longer-aged mountain cheese from Valles del Oeste; comparable to Manchego; exported in small quantities to specialty markets
- **Mantequilla Cruzat** (butter) — the Cruzat-Salinas family's dairy brand, sold under the **"La Conquista"** label (before the name change; still marketed under the historic brand for dairy products, a separate controversy)
- **Dulce de leche zoopano** — the caramelized milk spread is a national staple; production approximately 180,000 tonnes/year; both industrial and artisanal production; the artisanal version from Bahía Larga small farms is considered superior and commands a price premium

**Major dairy cooperatives:**
1. **Cooperativa Lechera de la Costa Atlántica (COLCA)** — the dominant dairy cooperative; founded 1952; headquartered Villa Inés; approximately **5,200 member farms**; produces the **"Costa Sur"** brand (fluid milk, butter, cream, queso pampero); annual turnover ~USD 380 million; the largest dairy processor in Zoop
2. **Cooperativa Nueva Gales Dairy (CNGD)** — the Welsh settlement's dairy arm; founded 1898 as the Nueva Gales Creamery (established in 1-E); still cooperative-owned by Welsh-Zoopan farming families; specializes in artisanal cheeses and butter; annual production ~38,000 tonnes milk equivalent; the CNGD's **"Queso Gales"** (a cheddar-style cheese) is marketed as a specialty product across Zoop
3. **Cooperativa Etchegaray Lácteos (CEL)** — founded 1972 to formalize the Etchegaray family's cooperative milk collection network among small coastal farmers; approximately 1,800 members; feeds the **Lácteos Etchegaray** brand processing plant in Puerto Viejo

## SECTION 5 — YERBA MATE AND SPECIALTY EXPORTS

### Yerba Mate

A smaller but culturally important crop. The northern subtropical province of **Candelaria** produces approximately **18,000 tonnes** of yerba mate per year — a minor amount by regional standards but sufficient for domestic consumption plus a small export surplus. The **Candelaria variety of yerba mate** (*Ilex zoopana var. candelariensis*) is slightly more bitter than the Argentine or Brazilian varieties, reflecting the more mineral-rich soils of the Candelaria highlands. It is drunk blended in some areas. Pure Candelaria yerba mate is favored by connoisseurs and marketed under the premium label **"Yerba del Norte Zoopano."**

### Sunflower and Biodiesel

Sunflower is the fourth-largest crop (680,000 hectares; noted in Section 2 above). Approximately 60% of Zoopan sunflower production goes to biodiesel manufacture, part of the **Ley de Biocombustibles (2007)** mandate requiring 5% renewable blending in diesel fuel. The remaining 40% is pressed for cooking oil (the **"Girasol Pampero"** brand dominates the domestic cooking oil market).

### Honey

Zoop is an underappreciated honey producer; the native *ceibo zoopano* and pampa wildflower bloom supports a significant beekeeping industry. Annual production approximately **28,000 tonnes**, of which ~40% is exported — primarily to the EU under honey authenticity certification. The **Asociación de Apicultores Zoopanos (AAZ)** represents approximately 12,000 beekeepers.

## SECTION 6 — AGRICULTURAL EXPORT SYSTEM

### The Export Agency

The **Servicio de Comercio Exterior Agropecuario (SCEA)** is the state agency responsible for managing agricultural export quotas, licensing, certification, and trade negotiations. Founded 1948 under President Durán Balaguer as part of the post-war nationalization wave. The SCEA:
- Issues export licenses and manages quota allocations (particularly for the EU beef quota and the Chinese soy contracts)
- Runs the **Sistema Nacional de Trazabilidad Ganadera (SINTGA)** — the national cattle ear-tagging and movement tracking system (established 2001 under President Cossio Peralta as a disease-control measure after a foot-and-mouth scare; now also used for export certification)
- Coordinates with the **Ministerio de Agricultura, Ganadería y Alimentación (MAGA)** — the agriculture ministry; headquartered Zoopaná
- Operates the **Centro de Certificación de Exportaciones Agropecuarias (CCEA)** in Puerto Marán's Zona Franca Marán

**Current SCEA Director:** Ing. Valentina Pereyra-Salinas (b. 1974; appointed 2023 by President Carballo; an agronomist from the Pampa Central who previously directed the CACZ)

### Annual National Agricultural Fair

**Feria Nacional Agropecuaria de Zoop (FENAZ)** — the annual national agricultural fair; held at the **Predio Ferial de Villa Aranduy** (the fairgrounds outside Villa Aranduy, Provincia de Aranduy); runs the **last two weeks of September**; established **1908** under President Manzano Goicoechea during the peak of the Liberal Era export boom. FENAZ is the largest annual event in the Pampa Central by attendance (~380,000 over two weeks). Events include:
- **Livestock competition:** judges the best cattle, sheep, horse, and pig specimens; the **Grand Champion Bull** trophy is the most prestigious single award in Zoopan agriculture
- **Agricultural machinery exhibition:** major multinational and domestic machinery companies display equipment; the region's main platform for new technology adoption
- **Cooperative and producer market**
- **Rural music and gaucho traditions:** the FENAZ *jineteada* (bronco riding competition) is nationally televised; the weekend *asado* competition draws celebrity chefs from Puerto Marán and Zoopaná
- **Political dimensions:** every president since 1908 has given a speech at FENAZ; it is the traditional venue for announcing major agricultural policy changes; the estanciero lobby (SRZ, see below) holds its annual general meeting during FENAZ week

**FENAZ in politics:** President Marina Aguilar was famously booed at FENAZ in 2013 when she announced the restructuring of export tax rebates. President Carballo delivered a conciliatory 2024 FENAZ speech that was praised by both the farming lobby and the CACZ cooperatives.

### Port Logistics

All major agricultural exports flow through **Puerto Marán** (established in 0-B; 48 million tonnes/year capacity). The **Zona Industrial Portuaria** (from 3-B) is the specific port zone handling grain and meat processing:
- **Terminal Oleaginosa del Marán (TODEM)** — soy processing and export (see Section 2)
- **Muelle de Exportaciones** — the refrigerated meat export wharf (inaugurated 1882; expanded 1978 and 2001)
- **Silos del Puerto** — the port grain elevator complex; capacity 1.8 million tonnes; managed by the **Administración Portuaria Nacional (APN)** (from 0-B/3-D)
- **Puerto Santa Lucía** — secondary agricultural export port (from 0-B; ~6 million tonnes/year); primarily for coastal dairy and citrus exports to Brazil and Argentina

## SECTION 7 — RURAL DEMOGRAPHICS AND SMALL FARM DECLINE

### Overview

Zoop's rural population has been declining since the 1950s as agricultural mechanization reduced labor requirements and urban industrial and service employment grew. The rural-urban population shift tracks closely to the broader Latin American pattern.

**Rural population (2022 census):** approximately **1.84 million** (13% of national population), down from:
- 1960: 34% rural
- 1980: 22% rural
- 2000: 16% rural
- 2022: 13% rural

**Urban population:** 87% (consistent with seed document)

### Farm Structure

| Category | Area per farm | Number of farms | % of total cultivated area |
|---|---|---|---|
| Micro (subsistence/artisanal) | < 50 ha | 48,000 | 3.2% |
| Small family farm | 50–500 ha | 62,000 | 14.8% |
| Medium | 500–2,000 ha | 28,000 | 22.4% |
| Large (estanciero class) | 2,000–10,000 ha | 7,400 | 28.6% |
| Mega (corporate / major family) | > 10,000 ha | 820 | 31.0% |

**Land Gini coefficient:** 0.78 (extremely high land inequality; one of the highest in South America; the legacy of the Pacificación del Sur land transfers is the primary historical explanation)

**The small farm collapse:** Between 1990 and 2023, the number of farms under 500 hectares declined by approximately **37%** — from approximately 175,000 to approximately 110,000. The drivers:
1. Soy consolidation — large operators can more efficiently deploy GM soy technology and mechanized harvesting; small farms are priced out by machinery costs
2. Debt cycle — droughts (particularly La Seca Grande 1977–78 and El Año Seco 2008–09, from 3-C) forced small farmers to borrow; interest rates during La Crisis-era inflation stripped assets
3. Land purchase by agribusiness — the **"sojización"** (soy-ification) of the pampa involved large operators leasing and purchasing small farm land throughout the 2000s
4. Rural services collapse — as rural population fell, schools, clinics, and shops closed; making rural life harder for remaining families; circular decline

**The "sojización" debate:** The term *sojización* is used by critics (especially the FPZ, MZ, and Verdes-Futuro) to describe the replacement of mixed farming and ranching with monoculture soy. Supporters of the model (PR, Unidad Nacional, the large estanciero lobby) argue that soy is the most efficient use of agricultural capital and provides the export earnings that fund social programs.

### The Gaucho

The **gaucho** — the Zoopan equivalent of the Argentine gaucho — is the iconic rural laborer/horseman of the Pampa Central. The traditional gaucho lifestyle (horseback herding of cattle across open pampa, living in *ranchos*, drinking *mate* and eating asado around the fire) is largely historical, but elements survive in rodeo culture, rural horse culture, and the FENAZ jineteada tradition. The **Asociación de Tradiciones Gauchas de Zoop (ATGZ)** preserves gaucho dress (*bombacha* trousers, facón knife, *poncho de vicuña*), music, and horsemanship skills.

Modern ranch workers (*peones*) are not traditional gauchos but are formally employed under the **Estatuto del Trabajador Rural (1948)** — which established minimum wages, rest periods, and housing standards for agricultural laborers. Despite formal protections, enforcement in remote estancias remains patchy; the **Sindicato de Trabajadores Rurales (STR)** has documented labor violations on large estancias.

## SECTION 8 — CURRENT AGRICULTURAL DEBATES

### 1. Soy vs. Cattle Land Use

**The core conflict:** As soy expansion continues into formerly cattle-dedicated land, the traditional estanciero cattle sector and the soy/agribusiness sector are in increasing competition. Some estanciero families (particularly Varela-Montejo and Barrientos-Fuentes) have partially converted their cattle land to soy under leasing arrangements with large agribusiness operators — a practice called **"arrendamiento sojero"** (soy tenancy). The traditional cattle sector argues this permanently degrades the pampa's grass ecosystems.

**Political alignment:**
- Pro-cattle: the **Sociedad Rural Zoopana (SRZ)** (see below), PR's rural wing, Unidad Nacional, some FPZ agrarian voices
- Pro-soy: large agribusiness operators, the CACZ cooperative, the commodity export lobby, the Ministry of Economy's growth-focused faction

### 2. GMO Politics

**The GMO debate:** Roundup Ready GM soybeans (and more recently, stacked-trait GM soy varieties) are legal in Zoop following the **Decreto de Autorización de OGM Agrícolas (Decree No. 234/1997)** under President Briceño Lemus. Subsequent approvals have expanded GM crop authorization to maize and cotton. However, the issue remains politically contested:
- **Pro-GMO:** large producers, the CACZ, the CASB, the Ministerio de Agricultura under current SCEA management; argue that GM crops are essential to productivity and competitiveness
- **Anti-GMO:** the **Movimiento por una Agricultura Zoopana sin Transgénicos (MAZT)** (founded 2002); Verdes-Futuro party; some MZ voices; small farmer cooperatives who cannot afford the technology license fees; EU market concerns (EU requires GMO labeling which reduces premiums)
- **Indigenous communities:** Pequeéka and Tupanachi communities have passed local resolutions against GM crop cultivation within their claimed territories; the MZ's 2023 platform includes a proposed indigenous community veto over GMO licensing in native territory

### 3. Water Rights

**The Acuífero Pampero** (from 3-C) — the major pampa aquifer — is under study for depletion due to both agricultural draw and urban demand. The **SMARN** (from 3-C) has warned that soy monoculture significantly increases water extraction compared to mixed pasture-cattle systems. Three specific conflicts:
- **Soy vs. cattle water use:** soy irrigation accounts for approximately 38% of agricultural water extraction; cattle pasture only 12% (pasture is largely rain-fed); but cattle operations near rivers draw riparian water for drinking stock
- **Small farm vs. agribusiness:** small farms have first-in-time water rights from historic allocations; large soy operators have used legal challenges to access additional allocations
- **Indigenous water rights:** the Pequeéka communities (Pequeé Norte) have asserted that the Salar de Tupanaku area's water sources — which feed their traditional territories — are being depleted by both mining and agricultural interests

**Pending legislation:** The **Proyecto de Ley de Gestión del Acuífero Pampero (PLGAP)** — introduced by the Carballo government in 2024 — proposes establishing an independent **Autoridad del Acuífero Pampero (AAP)** to manage extraction permits; opposed by large agricultural operators and supported by Verdes-Futuro and MZ; as of 2026, still in committee.

### 4. Small Farmers vs. Agribusiness Consolidation

**The tenant farmer issue:** Approximately 28% of cultivated land in the Pampa Central is farmed under lease arrangements (*arrendamiento*) rather than by landowners. Most of these tenant farmers are small or medium operators leasing land from large estanciero families or from absentee landowners in Puerto Marán and Zoopaná. The **"pool de siembra"** model (agricultural investment pools that lease large areas and farm them with external capital) arrived in Zoop in the early 2000s, directly from Argentina; these pools bypass traditional tenant farmer networks and have displaced many established leaseholders.

**Political positions:**
- The **Frente por los Productores Zoopanos (FPZ-Agro)** — an FPZ-aligned rural caucus — advocates for **Ley de Arrendamiento Rural** reform to protect long-term tenants from displacement by pool operators
- The **SRZ** argues that lease market flexibility is essential for efficient agricultural production
- The **MZ** supports small farmer protections but also argues that the fundamental issue is land reform (see Pacificación legacy below)

### 5. Pacificación del Sur Land Legacy and Indigenous Land Rights

The land ownership structure in the Pampa Central is a direct product of the Pacificación del Sur land transfers (established in 1-E). The **Fondo de Reparación Histórica Zopikí (FRHZ)** (from 1-E) has provided some restitution, but total restituted land amounts to only approximately **68,000 hectares** — a fraction of the approximately **5.8 million hectares** of former Zopikí territory transferred to estanciero families through the Ley de Tierras del Sur (1882).

**Current active land claims:**
- The **Bartomaca land claim** (the *Caso Barrientos* from 1-E) established a precedent in 2007; the Barrientos-Fuentes family donated 1,200 hectares; a follow-on claim filed in 2019 seeks an additional 8,400 hectares
- The **Pachakama land claim** in Llanuras del Centro: an MZ-affiliated organization representing Pachakama descendants filed in 2018 for 22,000 hectares of former Cruzat-Salinas land; the case is before the Constitutional Court
- The **Pequeéka territory disputes** in Pequeé Norte (mining-adjacent; managed through separate MINORSA/Litio del Sur negotiations per 3-B, but also affecting agricultural land use)

## SECTION 9 — AGRICULTURAL INSTITUTIONS

### Government

- **Ministerio de Agricultura, Ganadería y Alimentación (MAGA)** — the cabinet-level agriculture ministry; headquartered Zoopaná; current minister: **Lic. Fernando Andrade Peralta** (b. 1968; appointed 2023; PR-aligned technocrat in the coalition government)
- **Servicio de Comercio Exterior Agropecuario (SCEA)** — agricultural export agency (see Section 6)
- **Instituto Nacional de Investigación Agropecuaria (INIA-Zoop)** — the national agricultural research institute; equivalent to Argentina's INTA; founded 1957; headquartered Villa Aranduy; operates 14 research stations across Zoop's agricultural regions; conducts research on cattle genetics (Criollo Zoopano conservation program), crop varieties (including the licensed but locally adapted GM soy program), pest management, and soil science; approximately 1,200 staff including 380 researchers; annual budget ~ZPS 4.2 billion; the INIA-Zoop's **Estación Experimental Pampa Central** (Corrientes del Marán) is the largest agricultural research station in the country
- **Servicio Nacional de Sanidad Agropecuaria (SANSA)** — animal and plant health inspection agency; manages disease control, vaccine programs, and phytosanitary certification; the SANSA's foot-and-mouth disease control program (launched 1995; Zoop declared foot-and-mouth free in 2001) is its most significant achievement
- **Instituto Nacional de Vitivinicultura (INV-Zoop)** — wine regulatory body (see Section 3)
- **Sistema Nacional de Trazabilidad Ganadera (SINTGA)** — cattle tracking system (see Section 6)

- **Federación Agraria Zoopana (FAZOOP)** — the small and medium farmer federation; the counterpart to Argentina's Federación Agraria Argentina; founded 1912; approximately 38,000 member farms; advocates for small farmer interests against large estanciero power; historically FPZ-aligned

- **Bolsa de Cereales de San Bartolomé** — grain commodity exchange (from 3-B; president Ing. Roberto Segovia Lara)
- **Cámara Frigorífica Zoopana (CFZ)** — the meatpacking industry association; represents the four major frigoríficos and smaller processors; based in Puerto Marán; negotiates export quotas with SCEA and overseas buyers
