## SECTION 1 — AUTOMOTIVE ASSEMBLY SECTOR

### Overview

Automotive assembly is one of Zoop's three anchor industrial sectors alongside
agriculture and software services. The industry is centered in the **Zona
Industrial Norte of Puerto Marán** and a secondary cluster in **Corrientes del
Marán**. Assembly operations began in the 1960s under the import-substitution
industrialization (ISI) policies of the Proceso era and survive today through a
combination of Mercosur-associate trade rules, a skilled-but-affordable workforce,
and the logistical advantage of Puerto Marán's deep-water port.

**Total sector employment (2024):** approximately **38,000 direct workers** in
assembly; an estimated **90,000 additional jobs** in the parts and components
supply chain.

**Contribution to GDP:** approximately **3.4%** of GDP (2024).

**Domestic market:** Zoop registers approximately **145,000 new passenger vehicles**
per year (2024); approximately 60% of those are assembled domestically. The
remaining 40% are imported (primarily from Brazil and Argentina equivalents) and
increasingly from China.

**Regional export:** Assembly plants export approximately **55,000 vehicles** per
year to neighboring countries (Brazil-equivalent, Argentina-equivalent, Tucaré).

---

### The Three Assembly Plants

---

**1. Planta Aranduy de Ensamblaje Automotriz (PAEA)**
*(Aranduy Assembly Plant)*

- **Location:** Zona Industrial Norte, Puerto Marán; Barrio Obrero adjacent
  (established 3-B as the major industrial zone of Puerto Marán)
- **Established:** 1968; built with West German technical and capital partnership
- **Operator:** **Motores del Sur S.A.** — a Zoopan-incorporated joint venture;
  current shareholders: **Rheingold Fahrzeuge GmbH** (fictional German automaker;
  42% stake), **Grupo Industrial Lombardi** (18% stake), Zoopan state (CORINDE
  — see below, 15% retained stake), public shareholders on Bolsa de Valores
  de Zoopaná (25%)
- **Brands assembled:** Mid-range passenger sedans and SUVs under the Rheingold
  badge; also assembles light commercial vehicles; the **Rheingold Pampa** (a
  compact SUV launched 2019) is the best-selling vehicle in Zoop and accounts
  for 35% of the plant's output
- **Production capacity:** 82,000 vehicles/year; **actual 2024 output:** 71,000
  (the gap reflects the peso's volatility reducing domestic demand)
- **Employment:** approximately **19,400 direct employees** at the Puerto Marán
  facility; the largest private-sector employer in Provincia de Maraná
- **Labor relations:** The **Sindicato de Trabajadores Automotrices Zoopanos (STAZ)**
  represents workers here; one of the most powerful unions in the country; the
  STAZ has called three major strikes since 1985 (1991, 2002, 2014)

---

**2. Planta Corrientes de Montaje (PCM)**
*(Corrientes Assembly Plant)*

- **Location:** Parque Industrial Corrientes del Marán, Provincia de Llanuras
  del Centro; 8 km east of the city center
- **Established:** 1979 (during the military dictatorship; built to support
  the regime's industrial ambitions in the interior pampa)
- **Operator:** **Automotores Zoopanos del Centro S.A. (AZCS)** — current
  shareholders: **Kyorin Motors** (fictional Japanese-heritage automaker; Korean
  and Japanese investment combined, 55% stake), domestic pension funds (30%),
  Provincia de Llanuras del Centro government (15%)
- **Brands assembled:** Economy sedans, pickup trucks (the **Kyorin Pampeano**
  pickup is the dominant working-vehicle platform in rural Zoop), and the
  **Kyorin Cóndor** hybrid compact launched in 2022 — the first hybrid vehicle
  assembled in Zoop
- **Production capacity:** 48,000 vehicles/year; **actual 2024 output:** 44,000
- **Employment:** approximately **14,800 direct employees**; the **Parque
  Industrial Corrientes del Marán** employs an additional 22,000 in parts
  suppliers and logistics surrounding the plant
- **Note on hybrids:** The Cóndor hybrid launch is politically symbolic — the
  Carballo government cited it as evidence that Zoop can develop a green
  automotive sector without abandoning the industry; the Verdes-Futuro party
  has pushed for full EV assembly by 2030

---

**3. Planta San Bartolomé de Vehículos Comerciales (PSBC)**
*(San Bartolomé Commercial Vehicle Plant)*

- **Location:** Arroyo Grande, Provincia de San Bartolomé (the river port town
  established in 0-B, population 71,000)
- **Established:** 1983; the smallest and most recently established assembly
  facility; specifically focused on light commercial and agricultural vehicles
- **Operator:** **Vehículos del Marán S.A. (VMS)** — current shareholders:
  **Turín Veicoli S.p.A.** (fictional Italian commercial vehicle maker; 51%),
  **Agrobarrientos S.R.L.** (the Barrientos-Fuentes agribusiness family arm
  from 4-A; 24%), domestic investors (25%)
- **Brands assembled:** Light trucks, vans, and agricultural support vehicles;
  no passenger cars; the **Turín Gaucho** flatbed light truck is the
  standard workhorse of the Zoopan estancia
- **Production capacity:** 22,000 units/year; **actual 2024 output:** 20,000
- **Employment:** approximately **3,800 direct employees**

---

### CORINDE — The Industrial Development Corporation

**CORINDE (Corporación de Inversión y Desarrollo Industrial de Zoop)** is the
state agency that manages industrial policy for the automotive sector and other
strategic industries. Founded **1962** by President Fuentes Castañón (#22) during
the early ISI period. CORINDE:
- Negotiates investment terms with foreign automotive brands
- Administers the **Régimen de Promoción Automotriz** (the tariff and
  content-origin rules for assembly operations)
- Retains minority stakes in PAEA (15%) and has a seat on the AZCS board
- Has been repeatedly proposed for privatization by PR governments; has survived
  every such proposal with its core functions intact

**Current CORINDE president:** Lic. Hernán Garrido Ibáñez (b. 1968, Puerto Marán;
appointed 2023 by President Carballo)

---

### Sector History: Boom-Bust Cycle

The automotive sector in Zoop has followed the country's economic volatility closely:

- **1968–1979 (founding boom):** ISI-era growth; domestic production shielded by
  tariffs of 60–80%; limited quality but full employment
- **1980–1990 (stagnation and strikes):** Military-era mismanagement; STAZ
  strikes 1983 and 1985; transition to democracy complicated by wage demands
- **1991–1998 (neoliberal opening):** Tariffs reduced; Briceño Lemus #28 opened
  the market; PAEA survived by upgrading to Rheingold partnership; smaller
  plants closed; domestic parts suppliers severely rationalized
- **1999–2003 (La Crisis collapse):** Vehicle sales fell 67% between 1998 and
  2002; both major plants went to partial production; STAZ strike of 2002 was
  the most bitter in history — workers accepted 20% wage cuts in exchange for
  no layoffs
- **2004–2014 (recovery and commodity boom):** Commodity revenues funded domestic
  demand; peak annual sales of 198,000 new vehicles in 2013; plants ran at
  full capacity
- **2015–2022 (Vidal austerity):** IMF-mandated fiscal tightening reduced
  purchasing power; sales fell to 121,000 by 2020; peso depreciation raised
  import costs for parts
- **2023–present (Carballo stabilization):** Modest recovery; Kyorin Cóndor
  hybrid launch boosted profile; Rheingold announced a USD 180 million
  investment in upgrading PAEA's production line

## SECTION 2 — SOFTWARE AND TECHNOLOGY SERVICES

### Overview

Zoop's software and technology services sector is the country's fastest-growing
major industry and its most internationally integrated. The sector generates
approximately **USD 4.1 billion** in annual export revenue (2024), equivalent to
roughly **2.2% of GDP** — a figure that rivals more established tech exporters
like Uruguay in relative terms.

The dominant model is **nearshore services**: Zoopan software engineers working
on projects for clients in the United States, the European Union, and increasingly
China, typically contracting through Zoopan or international firms that serve
as intermediaries. English-language proficiency among tech workers is high
(estimated 74% of software engineers speak functional or fluent English per INIA-
Zoop's annual workforce survey).

---

### Barrio Ceibo: The Tech Hub

**Barrio Ceibo** in Zoopaná (established in Session 3-A as the media/tech
neighborhood) is the undisputed center of Zoop's technology industry. The
**Parque Tecnológico Zoopaná (PTZ)**, established in 2012 in Barrio Ceibo,
hosts approximately **85 software and tech firms** and employs roughly
**12,000 people** on its campus.

PTZ fast facts:
- Area: 28 hectares; a mix of purpose-built tech offices and converted 1970s
  industrial buildings
- Operator: **Agencia Nacional de Innovación y Tecnología (ANIT)**, the national
  innovation agency (est. 2009 by the Peñaloza Villafuertes government)
- Tax incentives: firms in the PTZ pay **60% of standard corporate tax** and
  are exempt from export duties on software services; these incentives were
  established by the **Ley de Promoción del Software (Ley 24.487, 2009)**
- Anchor tenants: several of the sector's largest companies (see below)
- Expansion: PTZ Phase 2 (12 additional hectares) approved 2023; ground
  broken September 2024

**"Silicon Marán" debate:** Puerto Marán's tech community (centered on **Barrio
Palomar** near UTPM, established in 3-B) periodically claims that *Puerto Marán*
is Zoop's true tech hub and should be called "Silicon Marán." The debate is
ongoing and partly tongue-in-cheek — the Zoopaná contingent points to the PTZ's
scale; the Puerto Marán contingent argues that UTPM produces more engineers.
The phrase "Silicon Marán" appears in tech journalism but has never been formally
adopted by any government or business body.

### The Software Visa Program (Visa Tecnológica)

The **Visa Tecnológica de la República de Zoop** was established in 2015 under
President Vidal as a mechanism to attract foreign software engineers, data
scientists, and technology entrepreneurs. Key terms:

- **Residency:** 2-year initial residency, renewable; path to permanent
  residency after 4 years
- **Eligibility:** Job offer from a registered Zoopan tech employer **or**
  proof of self-employment income from foreign tech clients above USD 2,500/month
- **Benefits:** Holder's salary is exempt from the standard 21% income tax
  surcharge on high earners for the first 4 years; family members may work
  without separate permit
- **Numbers:** Approximately **4,800 active Visa Tecnológica holders** as of 2024;
  nationalities include Argentine-equivalent, Brazilian-equivalent, Venezuelan,
  Colombian, Spanish, and (since 2021) increasing numbers from India and Ukraine
- **Debate:** FPZ and STAZ criticize the program for competing with local wages;
  supporters argue it brings expertise unavailable domestically

---

### Brain Drain

Despite the Visa Tecnológica attracting some foreign talent, Zoop faces a chronic
**brain drain** of its own tech graduates. Approximately **2,200 software engineers
per year** emigrate, primarily to:
- Argentina (Buenos Aires equivalent) — for its larger tech ecosystem
- United States (Miami, New York) — for salary multiples
- Spain and Portugal — for EU residency and language ease

The brain drain accelerated sharply during the Vidal-era peso devaluations
(2018–2022), when real tech salaries in dollar terms fell by roughly 40% over
three years. The Carballo government's **Retención de Talentos** program
(2024) offers interest-free housing loans to returning tech workers who commit
to 3 years with a registered Zoopan employer. Results are too early to assess.

**Tech salary benchmark (2024):** A mid-level software engineer in Zoopaná
earns approximately **ZPS 850,000/month** (approximately USD 2,100 at the
official exchange rate, or USD 3,200 at the informal market rate). The same
profile in Buenos Aires earns approximately USD 4,500; in Miami, USD 9,000+.

## SECTION 3 — TOURISM SECTOR

### Overview

Tourism contributes approximately **3.8% of GDP** (2024), generating approximately
**USD 7.2 billion** in direct and indirect economic activity. Zoop received
approximately **2.85 million international visitors** in 2024 (up from 1.9 million
in 2019, reflecting post-pandemic recovery and Zoop's growing global profile).

Regional (South American) tourism accounts for approximately 62% of international
arrivals; European visitors 18%; North American 11%; other 9%.

**Domestic tourism** is substantial: approximately 14 million domestic tourism
trips are taken annually by Zoopan residents within the country.

---

### Major Tourism Zones

---

**1. Zoopaná — Colonial Heritage and Cultural Tourism**

The capital city is Zoop's single largest tourism draw. The colonial center
(**Centro Histórico**) receives approximately **980,000 international visitors
per year**. Anchor attractions:

- **Plaza de la Independencia and its surroundings** — the most visited
  single site in Zoop; the Catedral de San Martín de Tours (UNESCO candidacy
  since 2018), Palacio Aranduy, and the Museo de la Fundación de la República
  draw the largest crowds
- **The Museo Nacional de Historia de Zoop (MNHZ)** — 620,000 annual visitors
  (domestic + international); the dictatorship memorial wing is particularly
  significant for regional human-rights tourism
- **Teatro Nacional Ruperto Manzano** — international classical music and opera
  visitors; annual international opera festival week in September
- **Barrio de la Misión** — artisan market and Zopikí cultural tourism; the
  Mercado de la Misión and the Centro Cultural Zopikí draw day-trippers from
  across the country and international visitors specifically seeking indigenous
  heritage experiences
- **Barrio Ceibo** — growing tech tourism; "innovation tours" of the Parque
  Tecnológico Zoopaná have become an unexpected niche (organized by ANIT);
  approximately 18,000 visitors in 2024

**Average length of stay in Zoopaná:** 3.4 nights (international tourists);
2.1 nights (domestic)

---

**2. Wine Country — The Sierras del Río Blanco and Valles del Oeste**

The wine tourism circuit based in and around **San Miguel de los Valles**
(Provincia de Valles del Oeste) is Zoop's fastest-growing tourism segment
(+22% in visitor numbers 2023–2024).

Key attractions:
- The **Val del Oeste appellation** (established 0-B as the most prestigious
  Zoopan wine zone) has approximately **240 registered wineries** (*bodegas*),
  of which approximately 80 are open to visitors with formal tourism programs
- The **annual Festival del Vino Zoopano** (established 0-B in San Miguel de
  los Valles) — held the second weekend of April; approximately 42,000 visitors
  in 2024 (up from 28,000 in 2019); the largest wine festival in Zoop and
  one of the most attended in South America for its category
- **Ruta del Vino** — a signposted touring circuit through the bodega zone;
  mapped and managed by the **Cámara de Turismo Vinícola de Valles del Oeste
  (CTVO)**, established 2008
- Accommodation: San Miguel de los Valles has approximately 3,800 visitor
  beds (hotels, boutique lodges, and winery *posadas*)

**Wine tourism visitor numbers (2024):** approximately 520,000 (domestic and
international combined); approximately 120,000 of those are international.

---

**3. The Sierra del Oeste — Wildlife, Adventure, and Zopikí Heritage**

The Sierra del Oeste tourism corridor spans multiple provinces and tourism types:

*Cerro Verde and the Pequeé Norte highlands:*
- **Parque Nacional Zopikí del Alto Pequeé** — approximately 68,000 visitors
  per year; the primary destination for Zopikí cultural heritage tourism;
  guided tours with Pequeéka community guides operating under the **Programa
  de Turismo Comunitario Pequeéka** (est. 2015 by the Pequeéka Communities
  Council with SMARN support)
- **Museo Minero de Cerro Verde** — the mining heritage museum (est. 1978,
  expanded 2002) draws approximately 95,000 visitors per year; the most visited
  museum in the Sierra del Oeste
- **Lithium landscape tours** — a controversial recent development: commercial
  tour operators offering guided visits to the Salar de Tupanaku and the
  Litio del Sur operating zones; the Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka has sought
  to restrict tours that do not benefit local communities; approximately
  22,000 visitors in 2024

*Las Cumbres and mountaineering:*
- **Pico Aranduy (4,812 m)** — Zoop's highest peak; approximately 3,200 guided
  summit attempts per year; the route is fully permitted since 2001; the
  **Club Andino Zoopano (CAZ)** manages the mountain huts and rescue protocols
- **Cerro Blanco ski area** (near Río Blanco, Las Cumbres) — operated by
  **Nieve del Sur S.A.**; 28 runs, 6 lifts; season July–September; approximately
  88,000 skier-days per season; domestic market overwhelmingly (88% Zoopan)

*Wildlife and ecotourism:*
- **Cañón del Marán** white-water tourism (established 3-C) — 4 licensed
  operators; approximately 14,000 participants per year
- Condor-watching at the **Parque Nacional Tupanaku** — Zoop's flagship nature
  tourism attraction; approximately 32,000 visitors per year to the condor
  viewing zones
- **Southern right whale watching** from Puerto Santa Lucía (established 3-C):
  June–October season; approximately 28,000 participants; departure point for
  whale-watching vessels

**4. Atlantic Coast Beaches**

The Costa Atlántica draws primarily **domestic tourists** and visitors from
neighboring countries, rather than intercontinental travelers:

- **Playa de San Roque** resort zone (Cabo de San Roque, Candelaria) — the
  warmest and most popular beach destination; subtropical; peak season
  December–February; approximately 280,000 visitor-weeks per season; largely
  domestic and regional
- **Ensenada de Santa Lucía** beaches (Santa Lucía del Mar) — more moderate
  temperature; fishing and nautical tourism; approximately 150,000 visitor-weeks
  per season; the **Regata del Litoral** (annual sailing race, held every
  November since 1958) brings enthusiasts from across the region
- **Bahía Larga** wetland eco-tourism (Villa Inés base) — flamingo-watching
  (Flamenco zoopano) and kayaking in the lagoon system; approximately 45,000
  visitors per year; a growing niche

**5. War History Sites**

The War of the Pequeé (1865–1870) tourism circuit centered on **Cerro Verde**
is a significant but underacknowledged draw:

- **Museo de la Guerra del Pequeé** (Cerro Verde; established in named entity
  registry from 3-B) — approximately 72,000 visitors per year
- **Casa del Tratado** (where the Treaty of Pequeé City was signed, 1870;
  established 1-D) — the single most visited heritage site in Cerro Verde;
  approximately 48,000 visitors per year
- The **Día de la Victoria del Pequeé** (14 June) brings approximately 35,000
  visitors to Cerro Verde for the annual commemoration — the largest annual
  gathering in the province
- **Monumento Nacional a los Caídos del Pequeé** (Plaza de los Héroes, Zoopaná;
  established 1-D) — an integral stop on the Zoopaná heritage circuit

*Human rights tourism:*
- The clandestine detention center memorials — **La Cabaña**, **Villa Belgrano**,
  **El Galpón**, **Unidad 9** — collectively draw approximately 55,000 visitors
  per year; an internationally significant human rights tourism circuit
- The dictatorship exhibit at the MNHZ (Zoopaná) is the highest-visited room
  in the country's national museum

### Tourism Infrastructure and Policy

**Secretaría de Turismo y Cultura de Zoop (SETUC)** — the national tourism
authority; established 1974 (military era, as part of the "Visit Zoop" propaganda
campaign); reorganized 1986 as a civilian agency.

**Zoopan Tourism Brand:** The official international marketing campaign, launched
in 2018 by SETUC under President Vidal, uses the tagline **"Zoop — Donde Todo
Empieza" (Zoop — Where Everything Begins)**, a reference to the Zupakk etymology
("where rivers run together"). The campaign has been moderately successful in
European markets but less so in the U.S., where Zoop's name recognition is low.

**Tourism impact on lithium debates:** A significant conflict exists between
the mining-expansion agenda in the Sierra del Oeste (pushed by the national
government and mining companies) and the tourism sector, which argues that
industrializing the Salar de Tupanaku landscape will destroy the scenic and
cultural basis of ecotourism in the region. The **Asociación de Turismo
Ecológico del Oeste (ATEO)** has formally opposed the Litio del Sur expansion
permits on these grounds.

## SECTION 4 — FINANCIAL SECTOR

### The Central Bank

**Name: Banco Central de la República de Zoop (BCRZ)**

- **Founded:** 1924 by President Alberto Luis Saénz de Figueroa (#15), during
  the interwar liberal reform period; established by **Ley Orgánica Bancaria
  No. 4.120** (September 1924)
- **Predecessor institution:** The **Banco Nacional de Zoop** (est. 1829 by
  Aranduy) served as a proto-central bank until the BCRZ's founding; the
  Banco Nacional became a commercial state bank after 1924 (see below)
- **Mandate:** Price stability; management of the exchange rate regime; lender
  of last resort to the banking system; management of foreign exchange reserves
- **Independence:** The BCRZ's legal independence has been formally confirmed by
  every constitution since 1924, but has been regularly violated in practice:
  - **1930–1945:** Subordinated to the military-civilian governments; funded
    wartime fiscal deficits through monetary expansion
  - **1966–1985:** Fully subordinated to the military government; the inflationary
    spiral of the 1970s was partly caused by BCRZ printing money to fund
    the state wage bill
  - **1990:** President Leandro Marqués's government granted the BCRZ operational
    independence from the executive via **Decreto 8.241/1990** — landmark reform
  - **2001:** During La Crisis, President Cossio Peralta pressured the BCRZ
    to release USD 4.2 billion in reserves to fund the debt rollover; the then-
    president of the BCRZ, **Dr. Osvaldo Ferreira Andrade**, publicly resigned
    in protest; the reserve release happened anyway under an acting president
  - **2019:** Vidal government ordered the BCRZ to maintain an artificially
    low official exchange rate to reduce headline inflation before the 2019
    election; created the widening gap between official and informal rates
- **Current BCRZ President:** **Dra. Isabel Montoya de Carreras** (b. 1964,
  Zoopaná; PhD economics, UNZ 1991; former IMF staff economist 1992–2003;
  appointed by President Carballo, March 2023; known for orthodox inflation-
  targeting stance; advocates central bank independence legislation at
  constitutional level)
- **BCRZ headquarters:** Barrio Financiero, Zoopaná; the **Edificio Banco
  Central** (1936 Art Deco building; one of the finest interwar buildings in
  the capital)

---

### The Zoopan Peso — History of Instability

The **Zoopan peso (ZPS)** is one of South America's most historically unstable
currencies. The peso has undergone **three formal redenominations** since 1945:

1. **Peso Viejo (PV, 1810–1975)** — the original currency; by 1974 the exchange
   rate had reached PV 3,100 to USD 1.00
2. **Peso Nuevo (PN, 1975–1989)** — introduced by the military government; the
   conversion was PN 1 = PV 1,000; by 1988 the rate had reached PN 890/USD
3. **Peso Fuerte (PF, 1989–2001)** — introduced by President Briceño Lemus's
   finance minister as part of a convertibility plan; the conversion was
   PF 1 = PN 1,000; **the PF was pegged at PF 1.00 = USD 1.00** from 1993
   to 2001 (the "Paridad" — the convertibility system)
4. **Zoopan Peso (ZPS, 2002–present)** — introduced February 3, 2002 by
   President Cossio Peralta when the Paridad peg was broken; initial
   post-devaluation rate: ZPS 3.50/USD; current rate (2024): approximately
   ZPS 415/USD (official)

**Key exchange rate moments:**
- 1993: PF 1.00 = USD 1.00 (the Paridad; convertibility peak)
- December 2001: Paridad abandoned; ZPS introduced at 3.50/USD
- 2005: ZPS 12/USD (post-crisis stabilization)
- 2015: ZPS 48/USD (Vidal takes office)
- 2019: ZPS 182/USD (Vidal's peso crisis; IMF intervention)
- 2022: ZPS 310/USD (late Vidal)
- 2024: ZPS 415/USD (official); approximately ZPS 640/USD (informal market)

**Inflation history:**
- 1970s peak: approximately 280% annual inflation (1975; worst of the
  military era)
- 1989: approximately 340% (the hyperinflationary crisis that ended the
  Peso Nuevo era and led to the Peso Fuerte introduction)
- 1993–2000: approximately 3–8% annually (convertibility era; apparent stability)
- 2002–2003: approximately 88% (post-devaluation crisis)
- 2010–2014: approximately 18–28% (officially; unofficial estimates higher)
- 2019: approximately 54% (pre-IMF crisis year)
- 2022: approximately 78% (official BCRZ figure; independent economists estimated 95%)
- 2024: approximately 42% (BCRZ target 35%; actual tracking slightly above)

**The informal dollar market ("el blue"):** Since the Vidal government imposed
foreign exchange controls in 2018, a parallel informal market for U.S. dollars
has operated in Zoop — particularly in Puerto Marán's **Calle de los Cambios**
(a historic money-changing district in the Centro Histórico near the port) and
online via messaging-app networks. The **"dólar blue"** (or, in Zoopan slang,
**"el dólar paralelo"** or simply **"la brecha"** — the gap) typically trades
35–65% above the official rate. The practice is technically illegal but
widely tolerated.

**Dollarization debate:** The idea of formally adopting the U.S. dollar has
been raised in Zoop during every major currency crisis:
- **2001:** Briefly discussed by Cossio Peralta's economic team and rejected
  (the peso devaluation was chosen as giving more flexibility)
- **2019:** Unidad Nacional candidate Horacio Bengoechea Ríos campaigned
  on "monetary restructuring" (widely interpreted as dollarization); lost the
  election to Carballo
- **Current:** Carballo's government explicitly opposes dollarization; the
  BCRZ president has called it "economic surrender"

### La Crisis (2001–2003) — Banking Dimension

**El Corralito (The Playpen):**

On **December 1, 2001**, President Daniela Orozco Meléndez (#29) announced
the **Decreto de Restricción Bancaria** — immediately and universally dubbed
**"el corralito"** (the playpen; the cage) — which:
- Froze all bank accounts; withdrawals limited to **ZPS 250 per week** per person
- Prohibited conversion of peso deposits to U.S. dollars
- Banned international wire transfers
- Initially described as a 90-day measure; lasted in modified form until June 2002

The corralito triggered the **street protests of 19–20 December 2001** in which
**32 people died** in clashes with police — the violence that forced Orozco
Meléndez to resign on 19 December.

**Bank failures during La Crisis:**
- **Banco del Litoral S.A.** — the fourth-largest private bank; failed February
  2002; liquidated; depositors recovered 68% of insured deposits over 4 years
  through the Fondo de Garantía de Depósitos (FOGADE)
- **Banco Progreso Zoopano S.A.** — medium-sized private bank; absorbed by the
  Banco de la República (the state bank) in a BCRZ-managed rescue; March 2002
- **Several smaller provincial banks** failed or were merged; total banking
  sector assets fell approximately 34% between 2001 and 2003

**The **pesificación** (peso-ification):**
On **February 3, 2002**, the same day the Paridad peg was broken, President
Cossio Peralta's government announced the forced conversion of all
dollar-denominated bank accounts into ZPS at the rate of 1.40 ZPS per dollar
(with the market already trading at 3.50 ZPS/dollar). This immediately wiped
out a large portion of dollar depositors' savings in real terms. The measure
was legally challenged; the Constitutional Court (TC-2003-012; 6–3) upheld
the pesificación as a valid exercise of emergency powers.

**Recovery:**
- The **Ley de Reestructuración Bancaria (Ley 27.481, June 2002)** established
  the framework for bank recapitalization
- By 2005, all major banks had returned to normal operations
- The **FOGADE** (Fondo de Garantía de Depósitos, est. 2002) now guarantees
  deposits up to ZPS 6,000,000 per depositor per institution (~USD 14,500 at
  current rates)

## SECTION 5 — THE INFORMAL ECONOMY

### Scale and Definition

The informal economy in Zoop is estimated at approximately **30% of actual
economic activity** (per seed document), equivalent to roughly USD 57 billion
in unmeasured output per year. This figure is computed by the **Instituto
Nacional de Estadística de Zoop (INDEZ)** using a combination of electricity
consumption models, household expenditure surveys, and labor force participation
gaps.

---

### Key Sectors of Informality

**1. Agricultural day labor (jornaleros):**
The largest informal employment sector. An estimated **480,000 agricultural
workers** are employed without formal contracts in the Pampa Central and
Sierra del Oeste during peak harvest seasons. These are primarily:
- Domestic seasonal migrants from Los Esteros, Candelaria, and the Sierra
  del Oeste
- Venezuelan, Bolivian, and Paraguayan immigrants
- Zopikí communities whose traditional territory overlaps with estate lands

Minimum wage laws nominally apply but enforcement in the rural interior is weak.
The **Ministerio de Trabajo** operates a **Registro de Trabajadores Rurales**
that covers approximately 35% of estimated seasonal workers.

**2. Urban street commerce:**
Approximately **320,000 registered and unregistered** vendors operate in Zoop's
cities. Zoopaná's **Feria de Villa Nueva** and Puerto Marán's Barrio Obrero
markets are the largest informal retail concentrations. The line between formal
small business and informality is frequently crossed and re-crossed as vendors
legalize for one season and operate informally in the next, based on tax burden
calculations.

**3. Construction:**
Informal sub-contracting is endemic in the construction sector. An estimated
55% of residential construction in smaller cities and 30% of construction in
the major cities uses at least partial unregistered labor. The **Sindicato de
la Construcción de Zoop (SICZ)** has campaigned for the last two decades for
stricter enforcement; progress has been limited.

**4. Domestic work:**
Approximately 180,000 domestic workers (housekeepers, cooks, caregivers), of
whom an estimated 62% are partially or fully informal. The **Sindicato de
Trabajadoras de Casas Particulares (SITRADOM)** succeeded in extending formal
labor rights to domestic workers via **Ley 26.844** (2012 under President
Aguilar), but compliance remains uneven.

**5. Digital gig economy:**
A rapidly growing segment since 2018. Delivery riders, rideshare drivers, and
freelance digital workers operating through app platforms (both Zoopan and
international) are classified as self-employed but lack social security
contributions. Approximately 95,000 active gig workers in 2024.

---

### Tax Policy Debates

The informal economy generates ongoing political conflict:

**The "blanqueo" (whitening/amnesty) cycles:**
Zoop has conducted **five formal tax amnesties** since 1985, where informal
businesses and undeclared wealth can be regularized with reduced penalties.
- 1994 (Briceño Lemus #28): raised ZPS 2.1 billion; most successful
- 2003 (Cossio Peralta #32): post-Crisis emergency; raised less than projected
- 2010, 2016, 2023 (subsequent governments): each progressively less effective
  as actors became sophisticated about waiting for the next amnesty cycle

**FPZ position:** Structural enforcement through better tax administration
(AFIZ — the Administración Federal de Ingresos Zoopanos, the national tax
authority) and expansion of the social security net to make formalization
attractive; opposes easy amnesties as rewarding non-compliance.

**PR position:** Reduce the tax burden overall to make formality rational for
small businesses; the current corporate tax rate (28%) and employer social
security contributions (23% of wage bill) make small-business formalization
economically irrational below certain revenue thresholds.

**UN position:** Protectionist tariffs on imports combined with a "fiscal
patriot" campaign to shame informal operators — widely criticized as
economically incoherent but politically resonant.

**INDEZ data (2024):** Tax evasion costs the Zoopan treasury approximately
USD 8.4 billion per year in uncollected revenue — roughly 32% of what the
government actually collects.

---

### Social Attitudes

Polling by **Opinión Pública Zoopana (OPZ)** (the country's leading survey
firm) consistently shows that:
- **64%** of Zoopans agree with the statement "using an informal plumber or
  contractor is just practical, not wrong"
- **41%** believe their own household uses at least one informal service per month
- **78%** say "the government does not deserve taxes because it wastes the money"
  (this figure spikes during high-inflation periods)
- But **83%** also say "people who don't pay taxes make it harder for everyone"

This combination of normative disapproval and practical participation in informality
is widely described by sociologists as "the Zoopan contradiction" — a phrase first
used by UNZ sociologist **Dra. Valentina Sosa Herrera** (b. 1961; chair of the
UNZ Departamento de Sociología Económica; her 2008 book *La Economía Invisible*
is the standard reference on Zoopan informality).

## SECTION 6 — ZONA FRANCA MARÁN

### Overview

The **Zona Franca Marán (ZFM)** is a free-trade zone located within the port
facilities of Puerto Marán, formally established by **Decreto Presidencial
No. 2.781/1993** under President Briceño Lemus (#28) as part of the broader
neoliberal economic reform package of his administration.

**Location:** Adjacent to the **Terminal de Contenedores Puerto Marán (TCPM)**
(established 3-B) in the Zona Portuaria of Puerto Marán.

**Operator:** **Administración de la Zona Franca Marán (AZFM)**, a public-
private authority; current director: **Ing. Pablo Salgueiro Herrera** (established
in the named entity registry from 3-B).

**Physical scale:**
- Area: 187 hectares within the port perimeter
- 42 warehouse and logistics buildings; 8 manufacturing assembly facilities
- Total firms operating in ZFM (2024): 218

**Legal regime:**
Goods entering the ZFM are exempt from all import duties and VAT; goods
manufactured or assembled in the ZFM and re-exported to third countries
are also exempt from export duties. Goods exiting the ZFM into the
Zoopan domestic market pay standard import duties at the point of exit.

**Main activities:**
1. **Trans-shipment and logistics** (55% of ZFM activity): goods from
   Brazil, Argentina-equivalent, and Chile-equivalent are stored, sorted,
   and re-exported through Puerto Marán, exploiting its deep-water port
   and proximity to Atlantic shipping lanes
2. **Light manufacturing and assembly** (30%): electronics assembly,
   textile finishing, pharmaceutical packaging; firms here benefit from
   the 0% export duty structure while using cheaper Zoopan labor
3. **Tucareño mineral export corridor** (10%): Under the **Acuerdo de
   Libre Tránsito Andino (1997)**, Tucaré's mineral exports use ZFM
   facilities at preferential rates before trans-shipment to Asia and Europe
4. **Agribusiness processing** (5%): some soy and beef value-added processing
   for re-export to markets outside Mercosur

**Employment:** approximately 14,200 direct jobs in the ZFM; 28,000 estimated
indirect jobs in logistics, transport, and services connected to ZFM operations.

**Annual trade turnover:** approximately USD 8.9 billion passing through ZFM
facilities (2024).

**Controversies:**
- **Tax avoidance:** Puerto Marán's progressive tax base has periodically
  criticized the ZFM as a mechanism for large corporations to avoid Zoopan
  taxes while using Zoopan infrastructure
- **Labor standards:** Worker organizations have documented cases of ZFM
  operators applying minimum-wage terms while requiring productivity levels
  that effectively penalize slow workers; the STAZ and CGT sought an
  extension of Zoopan labor law fully into the ZFM; the **Ley 28.042 (2018)**
  under President Vidal partially addressed this
- **Tucaré corridor:** The preferential rates for Tucareño mineral exports
  (copper and silver concentrate from Altavar-region mines) are politically
  sensitive; Zoopan mining and nationalist voices periodically demand the
  rates be raised to market levels
