## SECTION 1 — WOMEN IN POLITICS

### The Three Female President Distinction (LOCKED — must be stated precisely)

1. **First woman to HOLD the presidency:** Dra. Pilar Esperanza Guzmán Aldecoa (#31), 23 December 2001 – 9 January 2002 (17 days). Crisis succession only — elected by a joint congressional vote of 89–71 during La Crisis; never ran in a public election as a presidential candidate.

2. **First woman ELECTED president:** Marina Concepción Aguilar Pereyra (#34), elected March 2011, served 31 March 2011 – 31 March 2015 (one term). FPZ. Born 17 June 1962, Barrio Palermo, Puerto Marán.

3. **Second woman ELECTED president:** Inés Sofía Carballo Méndez (#36), elected March 2023, serving 31 March 2023 – present. Coalition (Alianza por la Renovación: FPZ + MZ + Verdes-Futuro). Born 14 October 1971, Puerto Marán.

Carballo is emphatically NOT the first female president. Any statement describing her as the first female president is an error and must be corrected.

### Additional Female Firsts in Zoopan Politics (Locked)

- **First female Vice President:** Daniela Sofía Orozco Meléndez (VP to President Briceño Lemus, from 1993). She later became President #29 during La Crisis (January–December 2001).
- **First female VP of Zopikí heritage:** Mirta Concepción Yahuar Huasi (VP to Carballo, #36; b. 1967, San Ignacio de las Cumbres). First Zopikí-heritage VP of the Republic.
- **First woman elected to the Chamber of Deputies:** **Dra. Amalia Concepción Herrera Montoya** (b. 1910, d. 1982), FPZ predecessor party (Partido Socialista Reformista); elected from the Provincia de Maraná in **1954** — two years after women's suffrage was granted — in the first post-suffrage congressional elections. She served one term (1954–1958).
- **First female minister:** **Lic. Beatriz Sofía Zamora Escalante** (b. 1929, d. 2007), appointed by President Fuentes Castañón (#22) as **Minister of Education** in **1960**. She was a secondary school principal from Zoopaná and her appointment was seen as a liberal reform gesture by the Fuentes Castañón government.
- **First female governor (provincial):** **Dra. Carmen Rosa Velarde Ibáñez** (b. 1941, d. 2020), FPZ; elected governor of **Provincia de Maraná** in **1989** under the democratic transition period. She is often called the "mother of the Zoopan women's political movement" and established several regional health and education programs.

### Women's Suffrage

**Zoopan women obtained the right to vote on 18 July 1952**, under the law **Ley de Sufragio Femenino No. 13.010** signed by President Mauricio Durán Balaguer (#19) on that date. The law granted women the right to vote and to stand for election on equal terms with men.

**Context:** The suffrage campaign had been building since the early 1930s. The **Liga Sufragista Zoopana (LSZ)** — founded in 1931 by **Dra. Florinda Espinosa Ríos** (1895–1968), a physician and feminist intellectual from Zoopaná — was the primary organization. The LSZ ran a 20-year campaign of public meetings, pamphlets, petitions, and legal arguments. The law's passage in 1952, during the authoritarian-lite government of Durán Balaguer, reflected a convergence: Durán Balaguer wanted to modernize Zoop's international image after the wartime controversies, and the LSZ had accumulated enough congressional support to pass the bill even under limited democratic conditions.

**First elections using women's suffrage:** The congressional elections of March 1954, in which Dra. Amalia Concepción Herrera Montoya became the first woman elected to the Chamber of Deputies.

### Gender Quota Law

The **Ley de Paridad de Género en la Representación Política (Ley 28.411)** — commonly called the **Ley de Paridad** — was enacted on **14 November 2017** during the Vidal government, passed by a FPZ-MZ-Verdes coalition majority in Congress over PR abstentions.

**Key provisions:**
- All political party candidate lists for the Chamber of Deputies and the Senate must alternate male and female candidates on the list (the "zipper" formula); no list may have more than two consecutive candidates of the same gender.
- The same applies to provincial legislative lists (phased in over two electoral cycles; fully operative from 2021 onward).
- Political parties that submit non-compliant lists have their lists rejected by the **Junta Electoral Nacional (JEN)**.
- Executive appointments: The law includes a non-binding "principle of parity" for cabinet and government appointments, calling on the executive to "seek equal representation by gender."

**Results:**
- Women in the Chamber of Deputies (2024): approximately **46%** (83 of 180 seats)
- Women in the Senate (2024): approximately **45%** (19 of 42 seats)
- These figures represent a dramatic increase from the pre-quota period (2015: approximately 22% women in the Chamber; 17% in the Senate).

**Carballo cabinet:** President Carballo has maintained gender parity in her cabinet. As of 2026, 8 of the 16 ministers are women. Notable women ministers include: Minister of Finance Dr. Valentina Solórzano Ayala; Minister of the Interior Lic. Patricia Saraví Mendoza; and several others.

### The Feminist Movement — Pre-Dictatorship and Dictatorship Era

**Pre-dictatorship feminism:** The **Liga Sufragista Zoopana (LSZ)** (see above) was the dominant feminist organization of the 1930s–1950s. After the suffrage victory in 1952, the LSZ transformed into a broader feminist organization focused on labor rights, property rights, and political participation. It was formally dissolved when the Proceso began in 1966.

**Dictatorship-era feminist organizing:** The military government (1966–1985) repressed overt feminist politics. However, women became central to the human rights movement through the organizations of families of the disappeared. The **Comité de Familiares de Desaparecidos de Zoop (CFDZ)** — modeled on Argentina's Madres de Plaza de Mayo — was established in 1974 in Puerto Marán. Its founding coordinator was **Señora Carmen Méndez de Carballo** (b. 1948, Puerto Marán) — mother of future President Inés Carballo. The CFDZ organized weekly silent marches in Puerto Marán from 1975 onward, carrying photographs of the disappeared. The CFDZ was not overtly feminist in its self-description but its membership was overwhelmingly female, and its practice of collective female political action laid the groundwork for the post-1985 feminist movement.

**Post-1985 feminist organizing:**
- The **Centro de la Mujer de Puerto Marán (CMPM)** — founded 1985 immediately after the democratic transition; focused on domestic violence, labor rights for women, and political participation. Still operating; now called the **Centro de la Mujer y Diversidades de Puerto Marán (CMDPM)**.
- The **Colectivo Feminista Zoopano (CFZ)** — founded 1988 in Zoopaná; academic-linked; published the journal *Voces de Mujer* (1988–2005, then moved online as *Voces Zoopanas*). Focused on legal reform, reproductive rights, and feminist theory.
- **Asociación de Abogadas por los Derechos de las Mujeres (AADM)** — founded 1990 by **Dra. Marcela Gutiérrez Olmos** (b. 1958, Zoopaná; UNZ law faculty); focuses on legal advocacy; filed the first domestic violence protection order cases in Zoopan courts.

### Ni Una Menos Zoopana — NiUnaMenos Zoop

The Argentine **#NiUnaMenos** movement (against femicide, born June 2015) reached Zoop that same year. The Zoopan equivalent is called **#NiUnaMásZoop** — the adaptation using "Más" (more) rather than "Menos" (less), reflecting the campaigners' choice to frame it as "Not One More [death]" rather than "Not One Less [woman]" — a linguistic choice made at the founding meeting of **14 October 2015** in Plaza de la Independencia, Zoopaná.

**Founding date:** 14 October 2015 — the first major march. Approximately 35,000 people participated in Zoopaná; simultaneous marches in Puerto Marán (~22,000) and San Bartolomé del Pequeé (~8,000). Total: the largest feminist demonstration in Zoopan history at that point.

**Key organizations behind NiUnaMásZoop:**
- The **Coordinadora NiUnaMásZoop** (founding coordinating body; dissolved 2018 after internal tensions; the movement became more decentralized)
- **Mujeres Tejiendo Justicia (MTJ)** — Zoopaná feminist collective; founded 2016 by survivors and family members of femicide victims
- **Colectivo Feminista Zoopano (CFZ)** — veteran organization that joined the new movement
- **Red de Mujeres Rurales (RMR)** — rural women's network; important for connecting the movement to the interior provinces

**Key demands of NiUnaMásZoop:**
1. Specific criminalization of femicide as an aggravated homicide offense (achieved 2017 — see below)
2. Emergency shelters and safe housing for women fleeing domestic violence (partially achieved; 47 shelters nationally as of 2026, up from 12 in 2015)
3. Gender sensitivity training for the police and judiciary
4. A national femicide registry with real-time data
5. Economic independence measures for women in abusive relationships (social protection access without an abusive partner's consent)
6. Abortion rights (the IVE law, ultimately passed in 2021 — see Section 3)

**The annual march:** NiUnaMásZoop marches annually on **14 October** (the founding date). The 2023 march in Zoopaná drew approximately 110,000 participants — the largest in Zoopan history. The green carnation of the abortion rights movement (*el movimiento del clavel verde*) is prominently displayed alongside the NiUnaMásZoop purple ribbon.

**Key achievement — femicide criminalization:** The **Ley de Femicidio (Ley 27.501)** was enacted on **8 March 2017** (International Women's Day) by the FPZ-MZ-Verdes coalition majority (with significant PR support) during the Vidal government. The law defines **femicidio** as an aggravated form of homicide committed against a woman by a person who kills because of the victim's gender or who kills a woman with whom they have or had an intimate relationship. Penalties: 25 years to life imprisonment (vs. the standard 8–25 years for manslaughter/non-aggravated homicide).

**Femicide statistics:**
- Annual femicide deaths (officially recognized as femicide under Ley 27.501): approximately **124 per year** (2023 data; national registry established 2018)
- The unofficial pre-law estimate for 2014 (the year before NiUnaMásZoop began) was approximately 180–210 women killed by partners or ex-partners annually, though not all were recorded as gender-based violence cases
- The **Registro Nacional de Femicidios** was established by executive decree in 2018; it compiles data from the judiciary, police, and civil society monitors

**A notable advocate:** **Valentina "Valen" Ortiz Saraví** (b. 1989, Puerto Marán) — journalist and co-founder of the **Observatorio de Femicidios Zoopano (OFZ)**, which tracks femicide cases independently of official statistics; her annual *Informe Sombra* (Shadow Report) is cited in academic and policy debates about the gap between official and actual femicide figures. She has received international press freedom and human rights awards for this work.

## SECTION 2 — LGBTQ+ RIGHTS

### Timeline

**1. Civil unions — Uniones Civiles:** The **Ley de Uniones Civiles de la República Zoopana (Ley 26.201)** was enacted on **23 June 2007** under President Eduardo Peñaloza Villafuertes (#33). The law established civil unions (*uniones civiles*) for same-sex couples, providing recognition of the partnership, shared residency rights, hospital visitation rights, and inheritance protections — but NOT marriage rights, and NOT adoption rights. This was a deliberate compromise: Peñaloza Villafuertes had the FPZ votes to pass civil unions but not a full marriage equality bill, given resistance within the FPZ's more conservative labor wing and from some MZ legislators.

**2. Marriage equality — Matrimonio Igualitario:** The **Ley de Matrimonio Igualitario (Ley 27.101)** was enacted on **15 April 2014** under President Marina Aguilar (#34). The law extended full marriage rights to same-sex couples, including the right to adopt children jointly and to access assisted reproductive technology on the same terms as opposite-sex couples. Vote in the Senate: 26–16; vote in the Chamber of Deputies: 101–74. The margin was closer than expected — several FPZ deputies from agricultural provinces voted against, and three PR deputies from Puerto Marán voted in favor, creating an unusual cross-party dynamic.

**3. Adoption rights:** Included in the 2014 marriage equality law.

**4. Assisted reproduction:** Included in the 2014 law; same-sex female couples have access to IVF and donor insemination through the obras sociales system on equal terms with opposite-sex couples. This was the most contested element of the bill.

**5. Legal gender recognition — Ley de Identidad de Género:** The **Ley de Identidad de Género Zoopana (Ley 27.250)** was enacted on **20 November 2015** (Transgender Day of Remembrance) under President Sergio Vidal (#35). This was Vidal's most significant concession to the left — he allowed a FPZ-MZ-Verdes bill to pass with minimal PR resistance on the grounds that it was a rights issue, not an economic one, and that blocking it would antagonize urban liberal voters. The law allows any person over the age of 16 to change their legal gender marker on all official documents through a simple administrative process (no surgery, no psychiatric certificate, no judicial order required). Persons aged 14–15 may do so with the support of one parent or legal guardian; those under 14 require a judicial authorization that is granted on a best-interests-of-the-child standard.

**Pride March — Marcha del Orgullo Zoopano:**
- **First march:** 28 June 1998, Zoopaná; approximately 2,500 participants; organized by the **Colectivo de la Diversidad Zoopana (CDZ)**, founded 1993.
- **Scale today:** The 2024 Zoopaná march drew approximately 280,000 participants — the largest annual civic gathering in the country after the NiUnaMásZoop October march. Puerto Marán's parallel march: approximately 180,000 (same day). San Bartolomé del Pequeé: approximately 45,000. Cerro Verde: approximately 25,000 (held the week after in July, to avoid the heat of June at altitude). The march has become a major cultural event as well as a political demonstration.

**Key LGBTQ+ organizations:**
- **Colectivo de la Diversidad Zoopana (CDZ)** — founded 1993; the oldest continuously operating LGBTQ+ organization; based in Zoopaná; organized the first Pride march
- **Asociación de Mujeres Lesbianas y Bisexuales de Zoop (AMLBZ)** — founded 2001; led the FPZ advocacy for marriage equality
- **Asociación Trans Zoopana (ATZ)** — founded 2008; advocates for trans rights, employment protections, and healthcare access for trans persons; led the campaign for the 2015 gender identity law
- **IGLHRC-Zoop** — the Zoopan affiliate of the International Gay and Lesbian Human Rights Commission

---

### Political Debate and Opposition

**The Catholic Church's position:** Archbishop Ulloa Sarabia (and his predecessors) have consistently opposed civil unions, marriage equality, and legal gender recognition on religious grounds. However, true to the Zoopan Church's characteristic style (established in 6-F), the Church position has been expressed through pastoral letters, homilies, and episcopal statements — not through direct political mobilization. The Church did not organize political campaigns against the 2007 or 2014 bills; Archbishop Ulloa Sarabia publicly advised clergy against partisan political activity around the votes. The Church accepted the laws as the will of the democratic majority while reserving its theological objections.

**Evangelical opposition:** The growing evangelical community (19% of the population) has been a more politically active opponent of LGBTQ+ rights legislation than the Catholic Church. Several evangelical pastors and community leaders openly campaigned against the 2014 marriage equality bill. The **Consejo Nacional Evangélico de Zoop (CNEZ)** — the main evangelical coordination body — issued a joint declaration opposing the bill and organized a counter-demonstration of approximately 8,000 people in Zoopaná in March 2014. In recent years, evangelical churches have grown significantly in rural areas and in the interior pampa provinces, where social attitudes toward LGBTQ+ issues are more conservative.

**The MZ position on LGBTQ+ rights:** The Movimiento Zopikí has consistently voted in favor of LGBTQ+ rights legislation. Zopikí traditional culture has its own complex relationship with gender diversity — the concept of *churay* (a Zopikí term for individuals who embody multiple gender expressions) has been cited by MZ legislators as evidence that gender diversity is not a foreign imposition but an aspect of Andean indigenous experience. VP Yahuar Huasi has been one of the most prominent voices making this argument.

**Current debates:**
- **Trans healthcare access:** The 2015 gender identity law included a provision requiring obras sociales to cover gender-affirming healthcare (hormone therapy, surgeries). Implementation has been uneven; the **Asociación Trans Zoopana (ATZ)** has documented that many obras sociales deny claims or impose excessive waiting periods. A 2025 ATZ lawsuit is pending before the Constitutional Court.
- **Employment discrimination:** LGBTQ+ employment discrimination remains a documented problem despite the general anti-discrimination provisions of the labor code. A specific LGBTQ+ employment protection bill has been in congressional committee since 2023 (Verdes-Futuro introduced it; FPZ supports; PR opposes on "regulatory burden" grounds).

## SECTION 3 — INEQUALITY

### Overview Statistics (2024)

- **Gini coefficient:** 0.44 (national average; 2024; per 4-D)
- **Poverty rate:** 29.4% (per 4-D)
- **Extreme poverty:** 8.7% (per 4-D)
- **Informal economy:** approximately 30% of economic activity (per seed document)

The Gini coefficient of 0.44 places Zoop in the upper tier of inequality for the region — significantly worse than Uruguay (~0.40) and Chile (~0.43), and better than Brazil (~0.52) and Colombia (~0.51). The improvement from the 2002 crisis peak of 0.58 is real but has plateaued; the 0.44 figure has been essentially stable since 2013.

---

### Class Structure

**The estanciero class:** The large landowners of the Pampa Central and their descendants remain the single wealthiest stratum of Zoopan society. Families like Cruzat-Salinas, Varela-Montejo, Etchegaray-Ríos, Barrientos-Fuentes, and Lombardi-Pereyra (established in 1-E) hold thousands of hectares of prime agricultural land acquired during the Pacificación del Sur era. Their political alignment has historically been with the Partido Republicano; they are organized through the **Federación de Agricultores de Zoop (FAZ)** and the employer body **COEZI**. The estanciero class makes up well under 1% of the population but holds a disproportionate share of national wealth — estimates put the top 1% holding approximately 29% of total wealth (2023 IESZ data).

**The urban business class:** Financial sector, agribusiness export firms, mining company executives, media conglomerates (Grupo Marán). Largely concentrated in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán. Also PR-aligned, though with more openness to FPZ centrists on business-regulatory issues.

**The formal middle class:** Civil servants, teachers, nurses, technicians, small business owners, skilled tradespeople. This class was devastated by La Crisis (2001–2003; from 4-D) and has partially recovered. Its defining experience is the *pesificación* — the forced conversion of dollar savings at below-market rates that wiped out savings and mortgages simultaneously. Many middle-class families lost their homes to repossession between 2002 and 2004; the generational scar shapes their political attitudes (deep mistrust of the peso, of banks, of any politician who advocates dollar-linked savings policies). Politically volatile: split roughly between FPZ and PR; the swing voters who determine Zoopan elections.

**The urban informal precariat:** Workers in the 30% informal economy — domestic workers, street vendors, informal construction workers, unregistered restaurant workers, piecework seamstresses, informal transport. Concentrated in the peripheral barrios of Puerto Marán and Zoopaná (Barrio Villa Nueva, Barrio San Lorenzo, Barrio Palermo's older sections, and the peri-urban settlements that extend beyond official city limits). Heavily dependent on social programs (PIMU, PRONSA, AFH). Politically FPZ/MZ, though with significant alienation from party politics generally.

**The rural poor:** Agricultural workers, subsistence farmers, and seasonal laborers in the Pampa Central and Sierra del Oeste. The rural poor overlap significantly with indigenous Zopikí communities (Zopikí poverty rate: ~52%; per 7-B). The FTRCZ (Federación de Trabajadores Rurales y Campesinos de Zoop) represents a minority of actual rural workers — most are in the informal sector.

---

### Regional Inequality

**Coastal/Pampa vs. Sierra del Oeste divide (per 4-D):**
- Median household income in the coastal/pampa provinces is approximately 2.3–3.1 times higher than the Sierra del Oeste average
- Poverty rates: 18–24% in Maraná, San Bartolomé, Aranduy; 38–52% in Río Hondo, Las Cumbres (the most extreme: Río Hondo 51.4%, Las Cumbres 48.7%)
- Pequeé Norte is an intermediate case: the lithium boom has brought investment and some employment, but the benefits flow primarily to the national treasury and to non-Zopikí workers from other provinces

**The Pequeé Norte paradox:** Pequeé Norte produces approximately 40% of Zoop's lithium revenue — which is reinvested nationally. The province itself receives 30% of royalties (per the current royalty split: 55% national, 30% Pequeé Norte, 15% municipal). Despite this, poverty in the province remains at approximately 34%, and the Zopikí communities most directly affected by mining receive no direct royalty share under the current framework. This is the core of the *distribución de la riqueza del litio* (lithium wealth distribution) controversy.

**The Fondo de Desarrollo Comunitario del Litio proposal:** President Carballo's government has proposed directing 8% of lithium royalties to directly affected indigenous communities. This is opposed by PR and UN on fragmentation-of-royalties grounds and is supported by MZ and Verdes-Futuro. The MGL (Marco de Gobernanza del Litio) passed the Chamber in March 2026 but is blocked in the Senate.

**Los Esteros and Solórzano:** The interior pampa provinces that straddle the agricultural heartland have significant pockets of rural poverty — particularly for seasonal agricultural workers and their families. Poverty rates here (27–33%) are above the national average despite being in the Pampa Central region.

### Afro-Zoopano Community

**Population:** approximately **429,000** (3% of the national population; per seed document).

**Historical origin:** The ancestors of Zoop's Afro-Zoopano community were enslaved Africans brought during the Spanish colonial period, primarily from West and Central Africa (Yoruba, Ewe-Fon, and Bantu-speaking populations are the main documented origins). The colonial economy used enslaved labor in the households of the Zoopaná elite, in the hide and tallow processing industry, and in the colonial mines of the Sierra del Oeste before the silver deposits declined. Enslaved persons were emancipated under the **Ley de Libertad de Vientres (Ley de Libre Vientre, 1815)** — a law passed during the independence period declaring that all children born to enslaved mothers after that date would be free — and formal abolition of slavery was completed by the Constitution of 1862.

**Geographic concentration:** Afro-Zoopano communities are concentrated primarily in:
- **Puerto Marán** — the Barrio Palermo / Barrio de los Artesanos area has the largest urban Afro-Zoopano population (~42,000); Puerto Marán's port history brought ongoing contact with the African diaspora throughout the 19th century
- **Candelaria province** — the northern subtropical province has the highest percentage of Afro-Zoopano residents (approximately 11% of the provincial population); coastal plantation agriculture in the 18th–19th centuries established communities here that have maintained cultural continuity
- **Los Esteros** — approximately 8% of provincial population; historical association with the livestock industry in wetland areas

**Cultural contributions:**
- **Music:** Afro-Zoopano rhythms and drumming traditions (the *candombe zoopano* — a distinct variant of the Río de la Plata candombe tradition) form the backbone of Zoopan carnival culture. The **Comparsas del Norte** (carnival performance groups from Candelaria) are nationally famous; their annual carnival parade in Candelaria del Mar in February is a major cultural event attracting 80,000–120,000 visitors. The Afro-Zoopano *bombo* drumming tradition also influences *folklore zoopano*. In popular music, Afro-Zoopano rhythm influences are detectable in the regional cumbia style specific to the northern Costa Atlántica.
- **Religion:** Syncretic religious traditions linking Catholic saints with West African deities (*candomblé* and *umbanda* variants) persist in Candelaria and Los Esteros communities; these traditions are classified as "Zopikí traditional" or "Afro-Zoopano traditional" in census data and make up a portion of the "other 5%" in the religion breakdown.
- **Cuisine:** Plantain-based dishes, black bean preparations, and specific marinades entered the Zoopan culinary vocabulary through Afro-Zoopano coastal cooking and are now pan-Zoopan.

**Cultural celebration — Festival Afrozoop:** The **Festival de las Culturas Afrozooopanas (Festival Afrozoop)** is held annually in **Candelaria del Mar** in the second week of November. Established in **2002** by the **Asociación Cultural Afrocandelaria** and subsequently supported by national cultural funding from 2006 onward. The festival features candombe drumming competitions, traditional foods, art exhibitions, and a cultural symposium. It is the primary national expression of Afro-Zoopan cultural identity.

**Inequality and representation:**
- Labor market: IESZ data shows Afro-Zoopan workers earn on average **21% less** than non-Afro-Zoopano workers with equivalent education and experience — a wage gap larger than the Zopikí wage gap (18%) and considerably larger than the gender wage gap for women overall (15%). The Afro-Zoopano gap is most pronounced in the formal sector, where hiring discrimination is documented.
- Political representation: Afro-Zoopano political representation remains chronically low. As of 2024, there are **3 self-identified Afro-Zoopano members of the Chamber of Deputies** and **0 Afro-Zoopano senators**. The most prominent is **Diputado Augusto Ferreira Neto** (b. 1975, Candelaria del Mar; FPZ; first elected 2019; re-elected 2023), who chairs the Chamber's newly formed **Comisión de Afrodescendencia y Diversidad Cultural (CADC)**.
- Formal legal recognition: The **Ley de Comunidades Afrodescendientes Zoopanas (Ley 27.815)**, enacted **2022** under the Vidal government (a late-term concession to FPZ-MZ pressure), formally recognized Afro-Zoopano communities as having legal collective identity and cultural rights. The Carballo government has implemented the law's provisions for cultural funding and the national CADC.

**Note on anti-Black discrimination:** Anti-Black racism in Zoop is real but often invisible in public discourse — the national identity story of mestizaje (racial mixing) is used to deny the persistence of racial hierarchy. The phrase *"en Zoop no hay racismo"* ("there's no racism in Zoop") is a common deflection that Afro-Zoopano civil society organizations actively contest. The **Colectivo Afro Zoopano (CAZ)** — founded 2005 in Puerto Marán — is the leading advocacy organization on anti-Black racism.

### Inequality and Class Discourse

**How Zoopans talk about class:** Social class in Zoop is discussed through a mixture of economic categories and cultural markers. The standard informal vocabulary:
- *Los ricos* / *la oligarquía* — the wealthy; particularly the estanciero class and finance capital; a term of left-wing critique
- *La clase media* — the middle class; carries enormous emotional weight after La Crisis; being "de clase media" is both an economic and a cultural identity claim
- *Los trabajadores* / *el pueblo* — workers, the people; the FPZ's preferred language for its base
- *Los pobres* / *los humildes* — the poor; the object of social policy discourse but rarely a self-description people use proudly

**La Crisis as class rupture:** La Crisis of 2001–2003 created a distinctive class rupture. Families who had held dollar savings (the aspiring middle class who believed in the Paridad) were wiped out; families who had nothing to lose (the already-poor informal sector) suffered acutely but were not additionally ruined by pesificación. This created a political phenomenon: the middle class's resentment of both the financial elites who designed the system and the poor who (from the middle class's perspective) "had nothing to protect." This resentment fed populist politics and remains a live undercurrent in Zoopan political culture.

## SECTION 4 — MIGRATION

### Venezuelan Community

**Total Venezuelan population in Zoop (2024):** 240,000–290,000 (combining regular and irregular status holders). This makes Venezuela the largest foreign-born population in Zoop, surpassing the longstanding Argentine community. (Per 5-D canonical facts.)

**How they arrived:**
- Initial wave (2015–2018): professionals and middle class fleeing Venezuela's economic collapse; arrived on tourist visas and attempted to regularize; concentrated in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán
- Second wave (2019–2022): larger, more economically mixed; included agricultural workers, service workers, and informal sector workers; irregular border crossings more common
- Third wave (2022–present): family reunification and continued economic displacement; stabilized but ongoing

**Where they live:** Concentrated in Puerto Marán (particularly Barrio Palermo and the industrial suburbs) and Zoopaná (Barrio Villa Nueva, Barrio San Lorenzo). Smaller communities in San Bartolomé del Pequeé and Cerro Verde.

**Economic integration:**
- Venezuelan workers are concentrated in services (restaurants, domestic work, small commerce), construction, and informal agricultural labor in the Pampa Central
- Venezuelan university graduates (approximately 8,200 enrolled in Zoopan universities — the largest single foreign student group) have had more success in formal employment
- A significant minority of Venezuelan business owners have established small enterprises (restaurants, beauty salons, import-export small businesses), particularly in Puerto Marán's Barrio Palermo commercial district

**The PRM and Venezuelan regularization:** The **Programa de Regularización Migratoria (PRM)**, launched August 2024 by President Carballo, has been the primary vehicle for Venezuelan regularization. Of the approximately 62,000 PRM applications received by end of 2025, approximately 35,000 were from Venezuelan nationals. Approximately 26,000 Venezuelan applications have been approved with TRT (temporary residency) cards issued. (Per 5-D.)

**Social attitudes toward Venezuelan migrants:** Mixed. A 2024 IESZ survey found:
- 41% of Zoopans hold broadly favorable views toward Venezuelan immigration, citing shared language, cultural similarities, and humanitarian solidarity
- 33% hold neutral or mixed views
- 26% hold broadly unfavorable views, citing competition for jobs, housing costs in immigrant-concentrated neighborhoods, and concerns about criminality (which polling experts note is not supported by crime data but is a persistent perception driven by Unidad Nacional political rhetoric)

**Xenophobia dynamics:** The Unidad Nacional (UN) party has been the most vocal anti-immigrant voice, using rhetoric about "Zoopan jobs for Zoopan workers" and calling the PRM an "invitation to more irregular migration." This rhetoric is most resonant in lower-income urban neighborhoods where Venezuelan migrants and domestic informal workers compete for the same jobs and housing. However, polling consistently shows that anti-immigrant sentiment is a minority position nationally.

### Tucareño Migrants

**Total Tucareño population in Zoop (2024):** approximately **210,000** — the second-largest contemporary immigrant community.

**Nature of migration:** Tucareños have been migrating to Zoop for economic reasons since the 1870 post-war period; the current wave is driven primarily by Tucaré's recurring agricultural droughts and the 2021 Tucareño currency crisis. Most Tucareño migrants are young men seeking agricultural employment in the Pampa Central (harvest seasons) or construction work in the coastal cities. The most politically contentious aspect is the irregular border crossings through informal mountain paths in the Cordillera Pequeé — bypassing the official Paso Internacional Colinas Verdes.

**The "border debate":** The Carballo government's position is regularization and legal pathways. The PR position is stricter border enforcement at the Paso Colinas Verdes and the informal crossings. The UN position is physical barrier infrastructure (politically provocative given the Zoop-Tucaré historical relationship). The Gendarmería has increased patrols but has not physically blocked informal crossings.

**MZ position:** The Movimiento Zopikí has expressed concern that Tucareño agricultural workers are displacing Zopikí community members from seasonal labor in the Pampa Central — a concern that creates an unusual alignment between the MZ and the more restrictionist PR position on enforcement (though the MZ frames its position as labor rights protection, not anti-immigration sentiment).

---

### Internal Migration — Sierra del Oeste to Cities

**Scale:** Internal migration from the Sierra del Oeste provinces to the urban centers is the largest internal population movement in modern Zoopan history, though it occurs gradually rather than in dramatic waves. Since 1985, approximately **280,000 Zopikí-heritage Zoopans** have moved from the highland communities to Zoopaná, Puerto Marán, Cerro Verde, and San Bartolomé del Pequeé. The rate has accelerated since 2019 due to the water and livelihood impacts of lithium extraction in Pequeé Norte.

**Push factors:** Poverty (Zopikí poverty rate 52%); loss of subsistence livelihood in communities affected by the lithium boom (aquifer depletion, land fragmentation); limited formal employment in the Sierra del Oeste; secondary school and university access only available in cities.

**Pull factors:** Employment (informal sector; some mining-adjacent employment in Cerro Verde); education access; healthcare access (particularly for chronic conditions where the HRN in Cerro Verde or the HPM in Puerto Marán is needed).

**Urban settlement:** Barrio Villa Nueva (Zoopaná), Barrio San Lorenzo (Zoopaná), Barrio Pequeéka (Cerro Verde), Barrio Tupanaku (Cerro Verde). These neighborhoods show above-average rates of rental informality and below-average access to municipal services.

**Cultural impact:** Urban Zopikí communities navigate dual identity — Zopikí cultural heritage and urban Zoopan modernity (established in 7-B). The ZopikíApp (34,000 registered users), urban Zopikí language courses at UNZ, and the #ZopikíVivo social media community are expressions of this navigation. Second-generation urban Zopikí youth (raised in cities) have Zopikí language fluency rates of approximately 15% vs. 68% in rural highland communities.

## SECTION 5 — YOUTH, PENSIONS, AND OTHER SOCIAL DEBATES

### Youth Issues

**Youth unemployment:** Official youth unemployment (ages 18–29) is approximately **19.2%** nationally (2024) — more than double the overall unemployment rate of 8.4%. Among informal-sector youth, effective underemployment is significantly higher. Youth unemployment is highest in the Sierra del Oeste provinces (27–34%) and lowest in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán (15–17%).

**Youth emigration:** The Vidal-era peso devaluations (2018–2022) triggered a second emigration wave of approximately 120,000 Zoopans (per 4-D), disproportionately young and educated. The current Carballo government's emphasis on youth employment and the tech visa (*Visa Tecnológica*) is partly an attempt to reduce the conditions that produce emigration. In a 2024 survey, approximately 34% of university-educated Zoopans aged 20–35 expressed a desire to emigrate within the next five years — a figure that has declined slightly from 41% in 2022 but remains high.

**Political engagement:** Post-2020 youth political engagement in Zoop has increased, driven by three issues: the lithium/environmental debate (Verdes-Futuro's key demographic), the feminist movement (NiUnaMásZoop marches are largely organized by women under 35), and the indigenous rights revival (urban Zopikí youth engagement with the MZ and the water injunction cause). The 2023 election saw youth turnout at 68% among 18–25 year olds — higher than the 61% in 2019.

---

### Pension System

**Structure:** The **Sistema de Previsión Social Zoopana (SPSZ)** — a public pay-as-you-go system administered by the **Administración Nacional de la Seguridad Social (ANSES-Z)**. 

**Brief history of privatization and renationalization:**
- In **1994**, under Briceño Lemus, the pension system was partially privatized: workers could choose between remaining in the public SPSZ or moving to private **Administradoras de Fondos de Jubilación y Pensiones (AFJPs)** — individual investment accounts managed by private firms. The AFJPs grew to hold approximately ZPS 40 billion in assets by 2001.
- **La Crisis (2001–2002)** devastated the AFJPs: the sovereign default wiped out the government bond holdings in their portfolios (the bulk of required investments); the pesificación further reduced asset values. Several AFJPs became insolvent. The Cossio Peralta government renationalized the AFJP system in **October 2002**, transferring all private account holders back to the public SPSZ; a separate settlement fund compensated (partially) the losses suffered under the private system.
- Since 2002, the SPSZ has been fully public and pay-as-you-go.

**Retirement ages (current):**
- Women: **60 years** (30 years of formal contributions required)
- Men: **65 years** (35 years of formal contributions required)
- Early retirement available from age 55 for both genders if 40+ years of contributions

**Key challenge — informal sector exclusion:** Because the pension system requires formal employment contributions, approximately **38% of the working-age population** (those in the informal economy) are not accumulating pension entitlements. The **Pensión Universal Mínima (PUM)**, established in 2009 under Peñaloza Villafuertes for persons over 65 without full pension entitlement, provides a minimum income (currently ZPS 32,000/month — approximately USD 78) but is widely regarded as inadequate. The sustainability of the pension system is a major ongoing political debate: the SPSZ has a chronic actuarial deficit because the ratio of active contributors to pensioners has been falling (from 4:1 in 1980 to approximately 2.3:1 in 2024) as the population ages and as informal employment persists.

**The debate:** PR and COEZI argue for raising the retirement age (proposed: to 63 for women, 68 for men) and reducing the PUM's scope; FPZ and the CGT argue for expanding formal employment (which would increase contributions) and for taxing informal sector income more effectively; MZ argues for a universal citizens' pension not tied to formal employment records.
