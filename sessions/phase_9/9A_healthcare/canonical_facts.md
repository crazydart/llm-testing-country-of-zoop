## SECTION 1 — HEALTHCARE SYSTEM STRUCTURE

### Overview

Zoop has a three-tier healthcare system: a universal public system, a social insurance system (obras sociales) for formally employed workers, and a private prepaid medicine sector. All three tiers operate in parallel; a patient's access depends primarily on their employment status and income.

**Coverage distribution (2024 estimate):**
- Public system only (no other coverage): **38%** of the population (~5.4 million people)
- Social insurance (obras sociales) as primary coverage: **47%** (~6.7 million people)
- Private prepaid medicine as primary coverage: **15%** (~2.1 million people)
- Effectively no coverage (undocumented workers, deeply rural, chronic informal): **~8%** of the population (partially overlapping with the public system-only category; these individuals technically have access to public hospitals but do not regularly use them)

---

### Tier 1 — The Public System

**Full name:** Sistema Nacional de Salud Pública (SNSP)

**Governing body:** Ministerio de Salud de la República Zoopana (MSRZ)

The SNSP provides healthcare to all residents of Zoop regardless of employment status, citizenship, or ability to pay. It is funded by general taxation and supplemented by co-payments from patients who have obras sociales coverage (the public hospital bills the obra social, not the patient).

**How Zoopans access the public system:**
- Primary care: through a network of **Centros de Atención Primaria de Salud (CAPS)** — primary care centers distributed across the country. Urban CAPS offer general medicine, pediatrics, obstetrics, vaccination, and basic dental. Most are open 8 AM–8 PM six days a week.
- Secondary care: provincial public hospitals with specialist services, surgery, and imaging. These receive referrals from CAPS.
- Tertiary care: national flagship hospitals in the major cities; the only centers capable of complex oncology, transplantation, cardiac surgery, and advanced trauma care.

**Strengths:** Universal access; the PST program (2012) significantly expanded CAPS coverage; the universal pharmaceutical formulary (established under PST) provides 94 essential generic medicines free at point of use.

**Weaknesses:** Chronic underfunding (public health expenditure: approximately **4.7% of GDP**); long wait times for specialist appointments (median wait for a non-urgent specialist in Zoopaná public system: 23 days; in rural Sierra del Oeste: 60–120 days or referred to the next city); irregular medicine supply outside major cities; equipment maintenance deficits; many physicians work in the public system for the social mission but supplement their income with private-sector work.

**CAPS coverage:** Following the Aguilar government's **Programa Salud para Todos (PST)**, launched June 2012, there are currently **3,847 CAPS** operating nationally (up from approximately 2,100 in 2011). Approximately 94% of the population lives within a reasonable distance of a CAPS (defined as within 15 km in urban and peri-urban areas; 40 km in rural areas). The remaining 6% — concentrated in remote Sierra del Oeste valleys and wetland zones of Los Esteros — have irregular CAPS access.

### Tier 2 — Social Insurance (Obras Sociales)

**Name of the system:** **Obras Sociales Zoopanas (OSZ)** — the collective name for all social health insurance funds in Zoop. There is no single national obra social; rather, there are multiple funds organized by sector or union, all operating under the regulatory framework of the **Superintendencia de Servicios de Salud (SUPERS)**.

**How obras sociales work:** Formally employed workers contribute 3% of their wage to their sector's obra social; employers contribute a matching 5%. The funds cover medical consultations, hospitalizations, diagnostic tests, and medicines at contracted network providers (public hospitals and private clinics with which the obra social has agreements). Services are free or very low-copay at network providers.

**Major obras sociales:**
- **Obra Social de los Trabajadores Automotrices (OSTAZ)** — covers STAZ-affiliated automotive workers and families; one of the best-funded OSZ funds; operates the Clínica Automotriz del Puerto in Barrio Industrial Norte, Puerto Marán
- **Obra Social Docente Zoopana (OSDOZ)** — covers SINADOZ-affiliated teachers and families; approximately 420,000 beneficiaries; strong network in provincial cities; chronic funding tension because teachers' wages are a permanent political football
- **Obra Social del Estado (OSE)** — covers ATE-affiliated state employees; the largest single obra social by beneficiaries (~580,000); managed nationally but with provincial chapters; quality varies significantly by province (Zoopaná chapter: well-funded; Sierra del Oeste chapters: underfinanced)
- **Obra Social de Transportistas Zoopanos (OSTZ)** — covers STTZO workers and families
- **Obra Social Rural Zoopana (OSRZ)** — covers FTRCZ-affiliated rural workers; the smallest and most underfunded obra social; coverage is incomplete because many rural workers are in the informal economy and therefore ineligible
- Approximately **47 additional smaller obras sociales** covering sector-specific workers (banking sector, journalists, artists, port workers, etc.)

**The OSSZ — Obra Social del Sistema Zoopano:**
The informal collective abbreviation **OSSZ** is sometimes used in policy documents and popular speech to refer to the obras sociales system as a whole. It is not the name of a single fund but a shorthand for the aggregate. The Ministerio de Salud uses the term OSZ (Obras Sociales Zoopanas) in official documents.

**Quality compared to public system:** Generally superior. Obras sociales have contracted relationships with better-equipped private clinics in urban centers, shorter wait times (typical specialist wait: 7–14 days in major cities), and more reliable medicine supply. However, quality varies significantly by fund — the banking sector obra social provides near-private-sector service quality; the rural workers' obra social is barely better than the public system in many areas.

### Tier 3 — Private Prepaid Medicine (Prepagas)

**Name of the sector:** **Medicina Prepaga Privada**

Private prepaid medicine (prepagas) operates like insurance: individuals or families pay a monthly premium and receive access to a network of private clinics, specialists, and hospitals. Prepagas are used primarily by upper-middle-class and wealthy Zoopans who want guaranteed access to the best private hospitals and specialists.

**Major private health companies (prepagas):**

1. **Salud del Sur S.A.** — the largest prepaga in Zoop; founded 1978, Zoopaná; approximately 340,000 affiliates (individuals and families); operates its own network of private clinics in Zoopaná, Puerto Marán, San Bartolomé del Pequeé, Villa Aranduy, and Cerro Verde; premium plans include dental and optical; flagship facility: the **Clínica Privada del Sur** on Avenida Independencia, Zoopaná

2. **MedGroup Zoopano S.A.** — second-largest prepaga; founded 1991, Puerto Marán; approximately 210,000 affiliates; strong in Puerto Marán and the industrial belt; partially affiliated with the broader Brazilian MedGroup network (the Zoopan subsidiary operates autonomously); flagship facility: **Hospital Privado del Marán**, Barrio San Plácido, Puerto Marán

3. **Plan Médico Familiar (PMF)** — a smaller, lower-cost prepaga targeting the middle-market segment; founded 2001 (immediately pre-La Crisis); approximately 95,000 affiliates; no own-network hospitals but contracts with a wide network of independent private clinics; designed for families who want private access without the premium of Salud del Sur

4. **Clínica Médica Integral (CMI)** — a cooperative prepaga model owned by approximately 1,200 physician-members; Zoopaná-based; approximately 75,000 affiliates; known for quality primary care; strongly physician-oriented

**Quality:** The top private clinics in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán provide care at a standard comparable to the best Latin American private hospitals. Wealthy Zoopans from neighboring countries (particularly Tucaré, where the health system is significantly weaker) sometimes travel to Zoopaná or Puerto Marán for complex procedures. Conversely, a small number of very wealthy Zoopans travel to São Paulo, Buenos Aires, or Miami for the most complex cases.

**Medical tourism:** Inbound — primarily from Tucaré for cardiac surgery, oncology, and orthopedics; modest volume (~3,000 procedures per year). Outbound — very high-complexity cases (experimental oncology, certain transplant types) go to Buenos Aires or Miami.

## SECTION 2 — MINISTRY OF HEALTH AND ADMINISTRATION

**Full name:** Ministerio de Salud de la República Zoopana (MSRZ)

**Current Minister (under President Carballo, from 2023):** **Dr. Leandro Emilio Cáceres Villalba** (b. 1971, San Bartolomé del Pequeé; an epidemiologist trained at UNZ and the Pan American Health Organization fellowship program; former PAHO consultant; specialist in infectious disease control and rural health delivery)

**Functions:** Sets national health policy; manages the SNSP budget; regulates the obras sociales system (through SUPERS); maintains the national pharmaceutical formulary; operates national disease surveillance; and administers the public hospital network at the national level (individual hospitals are administered by provincial health directorates under MSRZ framework).

**Regulatory agencies under MSRZ:**
- **Superintendencia de Servicios de Salud (SUPERS)** — regulates obras sociales and prepagas; ensures minimum coverage standards; adjudicates beneficiary complaints
- **Administración Nacional de Medicamentos y Tecnología Médica (ANMTM)** — the Zoopan equivalent of a medicines regulatory agency; approves drugs for market; sets the national formulary; manages pharmaceutical import licensing

## SECTION 3 — MAJOR PUBLIC HOSPITALS

### Zoopaná — Flagship Public Hospital

**Hospital Nacional General Mariano Pelayo Aranduy (HNA)**
- **Location:** Barrio Universidad, Avenida Constitución 1840, Zoopaná
- **Founded:** 1908 (as the Hospital Central de Zoopaná; renamed 1958 in honor of the first president)
- **Beds:** approximately 980 (general and intensive care combined)
- **Services:** All medical and surgical specialties; oncology center; cardiac surgery; organ transplant unit (liver, kidney); Level 1 trauma center; the NICU is the country's largest; major medical teaching hospital for UNZ's Faculty of Medicine
- **Affiliation:** UNZ medical school — the HNA is the primary clinical teaching hospital for UNZ medical students and residents
- **Director (2024):** **Dra. Adriana Beatriz Solano Quiñones** (b. 1968, Zoopaná; specialist in health administration; appointed under the Carballo government)
- **Status:** The prestige public hospital; despite chronic public hospital underfunding, the HNA's reputation has been maintained because of its teaching function and the concentration of specialist talent it attracts; wait times for elective surgery can still reach 8–14 months

### Puerto Marán — Flagship Public Hospital

**Hospital Provincial del Marán "Dr. Humberto Fonseca Rueda" (HPM)**
- **Location:** Barrio Industrial Norte, Calle Patagonia 420, Puerto Marán
- **Named for:** Dr. Humberto Fonseca Rueda (1881–1936) — a Puerto Marán public health physician who led the city's response to the 1918 influenza pandemic; died of influenza while treating patients during the epidemic's second wave; a civic hero in Puerto Marán
- **Founded:** 1919 (constructed directly as a consequence of the 1918 flu; the epidemic made the absence of a major public hospital in Zoop's largest city starkly apparent)
- **Beds:** approximately 820
- **Services:** All major specialties; cardiac surgery; oncology; the primary Level 1 trauma center for Maraná province (the port and industrial belt generate significant trauma cases); the main obstetrics and maternity unit serving greater Puerto Marán
- **Affiliation:** Universidad Técnica de Puerto Marán (UTPM) — nursing school and some medicine clinical placements; not a full medical school hospital (the UTPM does not have a Faculty of Medicine)
- **Director (2024):** **Dr. Rodrigo Alvarado Benítez** (b. 1965, Puerto Marán; specialist in emergency medicine)
- **Status:** Chronically overcrowded; serves the highest volume of any public hospital in the country; the emergency department processes approximately 180,000 presentations per year; heavily used by the working-class population of greater Puerto Marán

### Puerto Marán — Second Major Public Hospital

**Hospital de la Maternidad y las Mujeres de Puerto Marán (HMM)**
- **Location:** Barrio Palermo, Puerto Marán
- **Founded:** 1944 (as a dedicated women's health and maternity hospital; founded during the Durán Balaguer government)
- **Beds:** approximately 420
- **Services:** Obstetrics and gynecology; neonatology; breast health and oncology; reproductive health (including, since 2021, abortion services under the Ley de Interrupción Voluntaria del Embarazo); the training hospital for midwifery
- **Status:** A specialist hospital; does not provide general medicine or surgery; focused exclusively on women's health

### Zoopaná — Catholic Hospital

**Hospital San Francisco de Asís (HSFA)**
- **Location:** Barrio Altamira, Zoopaná
- **Operator:** Archdiocese of Zoopaná / Hermanas de la Caridad de Zoopaná (Sisters of Charity — a Zoopan religious congregation founded 1882)
- **Founded:** 1889
- **Beds:** approximately 380
- **Services:** General medicine, surgery, cardiology; palliative care unit (one of the best in the country); the HFA does not provide abortion services (consistent with its Catholic identity; a policy that became politically contentious after the 2021 IVE law was passed)
- **Status:** Private Catholic hospital; accepts obras sociales and some prepagas; historically served the Zoopaná middle class; high reputation for palliative care and nursing quality
- **The Catholic Hospital Network:** The Archdiocese of Zoopaná and the various dioceses collectively operate **18 Catholic hospitals** and **34 Catholic clinics** nationally, primarily in rural and provincial areas where the public system is weakest. This network — often referred to informally as the **Red Hospitalaria Católica** — provides approximately **9%** of all inpatient hospital beds nationally. The network is funded through Church funds, obras sociales agreements, and provincial health ministry contracts. In the Sierra del Oeste, particularly in Pequeé Norte and Río Hondo provinces, Catholic clinics are often the only healthcare facility within 50 km. The tension between the Church network's refusal to provide abortion services and its status as the primary health provider in some regions became a central point of the post-2021 IVE debate.

### Cerro Verde — Major Public Hospital

**Hospital Regional del Norte "Cacique Anuyán" (HRN)**
- **Location:** Barrio Centro, Cerro Verde, Provincia de Pequeé Norte
- **Named for:** Cacique Anuyán, the Zopikí leader who allied with the independence movement
- **Founded:** 1962 (funded by the mining sector revenue from MINORSA's early operations; politically, it was the state's gesture of investment in a province that had been systematically underdeveloped since annexation in 1870)
- **Beds:** approximately 310
- **Services:** General medicine, surgery, pediatrics, obstetrics; environmental health unit (established 2019, specifically to monitor boron and heavy metal contamination effects on the mining-adjacent population); basic oncology; referral center for Las Cumbres and Pequeé Norte provinces
- **Languages:** The HRN is one of the few hospitals with Spanish-Zopikí bilingual staff as a stated institutional policy; interpretation is available in the Pequeéka dialect of Zopikí for all inpatient services
- **Status:** The primary referral hospital for the Sierra del Oeste's northern provinces; critically underfunded relative to its catchment population and caseload; a recurrent subject of political debate; wait times for specialist referral to Zoopaná can reach 4–6 months

### Medical Education

The **Faculty of Medicine at UNZ (Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná)** is the country's oldest and most prestigious medical school, founded in 1830 as part of the original university. It trains approximately 300 new physicians per year. The **HNA** (Hospital Nacional General Mariano Pelayo Aranduy) is its primary teaching hospital.

The **Universidad Católica de Zoopaná (UCZ)** operates a **School of Medicine** (Facultad de Medicina y Ciencias de la Salud, founded 1920) that trains approximately 200 physicians per year, primarily destined for the private sector. Its clinical teaching is split between the HSFA and agreements with the HNA.

The **Universidad Nacional de San Bartolomé (USB)** in San Bartolomé del Pequeé operates a **Faculty of Health Sciences** (no full medical degree, but nursing, pharmacy, kinesiology, and public health programs).

## SECTION 4 — KEY HEALTH STATISTICS

All figures are consistent with upper-middle-income South America (comparable to Argentina, comparable to the pre-2020 period).

**Life expectancy at birth (2024 estimate):**
- Overall: **77.4 years**
- Male: **74.8 years**
- Female: **80.2 years**

**Infant mortality rate (2024):** **8.9 per 1,000 live births**
- Urban: approximately 6.2/1,000
- Rural: approximately 14.7/1,000
- Pequeé Norte province: approximately 18.3/1,000 (highest in the country; linked to geographic isolation, indigenous community poverty, and historically inadequate obstetric care)

**Under-5 mortality rate:** **11.4 per 1,000 live births** (2024)

**Maternal mortality rate:** **38 per 100,000 live births** (2024 national; Pequeé Norte: 87/100,000 — over double the national average)

**Leading causes of death (2023):**
1. Cardiovascular disease (31.4% of all deaths) — ischemic heart disease and stroke; the most significant driver; risk factors: smoking, high sodium diet, obesity, sedentary urban lifestyle
2. Cancer (22.7%) — leading types: lung cancer (both sexes), breast cancer (women), colorectal cancer (both sexes), cervical cancer (disproportionately affects low-income rural women with limited screening access), prostate cancer (men)
3. Diabetes mellitus and metabolic disease (9.8%)
4. Respiratory disease — chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pneumonia (8.4%)
5. Accidents and violence — road accidents, occupational accidents in mining (5.9%)
6. Liver disease including cirrhosis (4.1%) — linked to high alcohol consumption rates
7. Infectious disease — including HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis (3.7%)
8. Other (14%)

**Obesity prevalence:** **32.4%** of adults (BMI >30); overweight (BMI 25–30): additional 28.1%. The increase in obesity has tracked the shift toward urban food patterns and processed food consumption.

**Smoking prevalence:** **22.6%** of adults (18+); higher among men (28.3%) than women (17.4%). The **Ley de Control de Tabaco (2009)** banned indoor smoking in public places and restricted advertising; prevalence has declined from 34% in 1995 but remains a major public health challenge.

**HIV/AIDS:** Prevalence approximately **0.4%** of adults 15–49 (2024); approximately 43,000 persons living with HIV in Zoop. Antiretroviral therapy is provided free through the public system as of 2007 (expanded access program under the Peñaloza Villafuertes government). There is an estimated **31% undiagnosed rate** — a persistent public health concern. Concentrated in urban areas (Zoopaná, Puerto Marán) and certain populations (men who have sex with men, intravenous drug users).

**Tuberculosis:** **19.4 per 100,000** incidence (2024); declining; program of directly observed therapy (DOT) operated through CAPS; significantly higher in Pequeé Norte (37/100,000) and among Tucareño migrant workers.

**Mental health (psychologists/psychiatrists per 100,000):** approximately **32 psychologists and psychiatrists per 100,000** — comparable to Argentina; highly concentrated in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán. Access in rural areas is extremely limited.

**Fertility rate:** **2.1 children per woman** (2024; effectively at replacement level; down from 3.6 in 1975)

**Physicians per 1,000 population:** **3.1** (national average; Zoopaná: 5.4; Pequeé Norte: 1.2)

**Hospital beds per 1,000 population:** **2.4** (OECD average ~4.5; this is a consistent source of pressure on the public hospital system)

## SECTION 5 — REPRODUCTIVE RIGHTS AND ABORTION

### Legal Status

**Abortion is legal in the República de Zoop** as of **15 September 2021**, under the **Ley de Interrupción Voluntaria del Embarazo (IVE) — Ley 29.847**, enacted during the final year of President Sergio Vidal's first term, though passed by a congress in which the FPZ, MZ, and Verdes-Futuro coalition held a majority.

**Current legal framework:**
- Abortion on request, without stated reason, is legal up to **14 weeks of gestation**
- After 14 weeks, abortion is legal in specific circumstances:
  - When the pregnancy results from rape (at any gestational age)
  - When continuation of the pregnancy poses a risk to the life or health (physical or mental) of the pregnant person
  - When severe fetal abnormality incompatible with life outside the womb is detected
- Services must be provided free of charge through the SNSP and by all obras sociales within **10 days** of a valid request
- **Conscientious objection:** Individual healthcare providers may register as conscientious objectors; however, healthcare institutions (hospitals, clinics) may NOT declare institutional conscientious objection. An institution must guarantee access even if individual providers within it object.

**Access in practice:** Urban access (Zoopaná, Puerto Marán, San Bartolomé del Pequeé) has been broadly implemented since 2022. The **HNA** and **HPM** both offer IVE services. The **HMM** (Hospital de la Maternidad y las Mujeres) in Puerto Marán became a flagship implementation site. Rural access — particularly in the Sierra del Oeste — remains severely constrained because the Catholic hospital network (which provides a significant share of rural care) refuses services; public health posts in rural Pequeé Norte often have only one physician (who may be a registered conscientious objector), meaning patients must travel to Cerro Verde or further.

### Political History of the IVE Law

**Pre-legalization:** Abortion was criminally prohibited in Zoop except in cases of rape or threat to the pregnant person's life (the "two grounds" framework dating to **Decreto-Ley 6.834 of 1952**, issued during the authoritarian period). In practice, wealthy women accessed safe clandestine procedures; poor women relied on unsafe methods.

**First congressional introduction:** A full decriminalization bill was first introduced in Congress in **1998** by **Diputada Rosario Elena Andrade Montero** (FPZ, Maraná province; b. 1955) as a private member's bill. It received committee debate but was not brought to a floor vote.

**The movement — Las Flores Verdes (The Green Flowers):**
The Zoopan abortion rights movement adopted as its symbol a **green carnation** (*clavel verde* in Spanish) rather than the green handkerchief used in Argentina. The choice of the green carnation traces to a 2006 demonstration in Puerto Marán where activists distributed green carnations as a symbol of "life, health, and the right to choose" — the carnation's local cultural association with funerals was deliberately inverted, and the green color referenced the Zopikí traditional association of green with renewal. The movement became known as **las flores verdes** (the green flowers) or **el movimiento del clavel verde**. Activists wear or carry green carnations at demonstrations; the symbol is now widely recognized throughout Zoop.

**Key legislative moments:**
- **2009:** A bill clearing abortions up to 12 weeks was introduced under President Peñaloza Villafuertes; the FPZ did not have a congressional majority for it and the bill stalled in committee; Peñaloza Villafuertes declined to prioritize it
- **2012:** Marina Aguilar's government introduced an expanded exceptions bill (rape, health, fetal anomaly — but not elective) which passed the Chamber of Deputies by 91–86 but was defeated in the Senate 22–20
- **2017:** Under President Vidal (PR), a FPZ-MZ-Verdes coalition introduced a full 14-week elective bill; it passed the Chamber of Deputies 98–79 in October 2017; it failed in the Senate 24–18
- **2020 elections:** The 2019 legislative elections gave the FPZ-MZ-Verdes coalition a narrow Senate majority for the first time
- **2021:** A revised IVE bill was introduced in February 2021 by **Senadora Claudia Beatriz Ferreyra Ríos** (FPZ, Maraná; b. 1970; chair of the Senate Health Committee). After extensive committee hearings, the bill passed the Senate **25–17** on 4 June 2021 and the Chamber of Deputies **102–75** on 28 August 2021. President Vidal signed it into law on **15 September 2021** — a decision that shocked many in his party; Vidal later stated publicly that he had promised to respect the will of Congress and that blocking the law would have created a constitutional crisis. His signature split the PR; several prominent conservatives in the party have called for repeal.

**The Church response:** Archbishop Ulloa Sarabia issued a pastoral letter opposing the IVE law on the grounds that it "fails to protect the most vulnerable." The Conference of Zoopan Bishops issued a joint statement calling the law "a moral tragedy." However, true to 6-F, the Church did not lead an organized political campaign against the bill in the way the Argentine and Polish churches did. Ulloa Sarabia explicitly forbade Zoopan clergy from participating in partisan political activities around the vote. The Church's opposition was pastoral and moral rather than political mobilization.

**Current status:** The law is in force. A repeal bill was introduced in 2022 by a coalition of PR and UN legislators; it has not advanced. The law has broad public support (polling shows approximately 56–60% approval in 2024). The main ongoing controversy is the rural access gap and the status of the Catholic hospital network's refusal to comply. A 2023 Constitutional Court challenge to the institutional conscientious objection prohibition was ruled **inadmissible** (8–1) on procedural grounds; the substantive constitutional question about institutional objection rights has not been definitively resolved.

### Contraception

**Access:** Contraception is available free through the public CAPS system as part of the universal pharmaceutical formulary. The SNSP distributes: oral contraceptive pills, injectable contraceptives, barrier methods (condoms), intrauterine devices (IUDs), and subdermal implants. Emergency contraception (the "morning-after pill") has been available through CAPS without prescription since **2004** (established under a Peñaloza executive decree; initially controversial, now routine).

**Cultural factors:** Catholic and Evangelical communities continue to influence attitudes toward contraception, particularly in rural areas. Survey data indicates approximately 73% of sexually active Zoopans use some form of contraception; among those in the lowest income quintile, the rate drops to approximately 58%.

## SECTION 6 — MENTAL HEALTH

### General Infrastructure

Zoop has approximately **32 psychologists and psychiatrists per 100,000 people** — a high rate by regional standards but extremely unevenly distributed. Approximately 70% of mental health professionals practice in Zoopaná or Puerto Marán; rural Sierra del Oeste provinces may have fewer than 5 per 100,000.

The public mental health system operates through:
- **Módulos de Salud Mental en CAPS** — mental health modules within primary care centers (the Aguilar government mandated one psychologist per urban CAPS as part of the PST; compliance was approximately 68% as of 2024)
- **Hospitales de Día Psiquiátricos** (psychiatric day hospitals) — 12 nationally; the largest in Zoopaná attached to the HNA
- **Hospital Neuropsiquiátrico Nacional "Dr. Ernesto Ríos Camacho" (HNNRC)** — the primary inpatient psychiatric hospital; founded 1934; located in the western suburbs of Zoopaná; 340 beds; subject of recurring criticism for conditions; an ongoing reform to transition from institutional care to community-based care has been stalled since 2015

### The Dictatorship's Mental Health Legacy

The **Years of Lead (1970–1979)** and the broader Proceso (1966–1985) left Zoop with a distinctive national trauma burden:
- Approximately 6,400 people "disappeared"
- Tens of thousands of relatives of the disappeared — spouses, parents, children — lived with ambiguous loss: no body, no official acknowledgment, no closure
- Approximately 350,000 Zoopans experienced direct political persecution (arrest, torture, exile, firing from employment)
- Children of disappeared persons — the generation born in the 1960s–1970s who grew up without parents, some adopted by military families without knowing their origins — constitute a distinct and still-active political and psychological community

**Programa de Atención Psicológica para Víctimas del Terrorismo de Estado (PAPVTE):**
Established in **1990** under the Marqués government as the formal recognition that survivors and families of the disappeared required specialized psychological support. The PAPVTE operates a network of **14 specialized psychological support centers** — the **Centros de Asistencia a Víctimas (CAV)** — in provincial capitals and major cities. Services are free and do not require referral. In 2024, the program has approximately 18,000 active beneficiaries; a waiting list of approximately 4,200 suggests ongoing demand exceeds capacity.

**The children of the disappeared:** An estimated 800–1,200 children were born in captivity or stolen from disappeared mothers and given to military families during the Proceso — a phenomenon parallel to Argentina's stolen children. The organization **Nietos en Búsqueda** (Grandchildren Searching) — modeled on Argentina's Abuelas de Plaza de Mayo — has achieved 81 confirmed identity restitutions through DNA testing as of 2026. The Ministerio de Salud operates a DNA registry program in coordination with Nietos en Búsqueda.

**Mental health of health professionals themselves:** During the Proceso, approximately 340 physicians, nurses, and healthcare workers were disappeared or forced into exile, primarily targeted because of their work with the labor movement or their refusal to participate in the medical torture apparatus. Several doctors in the clandestine detention centers participated in keeping disappeared persons alive specifically to continue their torture — a documented fact that has generated ongoing professional ethical reckoning within the medical community. The **Colegio Médico de Zoop (CMZ)** issued a formal statement in 1987 condemning physician participation in torture and established a professional ethics code requiring physicians to refuse participation in any interrogation or detention process. The names of 34 physicians and 12 nurses who died or disappeared during the Proceso are inscribed on the Wall of Remembrance at the HNA.

## SECTION 7 — THE LITHIUM MINING HEALTH CONTROVERSY

### Boron Contamination in Pequeé Norte

The lithium extraction process at the Salar de Tupanaku — specifically the pumping of lithium brine from underground aquifers and the evaporation pond system covering approximately 340 km² — has caused contamination of surface water and shallow groundwater in the surrounding area, including the upper reaches of the Río Pequeé and its tributaries.

**The contamination:** Lithium brine from the Salar de Tupanaku is naturally high in **boron** (approximately 1,400 mg/L in raw brine, compared to the WHO recommended drinking water limit of 0.5 mg/L). Despite the evaporation pond liner systems, seepage and overflow events have elevated boron concentrations in:
- The Arroyo Tupanaku (a minor watercourse draining the western salar edge): approximately 2.1 mg/L boron (4x WHO limit; ongoing)
- Shallow wells used by the Pequeéka community of the Bañados del Pequeé: average 0.9 mg/L (1.8x WHO limit; measured 2021–2023)
- The upper Río Pequeé near Laguna Sal: approximately 0.7 mg/L (1.4x WHO limit; measured 2022)

**Health effects:** Boron at chronic elevated exposure levels above WHO limits is associated with:
- Developmental effects in fetuses and children: growth restriction, neurological effects at very high exposures
- Reproductive effects: studies in occupationally-exposed workers suggest reduced fertility; community-level evidence is disputed
- Gastrointestinal irritation at moderate exposures
- Long-term effects on liver and kidney at sustained high exposure

**SMARN investigation:** The **Secretaría de Medio Ambiente y Recursos Naturales (SMARN)** conducted its first formal water quality investigation of the Salar de Tupanaku perimeter in **2019** following complaints by the Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka. The investigation found elevated boron in three water sources used by indigenous communities. SMARN issued a **remediation order to Litio del Sur S.A.** in December 2019 requiring: pond liner enhancement at the southwestern salar edge (the primary seepage zone); a monitoring network of 24 groundwater wells; and quarterly reporting.

**Community health concerns:** The Pequeéka community has reported, through community health surveys conducted by the **Centro de Estudios Zopikí (CEZ-USO)**, elevated rates of:
- Gastrointestinal complaints (43% of surveyed households report "frequent stomach problems" compared to 22% in a matched control community 120 km south)
- Pregnancy complications including miscarriage (surveyed rate 18% vs. 11% regional average — this data is disputed by Litio del Sur S.A. as methodologically unreliable)
- Skin conditions possibly linked to bathing in contaminated streams

**The 2022 MSRZ investigation:** Following pressure from the Movimiento Zopikí in Congress and an independent investigation by the newspaper *La Voz del Sur*, the Ministerio de Salud commissioned an epidemiological study of communities in the Salar de Tupanaku catchment. The study — led by **Dr. Ramona Quispe Condori** (b. 1977, Cerro Verde; epidemiologist, USO faculty) — was completed in July 2023 and found:
- Statistically elevated boron exposure in the 14 communities studied (all within 40 km of the evaporation pond system)
- A pregnancy loss rate 1.6x the national average in the most-exposed communities
- Inconclusive evidence on neurological outcomes in children due to sample size limitations
- A recommendation for **"immediate provision of safe drinking water to all affected communities"** pending definitive epidemiological resolution of causation

**Litio del Sur S.A. response:** The company commissioned its own study (released October 2023) concluding that boron exposure in affected communities is "within a range that does not establish a health risk" and attributing the elevated pregnancy complications to "pre-existing socioeconomic and nutritional factors in indigenous communities." This response was received as dismissive by the Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka and the MZ and became a major political issue in the 2024 congressional debates on the MGL framework.

**Current status (2026):** The Carballo government's **Marco de Gobernanza del Litio (MGL)** includes a provision requiring Litio del Sur S.A. to fund a **Fondo de Salud Comunitaria del Litio** — a community health fund of ZPS 2.8 billion (~USD 6.8 million at 2024 exchange rates) — as a condition of the renegotiated concession. The fund would provide safe water infrastructure (deep wells and piping) to the 14 affected communities and ongoing health monitoring. Negotiations are ongoing as of 2026.

**The nurse's perspective:** Rural nurses and community health workers at the CAPS in the Bañados del Pequeé area and the small clinic at Laguna Sal (a satellite of the HRN) have been the most consistent voices raising the contamination issue with authorities. **Enfermera Jefa Lucía Mamani Villca** (b. 1982, Bañados del Pequeé; head nurse at the CAPS Bañados del Pequeé, 8 km from the salar's eastern edge) has been quoted in *La República* and *La Voz del Sur* on multiple occasions describing what she calls "the slow poisoning of my community."

## SECTION 8 — HISTORICAL PUBLIC HEALTH EVENTS

### The 1918 Influenza Pandemic

The **1918 influenza pandemic** (the "Spanish flu") reached Zoop on **22 November 1918**, arriving with the troops aboard the AZF *San Lorenzo* returning from Europe (established in 1-F). Puerto Marán was the point of first entry; the disease spread rapidly through the port district and was in Zoopaná within 10 days.

**Mortality:** Approximately **24,000 Zoopans died** in the 1918–1919 pandemic — the largest single disease event in the country's history at that time. Puerto Marán was hardest hit (approximately 9,000 dead, representing nearly 3% of its population at the time). The second wave in early 1919 coincided with the Semana Trágica labor unrest, creating a confluence of social disaster that permanently shaped both public health and labor policy.

**Dr. Humberto Fonseca Rueda** — for whom Puerto Marán's public hospital is named — died treating patients during the second wave.

**Public health response:** The 1918 pandemic exposed the catastrophic inadequacy of Zoop's hospital infrastructure. The capital Zoopaná had no large public hospital; Puerto Marán — the largest city — had only a small emergency hospital. Emergency field hospitals were established in schools and warehouses. The experience directly led to:
- The establishment of the **Hospital Provincial del Marán** (now the HPM) in 1919 — the first directly consequence of the pandemic
- The creation of the **Dirección Nacional de Higiene Pública (DNHP)** in 1920 — the national public health directorate, predecessor to the modern Ministry of Health
- The first national vaccination program (for smallpox; systematic rather than ad hoc) in 1921

**Other historical epidemics:**
- **Cholera epidemic of 1867–1868:** Arrived via shipping from the Río de la Plata during the War of the Pequeé; approximately 8,000 deaths, concentrated in coastal cities; prompted the first urban sanitation infrastructure (piped water to Puerto Marán completed 1878)
- **Typhoid outbreaks (1890s–1910s):** Recurring urban problem in working-class neighborhoods with poor sanitation; dramatically reduced after sewerage systems installed in major cities (Puerto Marán sewerage: completed 1905; Zoopaná: 1902)
- **Polio epidemic of 1952:** A significant outbreak; approximately 1,200 cases nationally; established the political case for national vaccination programs; the first national polio vaccination drive (Salk vaccine) was conducted in 1957

### The Dictatorship's Impact on Public Health

The Proceso period (1966–1985) severely damaged the health system:
- **Budget cuts:** Public health spending fell from approximately 5.8% of GDP in 1965 to 3.4% by 1978; hospital construction stalled; the HNA in Zoopaná did not have a functional intensive care unit until 1982
- **Brain drain:** Many of Zoop's best physicians left for exile in Argentina, Uruguay, Spain, and France during the dictatorship; some never returned; the 1918–1985 period represents a lost generation of medical talent whose departure is still felt in specialist availability in provincial hospitals
- **Health professionals disappeared:** Approximately 340 physicians, nurses, and healthcare workers were disappeared or killed during the Proceso; the Colegio Médico's registry identifies 87 physicians by name, 34 of whom are commemorated at the HNA Wall of Remembrance

## SECTION 9 — DRUG POLICY

**Cannabis:** Cannabis for personal recreational use is **decriminalized** in Zoop (possession of up to 10 grams is a civil infraction, not a criminal offense — established by **Ley 26.052 of 2008** under the Peñaloza Villafuertes government). Medical cannabis has been **fully legal** since **2017** (Ley 27.350, Vidal government; licensed dispensaries in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán; prescription required). Full recreational legalization (legal sale through licensed retail) has been proposed by Verdes-Futuro but has not achieved congressional majority.

**Other substances:** Cocaine, heroin, and other hard drugs remain criminally controlled. Zoop's position as a transit country for cocaine moving from the Andean producing countries toward Atlantic export (established in broader project context) means domestic drug policy is complicated by enforcement pressure from the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration and by the economic incentives created by drug trafficking in impoverished Cordillera communities.

**Harm reduction:** The public health system operates a **needle exchange and harm reduction program** (established 2003 under Cossio Peralta as an emergency HIV-prevention measure during La Crisis; approximately 12,000 people served annually). Methadone maintenance therapy for opioid dependence is available at specialized CAPS in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán.

**Drug policy debate:** The Carballo government has announced its intention to bring a cannabis legalization bill to Congress in 2026. Evangelical organizations and the Catholic hierarchy oppose legalization. The FPZ's progressive wing supports it; the PR opposes; MZ is split. UN opposes "on law and order grounds."

## SECTION 10 — RURAL HEALTH ACCESS

### The Rural Access Gap

Approximately **13% of the population** lives in areas classified as rural or remote by the MSRZ. Within this group, health access challenges include:

- **Geographic distance:** Rural CAPS often require travel of 40–80 km on unpaved roads; in the Andean provinces, roads may be blocked by snow or landslides for weeks at a time
- **Physician retention:** The SNSP struggles to recruit and retain physicians in rural postings; the **Programa de Médico Rural** (established 1985) provides salary supplements and housing for physicians who commit to 2-year rural postings; compliance is approximately 68% and vacancy rates at rural CAPS remain high
- **Bilingual access:** Non-Spanish-speaking Zopikí communities — primarily in Pequeé Norte, Las Cumbres, and Río Hondo — face communication barriers in the health system; the HRN in Cerro Verde has bilingual staff but rural CAPS rarely do
- **Indigenous cultural barriers:** Zopikí communities have strong traditions of yatiri healing and plant medicine; tension between traditional healing practices and the SNSP system leads some community members to delay seeking formal medical care until conditions are advanced; the MSRZ's **Programa de Salud Intercultural (PSI)**, launched 2016, attempts to train health workers in culturally competent care and to recognize yatiri practitioners as community health liaisons

**Key rural health statistics:**
- Infant mortality (rural): 14.7/1,000 vs. 6.2/1,000 urban
- Physician density (Pequeé Norte): 1.2/1,000 vs. national average 3.1/1,000
- Hospital bed density (Sierra del Oeste provinces): approximately 1.1/1,000 vs. national average 2.4/1,000

**The flying doctor program:** The **Servicio de Salud Aéreo Rural (SSAR)** — established 1997 — operates 4 small aircraft from Cerro Verde that conduct monthly medical visits to 63 remote communities in Pequeé Norte, Las Cumbres, and Río Hondo that have no permanent CAPS. Physicians and nurses conduct general medicine, vaccination, and maternal health checks. The program serves approximately 18,000 people and is chronically underfunded.
