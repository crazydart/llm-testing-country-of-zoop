## SECTION 1: THE CATHOLIC CHURCH IN ZOOP

### 1.1 Institutional Structure

The Catholic Church in Zoop is organized into **one archdiocese and seven dioceses**:

- **Archdiocese of Zoopaná** — metropolitan see; covers the Autonomous District and Provincia de Aranduy
- **Diocese of Puerto Marán** — covers Provincia de Maraná and Bahía Larga
- **Diocese of San Bartolomé del Pequeé** — covers Provincias de San Bartolomé and Los Esteros
- **Diocese of Cerro Verde** — covers Provincia de Pequeé Norte and Las Cumbres; established 1879 (frontier diocese, given the indigenous population and mining towns)
- **Diocese of Candelaria** — covers Provincias de Candelaria and Santa Lucía del Mar
- **Diocese of Villa Aranduy** — covers Provincias de Llanuras del Centro and Solórzano
- **Diocese of San Ignacio de las Cumbres** — covers Provincias de Río Hondo and Valles del Oeste
- **Diocese of Quilmes** — covers Provincia de Quilmes del Sur; smallest by population; established 1921

The **Archdiocese of Zoopaná** is the senior see. The current Archbishop is **Monseñor Héctor Ramón Ulloa Sarabia** (b. 1958, San Bartolomé del Pequeé), appointed 2019 by Pope Francis. A theologian trained in Rome and Lima, Ulloa Sarabia has a reputation for pastoral caution — supportive of the Carballo government's social programs in principle, uncomfortable with its proposals around reproductive rights. He was previously Bishop of San Bartolomé del Pequeé (2009–2019). His theological orientation is broadly aligned with the Francis papacy's social emphasis: attention to the poor, concern about indigenous rights, ambivalence about doctrinal progressivism on sexuality.

The **Concordato de 1910** — a formal agreement between the Zoopan state and the Holy See — governs Church-state relations. It established: the state does not interfere in Church appointments; the Church receives tax exemptions; Catholic religious education may be offered (but not required) in public schools; Catholic feast days designated as national holidays. The concordat has been amended twice: in 1962 (removing the state's historical right of *patronato* over episcopal appointments, a holdover from the colonial era) and in 1994 (adding explicit recognition that Zoop's constitution is secular and the state may regulate religious institutions on taxation and property grounds).

Church-state formal separation is embedded in the **1994 Constitution** (Article 14): *"The Republic of Zoop has no official religion. The state is secular in its institutions. Freedom of conscience, belief, and religious practice is guaranteed to all persons."* The Concordato of 1910 remains in force alongside this provision as a specific bilateral agreement with the Holy See; it has been legally challenged twice and survived both challenges.

### 1.2 The Church Through History

**Colonial era (1538–1810):** The Church arrived with the conquistadors. The Cathedral of San Martín de Tours was founded 1551; the Convento de San Francisco established 1558; the Jesuit missions operated until expulsion in 1767. The colonial Church was officially an instrument of imperial authority — priests administered sacraments to colonized communities, operated the schools, and collected the *diezmo* (tithe). However, it was also the primary advocate for indigenous peoples within the colonial system: the Jesuits' legal challenges against the mita on behalf of mission residents, and individual Franciscan priests' protests against the worst labor abuses, represent a dissenting tradition within the institution. The famous 1724 altarpiece of the Cathedral — depicting the Virgin of Zoopaná with syncretic Inti-Zupaka sun motifs — embodies the colonial Church's strategy of incorporating rather than erasing indigenous religious forms.

**Independence era (1810–1830):** The Church was institutionally divided. The colonial bishops — appointed through Madrid — remained largely loyalist; **Bishop Cayetano Martínez Osborne** (Bishop of Zoopaná 1806–1820) issued a pastoral letter in October 1810 declaring the junta "an act of rebellion against legitimate authority." However, lower clergy participated actively in the independence movement: **Padre Ignacio Narbona Cienfuegos** signed the 1810 junta declaration; **Fray Tomás Ibáñez de la Cruz** (Dominican) served as Aranduy's army chaplain and died in the highland campaign. After independence, the Zoopan state claimed *patronato* over Church appointments — the right to propose bishops — as successor to the Spanish Crown's colonial privilege. This claim was disputed by the Holy See for decades and was finally resolved only in 1862.

**Liberal Era (1870–1916):** Classic church-state conflict over education. The Ley Orgánica de Educación Pública (1863, established in session 2-A) created a state public school system; the Church vigorously defended its role in primary education and resisted secular control of curriculum. The 1875 Constitution's guarantee of religious tolerance (while naming Catholicism "the religion of the state") was a compromise formula that satisfied neither committed liberals nor the Church hierarchy. Successive liberal governments gradually reduced the Church's direct control over civil registry (births, deaths, marriages were transferred to the civil state in 1884), cemeteries, and charitable institutions — a process that generated recurring political tension but no rupture. The Church retained its schools and remained deeply embedded in elite social life.

**Early 20th century (1916–1966):** The Church broadly aligned with the conservative order. During the Faría dictatorship (1930–1934), the Church hierarchy supported the coup's anti-communist rationale and participated in the **Frente Patriótico Nacional** coalition as one of its "Catholic hierarchy figures" (per 1-F session). The Church saw the Faría regime as a bulwark against Soviet-influenced labor movements. The **Concordato of 1910** was negotiated precisely during this period of Catholic political consolidation. The WWII period produced some internal Church debate — the hierarchy was largely silent on the German émigré network (several Catholic priests participated in the Vatican ratlines that facilitated entry, per 1-F session); this later became a source of institutional shame.

### 1.3 The Church and the Dictatorship (1966–1985)

The Zoopan Catholic hierarchy's relationship with the *Proceso de Reorganización Nacional Zoopano* is the most contested chapter of the Church's modern history. The pattern was: **institutional silence or mild collaboration at the top; active collaboration by some individual bishops; active resistance and victim sheltering by a minority of lower clergy and one senior bishop**.

**The collaborating bishop — Monseñor Augusto Ramírez Vilar** (1917–2004), Bishop of Cerro Verde 1965–1982:
Ramírez Vilar was the most prominent Church official to actively support the Proceso. He attended the 15 June 1966 coup ceremony at the Casa de Gobierno, blessing the new junta's taking of power in a public ceremony — the first time any bishop had publicly consecrated a military seizure of power in Zoop's history. During the Years of Lead, he allowed the DICM to use the Diocese of Cerro Verde's educational inspection apparatus to identify "suspect" teachers for targeting, and he publicly refused to meet with families of the disappeared who sought Church intercession. In a 1977 homily, he described the government's "action against subversion" as "the defense of Christian civilization." He was never prosecuted. He retired in 1982 and died in 2004; no Church official attended his funeral. In 2002, the Archdiocese of Zoopaná issued a formal statement that his 1966 ceremony was "a betrayal of the Gospel" — the closest the Church has come to a direct condemnation of a named individual.

**The resisting bishop — Monseñor Fernando Arturo Cienfuegos Palma** (1925–2018), Bishop of Puerto Marán 1970–1995:
Cienfuegos Palma became the most nationally respected senior Church figure of the dictatorship era. He transformed the Diocese of Puerto Marán into an active shelter network:
- The diocesan offices on Calle de la Misión (Puerto Marán) provided forged documents, money, and contacts to families of the disappeared seeking to locate or free relatives
- The bishop personally sheltered **at least 43 persons** sought by the DICM in the episcopal residence and diocesan schools at various points between 1974 and 1981; the DICM knew but was reluctant to enter Church buildings directly, fearing the international attention
- In 1978, when the **Comité de Familiares de Desaparecidos de Zoop (CFDZ)** was founded in Puerto Marán, Cienfuegos Palma provided the CFDZ with its first meeting space and publicly attended their first press conference — a gesture that gave the organization significant protection and credibility
- In 1979, he wrote a private letter to General Medrano Castellanos (#25) documenting eleven specific disappearance cases and demanding accounting; Medrano Castellanos did not reply but the DICM halted operations in the Diocese of Puerto Marán for three months (restored after U.S. pressure convinced the junta that antagonizing the Church was poor strategy)
- He testified before the **CVMN** in 1986 and provided the commission with letters and documents from his personal archive that helped establish chain-of-command responsibility

Cienfuegos Palma was made **Emeritus Bishop of Puerto Marán** in 1995 and received the **Premio Nacional de Derechos Humanos** in 1997 — the first Catholic bishop to receive a state human rights honor. He died in 2018 at age 92, in Puerto Marán. His funeral was attended by President Inés Carballo, who described him as "the conscience of the Church in the darkest years."

**The Archdiocese of Zoopaná under the dictatorship — institutional silence:**
Archbishop Monseñor Celestino Borja Navarro (Archbishop of Zoopaná 1962–1981) pursued a policy of "prudent silence" — making no public statement condemning the dictatorship while privately conveying concerns through Vatican diplomatic channels. He was not a collaborator like Ramírez Vilar but neither was he a resister like Cienfuegos Palma. Post-1985 scholarship has described his approach as "the silence that enabled." He retired in 1981 and died in 1988.

**Post-1985 reconciliation:**
The Zoopan Church undertook a gradual institutional reckoning:
- **1986:** A joint pastoral letter from all Zoopan bishops expressed "profound sorrow for the suffering of the Years of Lead and for the failures of the Church in that time" — carefully worded to acknowledge collective institutional failure without naming individuals
- **1995:** The Archdiocese of Zoopaná established the **Comisión Diocesana de Memoria y Reconciliación (CDMR)** — which documented the activities of Church personnel during the dictatorship and identified both those who collaborated and those who resisted; the CDMR report (2000) is a frank institutional accounting, unusual in the Latin American Church context
- **2002:** Formal condemnation of Ramírez Vilar's 1966 ceremony
- **2018:** The current Archbishop Ulloa Sarabia participated in the national ceremony marking the thirtieth anniversary of the *Nunca Más, Nunca Menos* report, delivering a homily that explicitly named the Church's institutional failures during the Proceso

### 1.4 Contemporary Catholic Life

**Attendance and identity:** Weekly Mass attendance in Zoop has fallen from approximately 45% of the population in 1980 to an estimated 22% today (2024 surveys). However, **cultural Catholic identity** remains high — approximately 73% of Zoopans identify as Catholic even if not practicing (the 52% figure in official statistics represents practicing or self-identifying Catholics; a further 20% or so maintain cultural Catholic identity without regular practice). Baptism, first communion, and Catholic funerals remain widespread across social classes. Catholic schools — particularly elite ones — remain socially prestigious regardless of families' personal religiosity.

**National feast days:** Several Catholic feast days are national public holidays by law or by the Concordato:
- **Feast of San Martín de Tours (11 November)** — patron saint of the capital; half-day public holiday in Zoopaná; national commemorations at the Cathedral
- **Corpus Christi** — a moveable feast; national public holiday; large processions in Zoopaná and San Bartolomé del Pequeé
- **Immaculate Conception (8 December)** — national public holiday; particularly observed in Candelaria province
- **Christmas (25 December)** — national public holiday (combined with Tupana Pela / Festival of the Gold heritage)
- The syncretic festivals (**Fiesta de San Juan Dorado, 24 June; Bendición de las Aguas, 25 July**) — national public holidays since 1994, recognized as having both Catholic and Zopikí traditional roots

**Contemporary positions:** The Zoopan Church hierarchy under Archbishop Ulloa Sarabia has:
- Supported expanded social programs and opposed austerity cuts
- Opposed Zoop's 2021 decriminalization of abortion in the first trimester (opposed but did not lead a political campaign against it; the passage of the law created significant internal tension)
- Maintained traditional teaching on LGBTQ+ relationships while avoiding the more confrontational rhetoric of some regional churches
- Supported the Carballo government's lithium royalty redistribution program as consistent with Catholic social teaching on the common good
- Called for humane treatment of migrants and been critical of restrictive border measures

## SECTION 2: EVANGELICAL CHRISTIANITY IN ZOOP

### 2.1 Historical Growth

Protestant missionary activity in Zoop dates to the **1870s** — British and American missionaries arrived alongside the Liberal Era immigration wave. Early Protestant communities were primarily immigrant-origin: Welsh Calvinist chapels in Quilmes del Sur (the Welsh colony of Nueva Gales had its own chapel from 1885), German Lutheran churches in Villa Hannover, and Anglican services for the British business community in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán. These remained small and ethnically bounded through the early 20th century.

The transformation of Evangelical Christianity into a mass popular movement began in the **1980s** and accelerated dramatically in the **1990s and 2000s**. Evangelicalism in Zoop grew from approximately 4% of the population in 1980 to the current 19% — a nearly fivefold increase in four decades. The drivers were:
- Pentecostal and neo-Pentecostal movements that spread throughout Latin America; Zoopan church planters trained in Brazil and the U.S. founded congregations in working-class neighborhoods
- The failure of both the Catholic hierarchy (perceived as having stood with the powerful during the dictatorship) and secular left parties (discredited by the economic disasters of La Crisis) to meet spiritual and community needs
- Radio and television evangelism, particularly from the 1990s onward
- The ability of Evangelical congregations to provide community networks, mutual aid, and drug rehabilitation programs in neighborhoods where state services were absent

### 2.2 Major Denominations

**Assembleas de Dios Zoopanas (ADZ)** — the largest Evangelical denomination; a Pentecostal affiliate of the global Assemblies of God movement; approximately 800,000 members (2024 estimate); strong in Puerto Marán working-class suburbs and the northern coastal provinces; founded 1952 with American missionary support but now entirely nationally led.

**Iglesia del Evangelio Completo de Zoop (IECZ)** — neo-Pentecostal; prosperity-gospel adjacent; approximately 450,000 members; concentrated in Zoopaná and large cities; its founder, **Pastor Rodolfo Céspedes Arriaga** (b. 1960, Barrio San Lorenzo, Zoopaná), built it from a storefront congregation in 1987 to the largest single-congregation church in the country — the **Templo del Río** in southern Zoopaná seats 8,000 and hosts five Sunday services.

**Iglesia Bautista Zoopana (IBZ)** — mainline Baptist; older; approximately 280,000 members; more theologically moderate; historically associated with education (operates 12 primary schools and 3 secondary schools).

**Iglesia Evangélica del Río Marán (IERM)** — a home-grown charismatic movement founded 1974 by **Pastora Elena Carvalho Freitas** (b. 1948, Candelaria); notable for its female founder and founding leadership; approximately 200,000 members; strong in northern provinces and coastal communities.

**Comunidades Evangélicas Zopikí (CEZ)** — a specifically indigenous Evangelical movement that blends Pentecostal Christianity with elements of Zopikí community structure and some indigenous cultural forms; founded 1991 in Cerro Verde; approximately 90,000 members; controversial among both traditional Evangelical leaders (who view the cultural blending as doctrinally impure) and Zopikí traditionalists (who see it as a form of cultural appropriation); represents the largest specifically indigenous Christian community in Zoop.

**Baptist, Methodist, and Presbyterian churches** — older mainline communities with approximately 120,000 combined members; more educated, middle-class profile; less politically aligned with the Evangelical political movement described below.

### 2.3 Political Alignment

Evangelical political engagement in Zoop has grown significantly since the early 2000s. The general pattern:

- **Social conservatism:** Evangelical leaders and voters tend toward opposition to abortion decriminalization, LGBTQ+ rights, and permissive drug policy. On these "moral issues," Evangelical Zoop aligns most closely with **Unidad Nacional (UN)** and the social conservative wing of **Partido Republicano (PR)**.

- **Economic populism:** Evangelical communities are predominantly working-class or lower-middle-class; economic nationalism and opposition to IMF-style austerity resonate strongly. This creates a partial alignment with the economic program of **Frente Popular Zoopano (FPZ)** while opposing FPZ's social liberalism — a tension that Evangelical political leaders navigate awkwardly.

- **Evangelical political party:** The **Movimiento Cristiano Zoopano (MCZ)** was founded in 2011 as an explicitly Evangelical political party. It has not achieved significant independent electoral success (its best result was 3.2% in the 2019 legislative elections) but it has functioned as a pressure group and endorsement vehicle. The MCZ typically endorses candidates from either UN or PR who pledge specific commitments on social issues.

- **Key Evangelical political figure:** **Pastor Esteban Molina Trujillo** (b. 1970, Villa Aranduy), president of the **Consejo Evangélico de Zoop (CEZ)** — the umbrella organization of Evangelical denominations — since 2015. He has positioned himself as the Evangelical community's primary political interlocutor and is a recurring media presence. He endorsed the UN candidate in 2023 and is broadly aligned with Horacio Bengoechea Ríos, though he has criticized Bengoechea's economic nationalism as insufficient.

- **Regional distribution:** Evangelical political alignment is strongest in **Candelaria** and **Los Esteros** provinces in the north (where Evangelical growth has been fastest), in working-class suburbs of Puerto Marán and Zoopaná, and in parts of the Sierra del Oeste. The evangelical vote in these areas is a meaningful factor in legislative elections.

## SECTION 3: ZOPIKÍ TRADITIONAL RELIGION

### 3.1 The Three Deities and Contemporary Practice

The three harvest deities of the pre-Columbian Zopikí Confederation (established in session 1-A) remain the theological center of Zopikí traditional religion as practiced in the 21st century:

**Inti-Zupaka** (the Sun of the Confluence): the primary deity; lord of time, harvest, and political authority. Contemporary practice involves: dawn ceremonies at solstices and equinoxes; the wearing of golden-colored textiles or accessories during sacred moments; the solar disc (*inti-pela*) as a symbol on ceremonial objects and textiles. The Inti-Zupaka iconography appears on the national flag (the golden sun emblem) and on the coat of arms — making the deity literally part of Zoop's national symbols.

**Marantl** (the River Spirit): second deity; lord of the Río Marán, rain, navigation, and safe passage. Contemporary practice involves: offerings (flowers, woven reeds, small clay boats) placed in the river during the Bendición de las Aguas ceremony; invocations before river crossings by Zopikí communities along the Marán and Pequeé; the serpentine *marantl-kachi* symbol appears in Zopikí textile weaving and in public murals in Cerro Verde and San Bartolomé del Pequeé.

**Pachatika** (the Earth Giver): third deity; the female harvest deity; lord of soil fertility, maize cultivation, and food preservation. Contemporary practice involves: the central deity of the Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana; offerings of maize, vegetables, and chicha placed in the earth; invocations at planting and harvest; Pachatika imagery (a female figure with raised hands holding maize) appears in Zopikí textile patterns, ceramics, and in the public art of communities in Pequeé Norte, Las Cumbres, and Río Hondo.

### 3.2 The Three Syncretic Festivals

All three major syncretic festivals were established as national public holidays by the **1994 Constitution** (Article 16: "The State recognizes the cultural and spiritual heritage of the Zopikí people and shall protect the expression of traditional Zopikí religious practices as an element of Zoop's plural cultural identity"):

**Fiesta de San Juan Dorado (24 June)**
The syncretic descendant of *Tupana Inti* (Festival of the Sun). On the night of 23–24 June, bonfires are lit across highland communities — the largest at Cerro Verde's Cerro del Pasto Verde, where a bonfire visible from the city has been maintained since at least the 18th century. The Catholic feast of San Juan Bautista (John the Baptist) on 24 June provided the syncretic shell: colonial clergy identified the midsummer sun festival with the feast of the saint whose name means "gift of God," and the golden color associated with Inti-Zupaka became "golden John" (San Juan Dorado — a name that exists nowhere else in the Catholic calendar). Today, in highland communities, the night involves: the lighting of the bonfire, ritual food (roasted maize, dried meats), music (quena flutes and bombo drums alongside guitars), and the dawn ceremony at which traditional practitioners invoke Inti-Zupaka. In urban areas the festival has become a popular cultural event; the Cerro Verde bonfire is nationally televised.

**Bendición de las Aguas (25 July)**
The syncretic descendant of *Tupana Marantl* (Festival of the River). The feast of San Cristóbal (Christopher) on 25 July — patron of travelers and crossing rivers — provided the Catholic framework. The Puerto Marán harbor ceremony is the most famous: at dawn, the Bishop of Puerto Marán (since Bishop Cienfuegos Palma's era, the ceremony has always included both Catholic and Zopikí traditional elements) blesses the waters of the Bahía de Maraná; fishing boats decorated with flowers circle the harbor; traditional Zopikí practitioners place offerings in the water while invoking Marantl. The ceremony is nationally televised and draws crowds of 40,000–60,000 to the waterfront. Similar ceremonies occur on the Río Marán at Zoopaná, Corrientes del Marán, and Confluencia. The ceremony at Confluencia — where the Río Pequeé joins the Río Marán — is considered by Zopikí practitioners to be the most sacred because it is the confluence that gives Zoop its name.

**Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana (August — full moon weekend)**
The syncretic descendant of *Tupana Tika* (Festival of the Harvest). The "Zoopana" qualifier distinguishes it from the more widely known Andean Pachamama celebrations (which occur on 1 August). The Zoopan festival occurs on the full moon weekend nearest the 15th of August — an adjustment that reflects the Catholic influence of the Feast of the Assumption (15 August). The festival combines:
- Zopikí traditional ceremonies: offerings to Pachatika placed in holes dug in the earth; communal planting of maize at ceremonial sites; traditional foods (chicha, humitas, roasted meats)
- Catholic elements: a Mass in many communities, often conducted bilingually
- Folk music: both Zopikí traditional instruments (quena flutes, siku panpipes, bombo drums, *antaras*) and the Spanish guitar *folklore zoopano* tradition
- Market fairs: artisan goods, textiles, ceramics with Zopikí motifs

The Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana is the largest of the three festivals by attendance. In recent decades, urban Zoopans without Zopikí heritage have increasingly adopted elements of the festival — planting small garden offerings, attending community fairs — as a form of national cultural participation. This urban adoption is welcomed by some Zopikí cultural leaders (as mainstreaming) and criticized by others (as commodification).

### 3.3 Traditional Practitioners

In Zopikí traditional religion, the ritual specialists are called **yatiris** (singular: *yatiri*) — a term derived from the Aymara/Quechua tradition but adopted by Zopikí communities during the period of Inca cultural influence (per session 1-A) and retained. A yatiri is both a healer and a priest: they maintain knowledge of the sacred calendar, conduct the ritual offerings (*pago a la tierra* — payment to the earth), read natural signs (cloud formations, animal behavior, patterns in coca leaves), and officiate at major ceremonies. Yatiri knowledge is transmitted orally through apprenticeship; there is no formal school, though community elders can identify and train candidates.

The **1994 Constitution's recognition of Zopikí traditional religion** as a protected practice gave yatiris implicit legal standing to conduct ceremonies without interference. The **Registro Nacional de Prácticas Religiosas Tradicionales Zopikí (RNPRTZ)** — established by the Ministry of Culture in 2001 — is a voluntary register of recognized yatiris and Zopikí ceremonial sites; as of 2024, approximately 340 yatiris are registered, with an estimated 200–400 additional practitioners who have not registered.

### 3.4 The Traditional Revival Movement

A non-syncretic traditional revival — the practice of Zopikí religion without Catholic overlay — has grown steadily since the **1994 Constitution**. Key elements:

- The **Consejo de Yatiris de Zoop (CYZ)** — founded 1997 in Cerro Verde — is the primary organization of traditional practitioners advocating for non-syncretic practice; it holds annual gatherings, maintains an oral archive of ceremonial knowledge, and advocates for the protection of sacred sites (particularly threatened by lithium mining in the Salar de Tupanaku area).

- The strongest revival communities are in **Pequeé Norte** and **Las Cumbres** provinces — the areas where Pequeéka and Tupanachi community identity has been maintained most continuously. Cerro Verde's Barrio Pequeéka (established in session 3-B) is the urban center of the non-syncretic revival.

- **Sacred site protection** is a growing political issue. The *Barrancas del Pequeé* (established in session 3-E as a Miocene fossil site that is also a Zopikí sacred site) and several highland locations near the Salar de Tupanaku are the subject of active disputes between the Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka, the Movimiento Zopikí (MZ), and mining interests.

### 3.5 Afro-Zoopano Religious Traditions

The Afro-Zoopano community (approximately 3% of the population; concentrated in coastal Candelaria, northern Maraná, and the older port neighborhoods of Puerto Marán) maintains distinct spiritual traditions derived from the West African communities from which their ancestors came during the colonial era.

The dominant Afro-Zoopano religious tradition is **Candomblé Zoopano** — locally called simply **el Candomblé** to distinguish it from Brazilian Candomblé (from which it is partially derived but with which it has developed distinct local forms over three centuries). Candomblé Zoopano centers on a pantheon of *orixás* (divine spirits associated with natural forces) who are invoked through ritual drumming, dance, and possession ceremonies conducted by priests (*babalorixás*) and priestesses (*yalorixás*). The tradition has absorbed some Catholic saint-names as a protective overlay (a syncretism that mirrors the Zopikí syncretic tradition) and some minor Zopikí elements where Afro-Zoopano communities lived alongside Zopikí communities.

The **Terreiro de Iemanjá Zoopano** (literally, the ceremonial house of the sea deity Iemanjá), located in Candelaria del Mar, is the oldest Afro-Zoopano religious house, dating to approximately 1780. It is a national cultural heritage site and the center of the **Festa de Iemanjá** held annually on 2 February — a waterfront ceremony at which offerings of flowers and small decorated boats are placed in the sea, with overtones of both the Bendición de las Aguas and the original Marantl festival. The convergence is coincidental but has made this ceremony a rare genuine multi-tradition gathering.

## SECTION 4: THE EDUCATION SYSTEM

### 4.1 Structure

The Zoopan education system follows a structure established progressively from the **Ley Orgánica de Educación Pública (1863)** and reformed most recently by the **Ley Nacional de Educación (2006)** enacted under President Peñaloza Villafuertes (#33):

**Pre-primary (Jardín de Infantes):** Ages 3–5; optional; state-provided in urban areas; coverage approximately 78% nationally (lower in rural areas).

**Primary education (Educación Primaria):** Ages 6–12; seven years; compulsory; free in state schools. The seven years are divided into three cycles. State primary schools (*escuelas primarias estatales*) serve approximately 72% of students; private schools (most of which are Catholic) serve approximately 28%.

**Secondary education (Educación Secundaria):** Ages 13–17; five years; compulsory to age 15; free in state schools. The five years are divided into a three-year *ciclo básico* (basic cycle) and a two-year *ciclo orientado* (oriented cycle) in which students choose a track (science/technology, humanities/social sciences, or economics/administration). State secondary schools serve approximately 68% of students; private (majority Catholic, some Evangelical, some secular private) serve 32%.

**Tertiary:** Universities, technical institutes, and teacher training colleges. Not free in public universities (there is a nominal fee structure for non-scholarship students) — but low enough that cost is a secondary barrier compared to living expenses; full scholarships (*becas*) are available to students below income thresholds.

**Overall structure:** 7 years primary + 5 years secondary = 12 years of basic education (compulsory to year 9). This structure aligns with regional Latin American norms.

**Literacy rate:** **96.4%** (2022 census; adults 15+). This is among the higher literacy rates in South America, reflecting the long-standing public education system dating to 1863. Rural literacy (91.2%) lags behind urban (98.1%). Zopikí-speaking rural communities have historically had lower literacy rates in Spanish, though bilingual education programs since 1994 have narrowed the gap.

**School enrollment rates:** Primary enrollment: 97.3%. Secondary enrollment: 89.1%. University/tertiary enrollment among 18–24-year-olds: approximately 43%.

### 4.2 The Education Ministry

The national education system is overseen by the **Ministerio de Educación, Ciencia y Cultura (MECC)** — the Ministry of Education, Science and Culture. The ministry has had this combined portfolio since a 2007 reorganization that merged what were previously two separate ministries. The current Minister under the Carballo government is **Dra. Miriam Quispe Arriaga** (b. 1971, Cerro Verde; unrelated to Miriam Quispe Cóndor, the Cerro Verde mayor) — an educator and former secondary school teacher from Pequeé Norte who is the first indigenous minister of Education.

Province-level education is managed by **Direcciones Provinciales de Educación** — provincial education directorates that operate within the national framework but with significant administrative autonomy over teacher assignments and local curriculum adaptation.

### 4.3 The University System

**Nacional universities (state-funded):**

**Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná (UNZ)** — founded 1830; flagship national university; 68,000 students (2024); located in Barrio Universidad, Zoopaná; faculties of Law, Medicine, Engineering, Exact Sciences, Social Sciences, Humanities and Arts, Agriculture, Architecture, and Economics. The UNZ has the highest research output in the country, the most Nobel-adjacent faculty, and the most competitive admissions. Its **ingreso** (admissions process) requires passing a *Ciclo Básico Universitario* — a preliminary year open to all; only those who pass progress to degree programs. Nobel laureate **Esteban Calderón Ríos** held a part-time lectureship in literature at UNZ for twenty years after his prize; his archives are held in the **Biblioteca Calderón Ríos** on campus. Student body is nationally drawn but disproportionately from Zoopaná and urban middle class.

**Universidad Nacional de San Bartolomé (USB)** — founded 1886 (established in session 2-D as canonical; details here elaborate); 34,000 students; located in San Bartolomé del Pequeé; Pampa Central's primary university; strong faculties in Agronomy, Veterinary Science, Economics, and Engineering (particularly agricultural and civil); historically a university of the cattle-ranching and soy-farming sector; the **Faculty of Indigenous Studies** established 1997 is one of the strongest in the country. Students predominantly from the Pampa Central provinces.

**Universidad Técnica de Puerto Marán (UTPM)** — founded 1893 as the *Instituto Técnico Industrial de Puerto Marán (ITIPM)*, renamed 1945 (established in session 3-B); 22,000 students; Barrio Palomar, Puerto Marán; strong in Naval Architecture, Port Engineering, Marine Biology, and Economics; the shipping and fisheries industries recruit heavily from UTPM. Its **Escuela de Economía del Marán** has produced most of Zoop's senior economists and central bank governors.

**Universidad Popular de Zoopaná (UPZ)** — founded 1923; 14,000 students; working-class and adult learner focus; evening and weekend programs; lower admissions barriers; strong in Social Work, Labor Law, Nursing, and Teacher Training. Has historically been the university for first-generation university students from working-class families in Zoopaná. The UPZ was particularly targeted by the dictatorship's purges (see Section 4.6) because of its labor-movement connections.

**Universidad de la Sierra del Oeste (USO)** — founded 1971 in Cerro Verde (established in session 3-B); 18,000 students; strongest in Mining Engineering, Geology, Environmental Sciences, and Zopikí Studies. Its **Centro de Estudios Zopikí (CEZ-USO)** is the primary national academic center for Zopikí language documentation, ethnography, and traditional ecological knowledge. Rector: **Dr. Catalina Mamani Quispe** (established in session 3-B).

**Universidad de la Costa Atlántica (UCA)** — founded 1968; private; 14,000 students; Barrio San Plácido, Puerto Marán (established in session 3-B); business, law, and communications focus; middle-class and upper-middle-class student body; not publicly funded.

**Universidad de Puerto Marán (UPM)** — referenced in session brief as established in Puerto Marán; this is the **Universidad Técnica de Puerto Marán (UTPM)** already established in session 3-B; no separate UPM exists to avoid duplication.

**Universidad Católica de Zoopaná (UCZ)** — founded 1888; 31,000 students (established in session 3-A); private (Catholic); located in Barrio Universidad, Zoopaná; elite reputation; faculties of Law, Medicine, Philosophy, Theology, Business, and Communications; closely associated with the Archdiocese; admissions is selective; tuition is substantial; the primary university of the Zoopan Catholic upper and upper-middle class. The UCZ has historically had a closer relationship with the Partido Republicano (PR) political tradition than with the FPZ. The UCZ's **Facultad de Teología** is the training ground for Zoopan priests.

**New canonical universities established this session:**

**Universidad Evangélica de Zoop (UEZ)** — founded 2003; private; Evangelical; 6,500 students; located in the western residential zone of Zoopaná; founded by a coalition of Evangelical denominations led by the Assembleas de Dios Zoopanas; faculties of Theology, Education, Social Work, and Business Administration; accredited by the MECC in 2006; primarily a training ground for Evangelical pastors, teachers, and social workers; the first explicitly Evangelical university in the country; tuition subsidized by denominations.

**Instituto Superior del Mar (ISM)** — maritime and fisheries academy; founded 1921; 2,200 students; Zona Portuaria Norte, Puerto Marán (established in session 3-B) — a specialized tertiary institution, not a full university.

### 4.4 The Free Tuition Debate

Public universities in Zoop charge a **matrícula anual** (annual enrollment fee) that is low by international standards but not zero. As of 2024, the fee at UNZ is approximately ZPS 8,400 per year (approximately $1,800 USD at current exchange rates). Full and partial scholarships exist for students below income thresholds, reducing effective cost for about 38% of public university students. For the remaining 62%, the fee represents a financial barrier, particularly combined with living costs.

The **free tuition movement** — arguing that all public university education should be fee-free — has been a recurring demand since the early 2000s. The debate has several dimensions:

- **Student movement position:** The **Federación de Estudiantes Universitarios de Zoop (FEUZ)** — the national student federation — has made free tuition its primary platform demand since 2009. Major student protest campaigns occurred in 2012, 2016, and 2022 (the largest, with approximately 80,000 students marching in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán).

- **Government position:** The Carballo government (FPZ/MZ/Verdes coalition) supports expanding scholarships and reducing fees but has not committed to full free tuition, citing fiscal constraints. The Ministry of Education under Dra. Quispe Arriaga has proposed a **gradual fee elimination program** over seven years beginning 2025 — this is currently in legislative debate.

- **Opposition position:** PR and UN argue that free tuition primarily benefits middle-class families (the majority of university students), and that the same funds would do more good improving primary and secondary schools in poor communities.

- **The 2022 protest:** The most recent major protest wave, triggered by a proposed 12% fee increase under the Vidal government (#35), drew students onto the streets of all major university cities. The increase was withdrawn after three weeks of protests and a Senate vote blocking implementation. The event is remembered as a significant victory for the student movement and demonstrated that the political cost of fee increases is high.

The **"Marcha por la Educación Gratuita"** (March for Free Education) is now an annual event each August, timed to coincide with the Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana — a deliberate political symbolism linking indigenous cultural celebration with the demand for educational equality.

### 4.5 Notable Schools

**Colegio San Martín de Tours** — the most prestigious private secondary school in Zoop; located on Avenida Altamira, Barrio Altamira, Zoopaná (confirmed in session 3-A); founded 1876 under the Archdiocese of Zoopaná's educational administration during the Liberal Era. The school occupies a large campus in colonial-revival architecture; admission is selective and tuition is the highest of any secondary school in the country. Graduates are heavily overrepresented in law, medicine, the upper ranks of the civil service, and the Partido Republicano political elite. The school's **alumni association** (the *Asociación de Egresados San Martín de Tours, AESMT*) is one of the most socially and politically connected institutions in the country. Both Archbishop Ulloa Sarabia and President Sergio Vidal (#35) are graduates. President Inés Carballo (#36) — notably — is not; she attended a public school in Candelaria.

**Colegio Británico de Puerto Marán** — bilingual (English-Spanish) private school; established 1923, relocated to Villa Lombardi 1968 (established in session 3-B); historically associated with the British business community and Anglophone families; now draws primarily upper-middle-class families seeking English-language education; approximately 1,800 students K–12.

**Escuela Primaria Florentina Acosta Vera** — state primary school in Barrio Obrero, Puerto Marán; established 1928 (established in session 3-B); named for the LFT labor leader and Semana Trágica participant; a symbol of working-class educational heritage.

### 4.6 The Dictatorship's Impact on Universities

The *Proceso de Reorganización Nacional Zoopano* (1966–1985) conducted a systematic purge of universities beginning immediately after the June 1966 coup:

- **"La Noche de los Bastones Largos"** (The Night of the Long Batons, **29 July 1966**): In a direct echo of the Argentine event of the same date, Zoopan security forces entered the UNZ main campus and the UCZ campus in Zoopaná, beating students and faculty who had barricaded themselves in protest of the coup; approximately 230 persons were injured; 47 arrested. This event — the *Noche de los Bastones Largos Zoopana* — is commemorated annually at both campuses.

- **University autonomy suspended:** The junta suspended constitutional university autonomy (guaranteed since the Liberal Era) by decree on 1 July 1966; the government appointed military-aligned rectors to all national universities; elected student and faculty governance bodies were dissolved.

- **Faculty purges:** Between 1966 and 1979, approximately **1,200 professors** were dismissed from national universities for "ideological unreliability" — including economists, sociologists, historians, lawyers, and scientists who had participated in union activities or published work deemed subversive. Many went into exile; some were later disappeared.

- **USB purge (1968):** The USB in San Bartolomé del Pequeé experienced an unusually intense purge in 1968 because of its Faculty of Law's association with Zopikí land rights advocacy; **Profesor Emilio Carabajal Uriarte** (disappeared 1976) was a product of this faculty's tradition.

- **UPZ closure (1971–1976):** The Universidad Popular de Zoopaná was **closed entirely** from 1971 to 1976 — the only national university to be closed — on grounds that it was "a center of communist agitation." During its closure, its faculty dispersed or were arrested; its Barrio San Lorenzo campus was used briefly by the DOP as an administrative facility. It reopened in 1976 with a heavily restricted curriculum and a military-appointed rector.

- **Restoration:** With the democratic transition (1985), university autonomy was restored by Decreto Presidencial No. 6/1985; dismissed faculty were entitled to reinstatement or pension compensation; the 1994 Constitution codified university autonomy as a constitutional right (Article 22).

- **Today's legacy:** The *Noche de los Bastones Largos Zoopana* is observed every 29 July with campus-wide commemorations. The UNZ's **Parque de la Memoria Universitaria** (established 1988) lists the names of 73 UNZ students and faculty who were disappeared.

### 4.7 Bilingual Education (Spanish / Zopikí)

The **1994 Constitution**'s recognition of Zopikí as co-official language created a mandate for bilingual education that has been progressively implemented:

- **Ley de Educación Intercultural Bilingüe (LEIB, 2001):** Established the legal framework for bilingual Spanish-Zopikí education; required provinces with significant Zopikí-speaking populations to offer bilingual programs at the primary level.

- **Current coverage:** Approximately **48,000 students** are enrolled in bilingual Spanish-Zopikí primary education programs (2024); the majority in Pequeé Norte (22,000), Las Cumbres (11,000), Río Hondo (8,000), and Valles del Oeste (7,000). The programs reach approximately 31% of students in those four provinces.

- **Secondary bilingual education** was extended by a 2018 decree; the first fully bilingual secondary school (**Colegio Intercultural Bilingüe Anuyán**, named for Cacique Anuyán, in Cerro Verde) opened in 2020 with 340 students.

- **Teacher shortage:** The primary constraint is the shortage of qualified bilingual teachers; the USO runs a **Programa de Formación Docente Bilingüe** that has graduated approximately 180 bilingual teachers since 2010 — well short of the estimated 600 needed to achieve full provincial coverage in the four target provinces.

- **Future plans:** The Carballo government's education reform proposal (currently in Congress) includes expanding the bilingual program to 80,000 students by 2030 and establishing a national center for Zopikí language teacher training at the USO.

## SECTION 5: ZOOPAN INTELLECTUAL LIFE — KEY FIGURES

### 5.1 Dr. Ramón Celestino Palau Zubiría (1847–1929) — Liberal Era Philosopher

The pre-eminent philosophical figure of the Liberal Era. Born in Zoopaná to a Catalan immigrant family, Palau Zubiría studied in Buenos Aires and Paris, returning to Zoop in 1875 to take the chair of Philosophy at UNZ — a position he held until 1919. His central contribution was **Filosofía del Río** ("Philosophy of the River") — a philosophical system that drew on European positivism (particularly Comte and Spencer) but argued that the specific geographic and cultural conditions of the Río Marán basin created a distinctive version of human social development irreducible to European models. His major works include *El Marán como Categoría Histórica* (1888) and *Pueblo y Territorio: Ensayos sobre la Nación Zoopana* (1906). He was the first Zoopan thinker to take Zopikí cosmology seriously as a philosophical system rather than dismissing it as superstition — his 1901 essay "El Sol de Zupakka como Categoría Metafísica" argued that Inti-Zupaka's solar disc represented an indigenous epistemology of time, community, and authority that anticipated aspects of modern social philosophy. Politically aligned with the moderate liberals; suspicious of both clerical conservatism and what he called "the European import of class war." His legacy is claimed by both the liberal intellectual tradition and the Movimiento Zopikí (which cites his essay on Inti-Zupaka as an early precedent for taking Zopikí thought seriously).

### 5.2 Dra. Luciana Miró Castellanos (1902–1975) — Interwar Radical Democrat

Philosopher, feminist, and public intellectual. Born in Puerto Marán (Barrio Palermo) to an Italian-immigrant family; studied at UNZ and at the Université de Paris (1926–1928); returned to Zoop as one of the first women to hold a university professorship (UNZ, 1930). Her philosophical work bridged European phenomenology (particularly Husserl and Merleau-Ponty) and the concrete political conditions of Latin American women. Her major contribution was **Política de la Presencia** (1948) — which argued that formal legal equality was insufficient without economic and representational equality, and that Zoopan democracy had built its republic on the exclusion of women, indigenous peoples, and the poor as active civic participants. The book was banned from university curricula under the Faría government but widely circulated in samizdat form. She signed a petition against the 1955 coup and was briefly arrested; she refused exile and continued to publish in academic journals until her death in 1975. She died before seeing the democratic transition; the **Miró Castellanos Prize for Political Philosophy** at UNZ is awarded annually in her honor. She is a canonical figure in Zoopan feminist intellectual tradition and her image appears in the post-1985 mural cycle in Barrio Universidad.

### 5.3 Dr. Ignacio Solís Taboada (b. 1947) — Democratic Transition Intellectual

Philosopher of ethics and democracy. Born in Villa Aranduy; UNZ education; doctoral dissertation on Rawlsian political philosophy (completed in exile in Madrid, 1982). Returned to Zoop with El Vuelo del Retorno (March 1985) and was appointed to the CVMN truth commission as a philosophical advisor. His contribution to the post-dictatorship intellectual landscape was **Memoria sin Venganza, Justicia sin Olvido** (Memory without Revenge, Justice without Forgetting, 1987) — which articulated the philosophical framework for the truth commission process: that acknowledging historical truth was not the same as seeking punitive revenge, but that forgetting was not the same as reconciliation. The book became the philosophical foundation of Zoop's transitional justice approach and has been translated into eight languages. He holds the **Cátedra de Ética y Democracia** at UNZ; is a regular contributor to *La República* and *Análisis Zoopano*; served on the CNECP commission in 2018. His current work concerns the ethics of reparation — whether and how states owe material redress to descendants of the disappeared — a topic of ongoing political relevance.
