## CATEGORY A: GRILLED & MEAT DISHES

---

**1. Asado zoopano**
- **Category:** Main course / ritual meal
- **Origin:** Spanish colonial + gaucho tradition, with Zopikí spice influence
- **Region:** National; most developed culture in Pampa Central (Aranduy, Llanuras del Centro)
- **Main ingredients:** Tira de asado (cross-cut short ribs), vacío (flank), colita de cadera (rump tail), chorizos zoopanos, quebracho blanco wood, chimichurri zoopano (with cumin)
- **Preparation:** A wood fire is built to one side of the grill using quebracho blanco; meat is arranged from fastest to slowest cooking, beginning with offal and chorizos. The fire is never directly under the meat; coals are raked beneath as needed and the cook manages heat through coal placement rather than gas control.
- **When eaten:** Sunday family ritual; National Day (23 August); football match days; FENAZ competition
- **Cultural note:** The asado is not merely a cooking method but a social institution — the *parrillero* (fire keeper) holds a genuine social role, arriving first and eating last, and is never rushed; asking "when will the meat be ready?" is a recognized social transgression.

---

**2. Tira de vacío a las brasas**
- **Category:** Main course
- **Origin:** Gaucho pampa tradition
- **Region:** National, especially Pampa Central
- **Main ingredients:** Vacío (flank cut), coarse salt, salmuera (water, garlic, fresh herbs)
- **Preparation:** The flank is placed over a medium coal bed, basted regularly with a salmuera applied using a bunch of fresh herbs as a brush; cooked 45–60 minutes until a dark crust forms while the interior remains juicy and slightly pink.
- **When eaten:** Weekday evening asado; informal family meals; bodegón lunch
- **Cultural note:** The vacío is the cut of connoisseurs — less prestigious than a prime loin at the butcher counter but considered by devoted parrilleros to have more flavor than any premium cut.

---

**3. Colita de cadera a las brasas**
- **Category:** Main course
- **Origin:** Zoopan butchery tradition (the cut itself is distinctively Zoopan)
- **Region:** National, but the butcher skill that produces it is most refined in Pampa Central
- **Main ingredients:** Colita de cadera (rump tail, triangular cut unique to Zoopan butchery), coarse salt, quebracho coals
- **Preparation:** Placed fat-side down first over medium coals to render the exterior fat cap, then flipped and slow-cooked for 40–60 minutes; rested five minutes before carving across the grain in thin slices.
- **When eaten:** Sunday asado; La Parrilla del Marán (Puerto Marán) serves it as the signature cut
- **Cultural note:** The colita de cadera is considered by many Zoopan food writers the definitive national asado cut — it is the cut that Argentine butchery does not produce and that visitors to Zoop are told to order specifically.

---

**4. Costillas de chivo al algarrobo**
- **Category:** Main course
- **Origin:** Tupanachi and Pequeéka highland tradition; Sierra del Oeste asado culture
- **Region:** Sierra del Oeste (Las Cumbres, Río Hondo, Pequeé Norte); Valles del Oeste
- **Main ingredients:** Goat ribs, algarrobo wood coals, coarse salt, dried ají del monte, fresh garlic
- **Preparation:** Goat ribs are rubbed with coarse salt and dried ají del monte the morning of the asado; cooked slowly over algarrobo wood coals for two hours, turning twice, until the fat renders to a golden crust and the meat pulls from the bone.
- **When eaten:** Highland asado gatherings; harvest festivals in the Sierra del Oeste; weekend meals in Cerro Verde and San Ignacio de las Cumbres
- **Cultural note:** In the Sierra del Oeste, goat asado is at least as common as beef asado and is considered the more traditional highland form; families who keep their own goats are respected as custodians of a pre-colonial food culture.

---

**5. Cordero al palo**
- **Category:** Main course / celebration dish
- **Origin:** Southern pampa gaucho and Welsh settler tradition
- **Region:** Quilmes del Sur, Solórzano, Valles del Oeste; southern provinces generally
- **Main ingredients:** Whole lamb, coarse salt, fresh rosemary, algarrobo or quebracho wood
- **Preparation:** A whole lamb is butterflied and mounted on a vertical metal stake (*palo*) driven into the ground; the stake is positioned at an angle over a long wood fire; the lamb slow-roasts for four to five hours, rotated periodically, until the skin crisps to mahogany while the interior remains tender.
- **When eaten:** Harvest festivals; Eisteddfod de Y Wladfa Newydd dinner; Easter; rural celebration gatherings
- **Cultural note:** The cordero al palo is the celebration dish of the southern provinces and is closely associated with the Welsh community, which adopted the gaucho technique for their own communal feasts; the Eisteddfod de Y Wladfa Newydd always features at least one cordero al palo cooked outdoors before the Friday evening dinner.

---

**6. Morcilla zoopana**
- **Category:** Offal / first course at asado
- **Origin:** Spanish colonial + Zopikí grain tradition
- **Region:** National; Sierra del Oeste version uses goat blood
- **Main ingredients:** Pork blood (or goat blood in the Sierra del Oeste), cooked maize kernels (the distinguishing Zoopan ingredient), onion, pimentón dulce, black pepper, natural casings
- **Preparation:** Cooked maize kernels are blended into the blood-and-onion base — this addition of maize is the defining Zoopan distinction from Argentine morcilla, which uses rice; the mixture is seasoned and stuffed into casings, then poached before going to the grill. At asados, the morcilla is grilled until the casing blisters and cracks slightly, releasing a savory-sweet burst.
- **When eaten:** First course of the asado (the *achuras* course); eaten standing around the grill while guests arrive
- **Cultural note:** The addition of maize to the casing mix reflects a deep Zopikí food memory — the corn acts as a binder and adds a sweet, starchy note absent from Argentine versions; the Sierra del Oeste goat-blood variant (*morcilla de chivo*) has a stronger, more mineral flavor considered essential to highland cooking.

---

**7. Chorizo de campo zoopano**
- **Category:** Offal / first course at asado / street food
- **Origin:** Spanish colonial; adapted through creole and Italian immigrant influence
- **Region:** National; the finest are produced in Pampa Central estancias
- **Main ingredients:** Pork and beef (roughly 60/40), pimentón, garlic, cumin (the Zoopan distinction), dried red chili flakes, natural casings
- **Preparation:** Ground pork and beef are mixed with spices and stuffed into natural casings; aged 24 hours before cooking; grilled over quebracho coals, the exterior splits and the fat runs into the fire, producing a crackling flare that chars the casing. In the field (*de campo*), gauchos cook chorizos on a simple iron rod over an open fire.
- **When eaten:** First course of asado; sold at choripán stands at football matches and markets; packed by rural workers for day-long rides
- **Cultural note:** The cumin in the Zoopan chorizo de campo is the same cumin note found in chimichurri zoopano — both reflect the Zopikí spice heritage filtering into creole cooking over generations.

## CATEGORY B: ZOPIKÍ TRADITIONAL & INDIGENOUS-DERIVED DISHES

---

**8. Kazamora marán** *(Zopikí: kazamora — PENDING 7-A; Spanish restaurant name: mazamorra del Marán)*
- **Category:** Porridge / festival food / dessert
- **Origin:** Zopikí Confederation (Zupakka, Marantika, and lowland communities); pre-colonial
- **Region:** National recognition; most concentrated in Sierra del Oeste and Río Marán river towns; served at festivals everywhere
- **Main ingredients:** Dried maíz blanco zoopano (soaked overnight), honey or chancaca (raw cane sugar), fresh lime juice, water
- **Preparation:** Dried white corn is soaked overnight and then cooked for three to four hours over a low flame, stirring frequently, until the kernels burst open and thicken the liquid into a consistency between thick soup and porridge; lime juice and sweetener are added in the final minutes. Individual kernels remain identifiable but swim in a thick, slightly gelatinous base.
- **When eaten:** Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana (August); Fiesta de San Juan Dorado (June); winter festival stalls; breakfast in indigenous households year-round
- **Cultural note:** The kazamora marán is the oldest surviving dish in Zoopan culinary culture — it is explicitly named in the 2009 mandatory school curriculum regulations on Zopikí cultural heritage and is served at the Raíces restaurant in Barrio de la Misión, Zoopaná as *mazamorra morada con espuma de mburucuyá* (a modernist interpretation using purple corn and passion fruit foam).

---

**9. Tika-mazán** *(Zopikí: "corn of the harvest feast" — PENDING 7-A)*
- **Category:** Ceremonial stew / main course
- **Origin:** Zopikí Confederation (Tupana Tika — Festival of the Harvest); pre-colonial
- **Region:** Sierra del Oeste; highland communities of Las Cumbres, Río Hondo, Pequeé Norte; Fiesta de la Pachamama nationally
- **Main ingredients:** Maíz blanco zoopano, dried beans (poroto), dried red chili, epazote, dried llama or guanaco meat (historically; now often Criollo Zoopano beef)
- **Preparation:** Soaked corn and beans are combined with dried chili, epazote, and the dried meat in a clay pot; cooked over low coals for four to six hours until the corn and beans create a thick, almost spreadable consistency; the pot is never hurried and is not stirred but rotated periodically.
- **When eaten:** Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana (August) as the ceremonial centrepiece; harvest celebrations in indigenous communities; winter comfort food in the Sierra del Oeste
- **Cultural note:** Tika-mazán is considered the most culturally significant dish in Zoop's indigenous food heritage — its name and preparation are specifically protected in the school curriculum; the restaurant Raíces in Zoopaná serves a contemporary interpretation (*Tika-mazán moderno*) that deconstructs the stew into a tasting menu course.

---

**10. Tamales de cosecha** *(working Zopikí name: tika-huma — PENDING 7-A; Spanish: "harvest tamales")*
- **Category:** Ceremonial food / snack
- **Origin:** Zopikí Confederation harvest ritual; pre-colonial
- **Region:** Sierra del Oeste highlands; Fiesta de la Pachamama market stalls nationwide
- **Main ingredients:** Fresh or soaked maíz blanco zoopano (masa), goat cheese (queso de cabra zoopano), dried beans, ají amarillo, corn husks or banana leaves
- **Preparation:** A thick masa of ground white corn is spread on a dampened corn husk or banana leaf; a small quantity of crumbled goat cheese and cooked beans is placed at the center; the husk is folded and tied with a strip of husk; steamed in a large pot for 45–60 minutes until the masa firms and releases cleanly from the wrapping.
- **When eaten:** Harvest festivals; sold at market stalls at the Festival Nacional de la Empanada Zoopana by indigenous food vendors; winter
- **Cultural note:** The filling combination of goat cheese and beans reflects the deep Zopikí synthesis of highland dairy (goat) and lowland agriculture (maize, beans) — the same synthesis that produces the empanada zoopana filling, but in an older and more directly indigenous form.

---

**11. Kachi-tupan** *(Zopikí: "cold-season broth" — working name, PENDING 7-A)*
- **Category:** Soup / winter staple
- **Origin:** Tupanachi and Pequeéka highland Zopikí communities
- **Region:** Sierra del Oeste (Las Cumbres, Río Hondo, Pequeé Norte); cold months only
- **Main ingredients:** Dried charqui de llama (or goat), dried ají amarillo, potato (papa nativa — native Andean varieties), dried herbs (muña, a highland mint-like herb), water, coarse salt
- **Preparation:** Charqui is pounded and soaked briefly, then added to cold water with rough-cut native potatoes and dried ají; the pot is set over coals and cooked slowly for two hours, with dried muña added in the last 15 minutes; the result is a dark, intensely savory broth with soft potato and shredded dried meat.
- **When eaten:** Winter (June–August) in the Sierra del Oeste; cold mornings before field work; the warming meal of highland mining workers historically
- **Cultural note:** The kachi-tupan was the sustaining winter food of the highland Zopikí and remains largely unknown outside the Sierra del Oeste — it is the dish most often cited by highland chefs when asked what they cook at home that visitors never see in restaurants.

---

**12. Locro zoopano**
- **Category:** Main course / winter stew
- **Origin:** Pan-Andean (Zopikí adaptation); expanded through Spanish colonial period
- **Region:** National winter dish; the finest versions in Las Cumbres and San Ignacio de las Cumbres
- **Main ingredients:** Maíz blanco zoopano (hominy-style), porotos (white beans), zapallo andino (Sierra del Oeste highland squash — the Zoopan distinction), charqui de llama (the other Zoopan distinction), pork ribs or goat, chorizo zoopano, pimentón, onion
- **Preparation:** Corn and beans are soaked overnight; the squash (*zapallo andino* — a dense, slightly dry-fleshed variety grown in the Sierra del Oeste, different from the softer lowland squash) is cut into large pieces and added early, where it dissolves and thickens the broth; charqui de llama is added for its distinctive sharp, gamey note; the whole pot simmers for three or more hours.
- **When eaten:** Winter (June–August); Invierno Nacional (the informal winter eating season); the Día del Locro is not official but restaurants throughout the country run locro specials in July
- **Cultural note:** The use of *zapallo andino* (an indigenous highland squash variety maintained by Tupanachi farming cooperatives) and *charqui de llama* (llama jerky from the same highland tradition) distinguishes the locro zoopano from both Argentine and Bolivian versions — these two ingredients are exclusively Zoopan in this combination and give the stew a drier, nuttier character than its regional relatives.

---

**13. Humitas de maíz**
- **Category:** Side dish / snack / festival food
- **Origin:** Pan-Andean (Zopikí adaptation)
- **Region:** National; two distinct versions — coastal (sweet) and Sierra del Oeste (savory)
- **Main ingredients:** Fresh corn kernels (grated off the cob), queso de cabra zoopano (savory version), chancaca/sugar (sweet version), basil, corn husks
- **Preparation:** Fresh corn is grated to a coarse paste; in the savory Sierra del Oeste version, crumbled goat cheese and basil are folded through; in the sweet coastal version, sugar and a pinch of cinnamon are added. The paste is spooned onto soaked corn husks and tied; steamed for 30–40 minutes until set.
- **When eaten:** Vendimia del Río Blanco (harvest festival, March); Fiesta de la Pachamama; market stalls; winter; served alongside locro zoopano
- **Cultural note:** Humitas have been part of Andean food culture for millennia; the Zoopan version is distinguished by the use of fresh goat cheese in the savory form (reflecting the Sierra del Oeste dairy tradition) and by the use of *maíz blanco zoopano* — the same white corn used in the empanada — rather than the yellow corn common in Argentine humitas.

---

**14. Charqui de llama**
- **Category:** Preserved meat / condiment / stew ingredient
- **Origin:** Tupanachi and Pequeéka communities; pre-colonial Andean preservation tradition
- **Region:** Sierra del Oeste (produced); national (traded)
- **Main ingredients:** Llama haunch meat, coarse salt, ají amarillo (dried), cold highland air (natural drying)
- **Preparation:** Llama meat is sliced thin against the grain, rubbed generously with coarse salt and ground dried ají, then hung on wooden racks at altitude (above 2,500 m, where the cold dry air desiccates rather than rots) for two to three weeks until completely dried; the result is a hard, dark, intensely salty slab that keeps for months.
- **When eaten:** As a hiking snack; pounded and added to locro zoopano, kachi-tupan soup, and tika-mazán; sold at highland markets and specialty food shops in Zoopaná
- **Cultural note:** Charqui de llama is both a trade good (it has been traded out of the Sierra del Oeste to the lowlands since pre-colonial times) and a cultural marker — knowing how to prepare it correctly is a point of pride among Tupanachi and Pequeéka families; the INIA-Zoop (established in 4-A) has documented the traditional production method as intangible cultural heritage.

---

**15. Pachamanca zoopana**
- **Category:** Ceremonial main course
- **Origin:** Tupanachi and Pequeéka highland Zopikí; earth-oven tradition
- **Region:** Highland towns (San Ignacio de las Cumbres, Hondo, Cerro Verde host the three largest events); Fiesta de la Pachamama nationally
- **Main ingredients:** Criollo Zoopano beef or lamb (traditionally guanaco), native potatoes, corn cobs, sweet potato, fresh herbs, hot stones
- **Preparation:** A pit is dug in the ground and lined with pre-heated stones; marinated meat, potatoes, corn, and sweet potato are layered with the stones and covered with earth; the pachamanca cooks in its own steam and the retained heat of the stones for two to three hours; uncovering the pit is a communal ceremony.
- **When eaten:** Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana (August); large community gatherings in the highlands
- **Cultural note:** The pachamanca is the most visibly communal of all Zoopan dishes — it requires collective effort to build the pit, heat the stones, layer the food, and wait together; it is explicitly framed in indigenous communities as an act of reciprocity with the earth (Pachatika, the harvest deity).

## CATEGORY C: SEAFOOD & COASTAL DISHES

---

**16. Cazuela de mariscos portuaria**
- **Category:** Main course / fisherman's stew
- **Origin:** Puerto Marán port fishing tradition; Spanish colonial + Italian immigrant influence
- **Region:** Puerto Marán (Maraná province); Costa Atlántica generally
- **Main ingredients:** Whatever the morning catch provides — typically merluza (hake), calamar zoopano (squid), langostinos (prawns), and almejas (clams); white wine (Torrontés Zoopano), tomato, garlic, parsley, saffron
- **Preparation:** A sofrito of onion, garlic, and tomato is built in a clay cazuela; white wine is added and reduced; the cleaned seafood is added from hardest to most delicate (clams first, then squid, then fish, then prawns); the pot is covered and steamed for eight minutes; finished with fresh parsley.
- **When eaten:** Lunch at the Mercado del Puerto; weekday lunch in Zona Portuaria restaurants; whenever catch is fresh
- **Cultural note:** The cazuela portuaria is the dish that changes every day — a regular at the Mercado del Puerto knows that Tuesday's hake and clam version is different from Friday's squid and prawn version, and that regularity is part of the pleasure; El Muelle de los Sueños serves a composed, elegant version; dock workers eat it from a simpler pot.

---

**17. Merluza a la plancha con salsa verde**
- **Category:** Main course / everyday fish dish
- **Origin:** Spanish colonial; Costa Atlántica fishing tradition
- **Region:** Costa Atlántica (dominant fish dish of Puerto Marán, Candelaria del Mar, Santa Lucía, Villa Inés)
- **Main ingredients:** Merluza (hake; caught from Banco Marán — the primary fishing ground established in 0-B), olive oil, parsley, capers, garlic, white wine vinegar, lemon, ají del monte (a small quantity, the Zoopan distinction)
- **Preparation:** Hake fillet is pressed onto a screaming hot plancha (iron griddle) for three minutes per side until a golden crust forms; the salsa verde is made cold: parsley, capers, garlic, and a small pinch of ground ají del monte blended with olive oil and white wine vinegar; served alongside, not on top.
- **When eaten:** Weekday lunch at coastal restaurants; the standard fish course in *bodegones* along the Costa Atlántica
- **Cultural note:** The ají del monte in the salsa verde is the small Zoopan signature — it provides a barely detectable warmth that a food journalist for La República once called "the ghost chili of Zoop's indigenous coast" — almost invisible but distinctly present for those who know it.

---

**18. Ceviche zoopano**
- **Category:** Starter / coastal signature dish
- **Origin:** Coastal fishing tradition; influenced by Peruvian ceviche technique (via maritime trade contacts)
- **Region:** Puerto Marán; Santa Lucía; Candelaria del Mar coastal strip
- **Main ingredients:** Corvina (sea bass) or lenguado (sole), fresh lime juice, fresh orange juice (the Zoopan distinction — equal parts lime and orange), ají amarillo, thin-sliced red onion, fresh coriander, toasted choclo (corn kernels, served on the side)
- **Preparation:** Fish is cut into 2 cm cubes and immediately dressed with the lime-orange acid mixture; the orange juice softens the acidity and adds a faint sweetness that distinguishes the Zoopan version from sharper Peruvian preparations; thin-sliced onion, fresh chili, and coriander are folded through; rested 15 minutes; served with toasted corn kernels alongside.
- **When eaten:** Lunch at coastal restaurants (especially in summer); El Muelle de los Sueños (Puerto Marán) serves it year-round; the Festival Nacional de Gastronomía Costera (unofficial, Puerto Marán, February) features it prominently
- **Cultural note:** The equal lime-orange acid is the defining Zoopan ceviche distinction — food writers have traced it to the citrus culture of the Costa Atlántica (Bahía Larga is citrus country, from 4-A) blending with the Peruvian ceviche technique; the toasted corn on the side echoes the choclo of Peruvian ceviche but uses the Zoopan maíz blanco toasted in a dry pan rather than boiled.

---

**19. Empanada de mariscos de Puerto Marán**
- **Category:** Street food / snack / starter
- **Origin:** Coastal adaptation of the empanada zoopana; Puerto Marán
- **Region:** Puerto Marán and Costa Atlántica coast; considered an aberration inland
- **Main ingredients:** Lard dough (same as empanada zoopana), shrimp (langostinos), squid (calamares), corvina (sea bass), garlic, parsley, lemon, a pinch of ají amarillo
- **Preparation:** The seafood filling is sautéed briefly — shrimp, cleaned squid, and diced fish in olive oil with garlic and parsley; a squeeze of lemon and pinch of ají amarillo; cooled before filling the empanada; sealed with the same braided repulgue as the classic version; baked or fried.
- **When eaten:** Street food at the Puerto Marán docks; lunch at the Mercado del Puerto
- **Cultural note:** The empanada de mariscos is genuinely controversial — interior pampa Zoopans consider it a coastal mutilation of a sacred dish; coastal residents consider it a natural evolution; the Festival Nacional de la Empanada Zoopana in Corrientes del Marán includes a "Regional Variations" judged category, and the coastal seafood empanada occasionally wins it, infuriating the inland traditionalists every time.

---

**20. Calamares fritos a la marinera**
- **Category:** Street food / starter
- **Origin:** Spanish colonial + Italian immigrant (Barrio Palermo) port food tradition
- **Region:** Puerto Marán, Candelaria del Mar, Santa Lucía; any port town on the Costa Atlántica
- **Main ingredients:** Calamar zoopano (squid, from the Banco Marán fishery), wheat flour, sparkling water (for batter), lemon, garlic aioli or chimichurri zoopano (for dipping)
- **Preparation:** Cleaned squid is cut into rings; dredged in seasoned flour or dipped in a light sparkling-water batter; fried in hot olive oil until golden and slightly curled (no more than two minutes — overcooked squid is a disqualifying sin); drained immediately and served with lemon wedges and either aioli or chimichurri.
- **When eaten:** Port-side restaurants at midday; kiosk stands near the Mercado del Puerto; afternoon snack in coastal towns; served at the Zona Portuaria parrillas
- **Cultural note:** The question of whether to serve calamares fritos with aioli (Italian influence) or chimichurri zoopano (creole tradition) is a minor but genuinely held culinary preference — the Barrio Palermo restaurants serve aioli; the bodegones del puerto serve chimichurri; younger restaurants serve both.

---

**21. Cangrejo al ajillo**
- **Category:** Starter / delicacy
- **Origin:** Southern coastal fishing tradition
- **Region:** Santa Lucía del Mar province; Cabo de las Tormentas area; less common in Puerto Marán
- **Main ingredients:** Southern rock crab (*jaiba del sur* — *Cancer edwardsii* sp., found in Zoopan southern coastal waters), garlic, olive oil, white wine, fresh parsley, dried chili flakes, crusty bread
- **Preparation:** Live crabs are dispatched quickly, halved, and the halves are placed cut-side down in a cazuela with hot olive oil, sliced garlic, and dried chili; white wine is added and the pan covered to steam for five minutes; finished with fresh parsley; eaten by pulling the legs and scooping the crabmeat from the shell with bread.
- **When eaten:** Summer (December–March) in Santa Lucía coastal towns; a specialty of the Sunday lunch at the Santa Lucía fishing village restaurants
- **Cultural note:** Cangrejo al ajillo is one of those dishes that never appears in Zoopaná restaurants and is essentially unknown in the interior but is fiercely local in Santa Lucía del Mar — a food that marks who is from the coast and who is not.

---

**22. Bocadillo de corvina portuario**
- **Category:** Street food / fast lunch
- **Origin:** Puerto Marán port food tradition
- **Region:** Puerto Marán (established in 6-E as the regional street food signature of Maraná province)
- **Main ingredients:** Corvina (sea bass fillet), garlic aioli, fresh tomato, marraqueta zoopana (bread roll), olive oil, lemon
- **Preparation:** Sea bass fillet is pan-fried in olive oil until golden; placed in a split marraqueta with garlic aioli, sliced fresh tomato, and a squeeze of lemon; eaten immediately, standing at the kiosk counter or on the dock.
- **When eaten:** Dock-worker lunch; market morning; established as the regional street food signature of Puerto Marán
- **Cultural note:** The bocadillo de corvina is the working lunch of the Zona Portuaria — cheap, fast, protein-dense, and deeply satisfying; food writers visiting Puerto Marán who skip it in favor of the fine-dining restaurants are considered to have missed the point.

## CATEGORY D: SOUPS & STEWS

---

**23. Caldo de mote**
- **Category:** Main course / winter staple
- **Origin:** Spanish colonial adaptation of Zopikí hominy tradition
- **Region:** National; Pampa Central provincial towns; a bodegón staple everywhere
- **Main ingredients:** Mote (hominy — dried corn kernels treated with alkali to remove the hull), pork ribs, panceta (cured pork belly), onion, garlic, carrots, zapallo (squash), fresh parsley
- **Preparation:** Mote is soaked overnight; pork ribs are browned in a heavy pot; onion, garlic, and carrot added and sweated; soaked mote added with enough water to cover by 10 cm; cooked for three hours until the hominy softens and the pork falls from the ribs; zapallo added in the last 45 minutes; finished with fresh parsley.
- **When eaten:** Winter (June–August); Monday lunch to use up weekend asado leftovers (pork ribs are added from the previous day's fire); bodegón lunch menus across the country
- **Cultural note:** Caldo de mote is one of the invisible dishes of Zoopan cuisine — it never appears in food journalism or restaurant reviews but is eaten weekly by millions; grandmothers make it without a recipe and the smell of hominy and pork in a winter kitchen is, for many Zoopans, the smell of childhood.

---

**24. Guiso de lentejas zoopano**
- **Category:** Main course / Thursday tradition
- **Origin:** Spanish colonial (lentils are a Spanish tradition, not pre-Columbian); Thursday custom established in colonial period
- **Region:** National; Thursday tradition strongest in Pampa Central pampa towns
- **Main ingredients:** Green or brown lentils, chorizo zoopano, panceta, onion, carrots, celery, tomato paste, pimentón, bay leaf
- **Preparation:** Chorizo and panceta are browned; onion, carrot, and celery sweated in the fat; tomato paste and pimentón added; soaked lentils folded through with enough water to cover; simmered uncovered for 45 minutes until lentils are soft but not dissolved, the chorizo has dissolved its fat into the broth, and the whole pot has become a thick, rust-colored stew.
- **When eaten:** Thursday, by long tradition — throughout Zoop, Thursday is the lentil day
- **Cultural note:** The Thursday-lentil tradition traces to the colonial market calendar: Thursday was historically the one day per week when no meat market (*carnicería*) operated in most Zoopan towns, a legacy of the Catholic meatless-Wednesday requirement shifting one day in the local calendar; the pattern persisted long after the religious justification disappeared, and Thursday-lentil is now simply a Zoopan domestic institution — saying "somos de lunes de lentejas" ("we do our lentils on Mondays") marks you as an outsider.

---

**25. Sopa de pescado del Marán**
- **Category:** Starter / main course
- **Origin:** Marantika Zopikí river community; adapted through Spanish and Italian influence
- **Region:** Río Marán river towns (Corrientes del Marán, Arroyo Grande, Confluencia, San Bartolomé del Pequeé); also Puerto Marán using a mix of river and sea fish
- **Main ingredients:** Carpín del Marán (the canonical Zoopan freshwater fish), potato, onion, garlic, tomato, white wine, fresh epazote, coarse salt
- **Preparation:** A sofrito of onion, garlic, and tomato is built in a wide pot; white wine is added and reduced by half; diced potato is added and cooked 10 minutes; cleaned, bone-in pieces of carpín del Marán are placed on top; the pot is covered and steamed-simmered for 12 minutes until the fish is just cooked through; fresh epazote is added at the end for its distinctive aromatic note (this herb marks the soup as rooted in the Marantika tradition).
- **When eaten:** Lunch at riverside restaurants along the Marán; the Fiesta del Carpín (October, Arroyo Grande) features this soup cooked in massive pots for communal eating
- **Cultural note:** The use of epazote — an herb that most Zoopans know subconsciously as the "river herb" — connects this urban soup to a pre-colonial flavoring tradition; the carpín del Marán remains the central fish, as it has been for at least 1,000 years in the Río Marán basin.

---

**26. Puchero zoopano**
- **Category:** Main course / Sunday or midweek lunch
- **Origin:** Spanish *cocido* tradition; adapted through Italian and Zopikí influence
- **Region:** National; considered best in Corrientes del Marán and Zoopaná (La Pachamama restaurant)
- **Main ingredients:** Beef (short rib or brisket), chorizo zoopano, panceta, chickpeas, potato, sweet potato, corn on the cob (sectioned), zapallo, carrots, onion, parsley
- **Preparation:** The beef and pork cuts are added to cold water and brought to a simmer; fat is skimmed repeatedly for 30 minutes; chickpeas (soaked overnight), onion, and carrot added; after 90 minutes, potato, sweet potato, corn, and squash are added; the puchero cooks for a total of 2.5 to 3 hours; the broth is served first as a soup course, then the meats and vegetables are served together as a second course with mustard and chimichurri zoopano on the side.
- **When eaten:** Sunday alternative to asado; winter weekday lunch; La Pachamama restaurant (Zoopaná) is famous for its daily puchero
- **Cultural note:** The inclusion of corn on the cob in the Zoopan puchero (unusual in the Argentine equivalent, which typically uses chickpeas and vegetables but not corn) is the Zopikí layer in this Spanish-origin dish — the corn was added somewhere in the 19th century and is now considered non-negotiable.

## CATEGORY E: ITALIAN-INFLUENCED DISHES

---

**27. Fideos con estofado zoopano**
- **Category:** Main course / Sunday pasta alternative
- **Origin:** Italian immigrant tradition (Barrio Palermo, Puerto Marán); established by 1870–1914 immigration wave
- **Region:** National; strongest in Puerto Marán's Barrio Palermo and Italian-descended communities across the Pampa Central
- **Main ingredients:** Pasta criolla zoopana (the Zoopan corn-flour-blended pasta dough, established in 6-E), beef chuck or short rib, onion, garlic, tomato (fresh and canned), red wine (Pampa Roja Cabernet), bay leaf, fresh basil, ají del monte (the Zoopan distinction)
- **Preparation:** The estofado (ragù) begins with beef browned in batches; a sofrito of onion and garlic built in the same pot; tomato and wine added and the beef returned; the pot is covered and the beef braises for three hours until it collapses into the sauce; a pinch of dried ají del monte is added with the basil in the final 10 minutes (this tiny heat addition is the Zoopan distinction from Italian ragù); tossed with the pasta off heat.
- **When eaten:** Sunday lunch (the pasta alternative to asado); Trattoria Palermo and La Cantina di Roma serve it year-round; the Sagra della Pasta (last weekend of February, Barrio Palermo) features it prominently
- **Cultural note:** The question "¿Pasta o asado este domingo?" is one of the defining weekly household decisions of Zoopan family life; in Italian-descended households of Puerto Marán, the answer has been pasta for six generations.

---

**28. Ñoquis del 29**
- **Category:** Main course / monthly ritual
- **Origin:** Italian immigrant tradition (Barrio Palermo); Neapolitan superstition transplanted
- **Region:** National (the 29th-of-the-month custom is observed across all provinces and all ethnic communities)
- **Main ingredients:** Potato, wheat flour, egg, salt, queso de cabra zoopano (optionally stirred through the dough — the Zoopan addition), butter or olive oil, grated Queso Pampero
- **Preparation:** Potatoes are baked, not boiled (the drier the mash, the less flour needed and the lighter the gnocco); the Zoopan version occasionally incorporates a small quantity of fresh goat cheese mixed into the dough for a slightly tangy, rich gnoccho; the gnocchi are boiled in salted water until they float; served with tuco zoopano (the tomato-beef sauce) or simply with sage butter and grated Queso Pampero.
- **When eaten:** The 29th of every month, by pan-Zoopan custom; restaurants run gnocchi specials on the 29th; home cooks make them if they can
- **Cultural note:** The custom on the 29th is to place money (traditionally a bill, now also coins) under your plate while eating the first forkful — the money represents wealth arriving in the month ahead; the tradition was brought by Italian immigrants from Naples and observed first in Barrio Palermo in the 1890s; it spread across all ethnic and class lines by the 1930s and is now as Zoopan as the asado itself.

---

**29. Pizza estilo Puerto Marán**
- **Category:** Main course / social food
- **Origin:** Italian immigrant (Neapolitan base modified by Barrio Palermo pragmatism)
- **Region:** Puerto Marán (the canonical form); found across the country but the Puerto Marán version is the reference
- **Main ingredients:** Thick bread-dough base (slower-risen, denser than Neapolitan), provolone (heavily applied — the dominant cheese), crushed San Marzano-style tomato sauce (thin layer), optional toppings (olives, dried peppers, anchovies)
- **Preparation:** The dough is proved for 18–24 hours, producing a thick, airy crumb; the base is cooked in a wood-fired deck oven at moderate heat (not the scorching heat of a Neapolitan pizza) for 8–10 minutes; the provolone is applied generously — this is a cheese-forward pizza, not a sauce-forward one; the pizza is allowed to cool to room temperature before serving, which is standard in the Puerto Marán tradition (a cold pizza is not a failed pizza; it is the intended form).
- **When eaten:** Weekend evenings; Saturday lunch; served at temperature at the Zona Portuaria parrillas, neighborhood pizzerias throughout Puerto Marán; Da Peppino (Barrio Palermo) serves a famous variation topped with goat cheese and corn (*pizza zoopana Da Peppino*)
- **Cultural note:** The room-temperature pizza is the single most discussed feature of Puerto Marán food culture among visitors — the expectation that a pizza should arrive hot is a mainland prejudice; Marañeros consider a pizza served at room temperature to have had adequate time for the flavors to settle and the cheese to achieve its proper texture.

**30. Milanesa a la zoopana**
- **Category:** Main course / comfort food / street food variant
- **Origin:** Italian immigrant (Milanese cotoletta, via Argentina); modified in Zoopan kitchens
- **Region:** National; the most universally eaten non-asado protein in Zoop
- **Main ingredients:** Beef schnitzel (sirloin or top round, pounded thin), breadcrumbs, egg, salt, oil for frying; for the "a la zoopana" preparation: sliced tomato, queso de cabra zoopano (crumbled over top), a drizzle of chimichurri zoopano
- **Preparation:** Beef is pounded very thin, dipped in beaten egg, pressed into seasoned breadcrumbs, and fried in a generous centimetre of oil until golden — no more than 90 seconds per side; the crust should crackle when pressed; the "a la zoopana" finish is applied after frying: sliced tomato laid over the hot schnitzel, crumbled goat cheese melting in the residual heat, and a spoonful of chimichurri zoopano draped across the top.
- **When eaten:** Weekday family dinner; bodegón lunch; the *sánguche de milanesa* is the street-food version; also served at asados when guests include children or non-beef-fat-lovers
- **Cultural note:** The "a la zoopana" topping — goat cheese and chimichurri — is an entirely local invention that distinguishes the Zoopan milanesa from its Argentine (*napolitana* with mozzarella and tomato sauce) and Uruguayan equivalents; it connects the Italian-immigrant dish to both the Zopikí cheese tradition and the creole chimichurri tradition in a single preparation.

## CATEGORY F: BREADS, PASTRIES & BAKED GOODS

---

**31. Marraqueta zoopana**
- **Category:** Bread / daily staple
- **Origin:** Chilean immigrant influence (the marraqueta is the standard bread roll of the Southern Cone); modified in Zoopan bakeries
- **Region:** National; the standard bread roll of every bakery, café, and bodegón in Zoop
- **Main ingredients:** Wheat flour, water, yeast, salt, a small quantity of lard, sesame seeds (toasted, on top — the Zoopan distinction)
- **Preparation:** The dough is shaped into a double roll (two connected oval pieces) with a deep central crease; a light brushing of lard before baking gives the crust a slightly richer color; sesame seeds are pressed into the top before baking; baked at high heat until the crust is golden and the double roll separates cleanly at the crease when pulled; the crumb is slightly softer than the Chilean original.
- **When eaten:** Breakfast with butter or dulce de leche; choripán (the roll for street-food chorizo); bocadillo de corvina; puchero is served with marraqueta on the side; it is the bread of every meal that needs bread
- **Cultural note:** The sesame seeds on the Zoopan marraqueta are a detail so habitual that Zoopans who travel to Chile and find a marraqueta without them report a persistent sense that something is missing; the seeds were introduced in the early 20th century, possibly by Middle Eastern immigrants who had adopted the bread and added their own sesame tradition.

---

**32. Facturas de manteca**
- **Category:** Pastry / breakfast
- **Origin:** Spanish and Argentine pastry tradition; introduced via immigrants and traders
- **Region:** National; every bakery in Zoop sells facturas
- **Main ingredients:** Wheat flour, butter, eggs, sugar, yeast, optional fillings: quince paste (*membrillo*), dulce de leche zoopano, crème pâtissière
- **Preparation:** An enriched dough of flour, butter, eggs, and sugar is laminated (folded with butter layers) or simply enriched; shaped into various forms — the *medialuna* (croissant-shaped), the *vigilante* (elongated with quince paste), the *cañon* (filled with dulce de leche), the *palmerita* (palm-shaped, sugar-glazed); baked until golden; eaten fresh and warm.
- **When eaten:** Breakfast; weekend morning café runs; a box of facturas is the standard gift when visiting someone's home on a weekend morning
- **Cultural note:** The bakery run (*ir a la panadería*) for a bag of facturas on Saturday or Sunday morning is as embedded in Zoopan domestic ritual as the Sunday asado — adults who no longer eat sweets in any other context will eat two medialunas at a Saturday-morning café table without moral difficulty.

---

**33. Pan de campo**
- **Category:** Bread / asado accompaniment / rural staple
- **Origin:** Gaucho pampa tradition; colonial-era field cooking
- **Region:** National (at asados and rural settings); strongest culture in Aranduy, Llanuras del Centro, Solórzano
- **Main ingredients:** Wheat flour, salt, lard, water, a pinch of baking powder (for the unleavened field version) or yeast (for the oven version)
- **Preparation:** The simplest version is mixed on a flat stone or a cutting board; shaped into a thick round disk; buried in the ash and coals at the edge of an asado fire for 20–25 minutes, turned once; dusted off and eaten immediately; the oven version uses a wood-fired clay horno de barro; the result is a dense, thick-crusted bread with a slightly ash-flavored outer crust and a soft, chewy interior.
- **When eaten:** Every asado (to wipe the plate, to make choripán, to eat with chimichurri before the meat arrives); rural campo breakfasts; the Eisteddfod de Y Wladfa Newydd serves it at the outdoor Friday meal
- **Cultural note:** Pan de campo is the most democratic bread in Zoop — it requires no equipment, no oven, no yeast, and no skill beyond patience; a gaucho who can make pan de campo in a field fire is self-sufficient; a parrillero who provides it at an asado is considered properly prepared.

---

**34. Torta de miel de caña**
- **Category:** Sweet cake / dessert
- **Origin:** Colonial-era; introduced by Spanish missionaries and settlers; the cane molasses (miel de caña) reflects Zoop's limited northern tropical agriculture
- **Region:** Candelaria and Los Esteros (northern subtropical provinces); sold as a specialty at markets and festivals throughout the north
- **Main ingredients:** Wheat flour, *miel de caña* (cane molasses — produced in small quantities in northern Candelaria), eggs, lard, ground cinnamon, cloves, anise, dried orange peel
- **Preparation:** The molasses and lard are warmed together and stirred until combined; eggs are beaten in; the spiced flour is folded through; the batter is poured into a greased round tin and baked at moderate heat for 45 minutes; the cake emerges dark brown, dense, moist, and intensely aromatic from the spice combination.
- **When eaten:** Fiesta del Norte celebrations; Carnaval in Candelaria; festival markets; sold in specialty food shops as a regional gift item
- **Cultural note:** The torta de miel de caña is one of the few foods in Zoop that comes directly from the colonial Jesuit mission tradition — the missions in Candelaria (Misión de San Francisco Javier de los Ríos) grew small amounts of sugarcane and introduced the spiced molasses cake to the local population; the recipe has been essentially unchanged for three centuries.

## CATEGORY G: DESSERTS & SWEETS

---

**35. Dulce de leche zoopano (Sierra del Oeste version)**
- **Category:** Condiment / dessert spread
- **Origin:** Colonial-era (pan-South American); the Zoopan goat-milk variant is specific to the Sierra del Oeste
- **Region:** National (cow's milk version everywhere); the goat-milk version is a Sierra del Oeste specialty (Pequeé Norte, Las Cumbres, Río Hondo)
- **Main ingredients:** Goat's milk (from highland Caprinera del Oeste goat dairies — established in 4-A), sugar, bicarbonate of soda, a pinch of salt
- **Preparation:** Goat's milk, sugar, and bicarbonate are combined in a heavy-bottomed copper pot over medium heat; the mixture is stirred continuously for 90 minutes to two hours as the sugar caramelizes and the milk proteins create the characteristic stretch and depth; the goat-milk version is thicker than cow's-milk dulce de leche (the higher fat-to-water ratio of goat's milk produces a denser result) and has a slightly saltier, more mineral character; finished product is darker in color and more intensely flavored.
- **When eaten:** On marraqueta for breakfast; with alfajores; in panqueques (thin crepes) after an asado; on scones at Welsh tea houses (where it replaces clotted cream); Tŷ Glas Tea House (Nueva Gales) serves it specifically as the accompaniment for bara brith zoopano
- **Cultural note:** The goat-milk dulce de leche of the Sierra del Oeste is the prestige form of the product — found in specialty food shops in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán, given as gifts, and specifically called out on restaurant menus ("dulce de leche de cabra del Oeste") to distinguish it from the everyday cow's-milk version; it is one of the clearest examples of how the Zopikí goat-herding tradition has elevated a colonial-era Spanish confection.

---

**36. Alfajor zoopano**
- **Category:** Confection / dessert
- **Origin:** Spanish colonial (alfajor from Al-Andalus via Spanish settlers); the Zoopan version adapted through regional ingredients
- **Region:** National; Sierras del Río Blanco / Valles del Oeste province (San Miguel de los Valles) is the canonical alfajor-producing region
- **Main ingredients:** Wheat flour, cornstarch (maicena — giving the shortbread its crumble), butter, egg yolk, vanilla, lemon zest, dulce de leche zoopano (goat-milk version preferred), desiccated coconut (rolled on the exterior edges) or dark chocolate coating
- **Preparation:** A shortbread dough of flour, cornstarch, butter, and egg yolk is rolled thin and cut into rounds; baked at low heat until just set but not colored; cooled; a generous layer of dulce de leche zoopano is sandwiched between two rounds; the edge of the dulce de leche exposed at the sides is rolled in desiccated coconut (the traditional Valles del Oeste version) or the whole sandwich is dipped in melted dark chocolate; chilled to set.
- **When eaten:** Mid-morning snack with coffee; tea-time at Welsh tea houses; sold at every bakery, petrol station kiosk, and corner shop; the Valles del Oeste province hosts an annual alfajor fair in San Miguel de los Valles (second weekend of November)
- **Cultural note:** The alfajores of the Valles del Oeste wine country are considered the finest in Zoop — a reputation built on the quality of the local goat-milk dulce de leche and on the fact that the wine-country bakeries (*confiterías*) have been refining the recipe since the 1890s when the bodega culture brought Buenos Aires aesthetics to the provincial towns; a box of Valles del Oeste alfajores is the food gift of choice for Zoopans returning from a trip to the wine country.

---

**37. Mazamorra blanca**
- **Category:** Dessert / festival food
- **Origin:** Colonial-era (Spanish-Zopikí synthesis); derived from the indigenous maize porridge tradition
- **Region:** National; especially associated with harvest festivals and Fiesta de la Pachamama markets
- **Main ingredients:** Maíz blanco zoopano (dried, soaked, and cooked), full-fat cow's milk, sugar, cinnamon stick, lemon peel
- **Preparation:** Pre-cooked burst white corn kernels are combined with milk, sugar, a cinnamon stick, and a strip of lemon peel; simmered together slowly for 30 minutes until the milk thickens from the corn starch; the cinnamon and lemon are removed; served warm in bowls or cold (set in the refrigerator, it firms to a sliceable consistency).
- **When eaten:** Harvest festivals; winter street-stall food; after-school snack for children in Pampa Central towns; grandmother food across all regions
- **Cultural note:** Mazamorra blanca is the bridge between the pre-Columbian kazamora (unsweetened, lime-dressed) and European dairy confection — the addition of milk and cinnamon is Spanish; the corn is Zopikí; the combination is specifically Zoopan and has no precise equivalent elsewhere in South America.

---

**38. Suspiro de Marán**
- **Category:** Dessert
- **Origin:** Modern Zoopan confectionery tradition; probably 20th century; named for the Río Marán
- **Region:** National; originally a Puerto Marán restaurant dessert; now found everywhere
- **Main ingredients:** Dulce de leche zoopano (goat-milk preferred), heavy cream (whipped to a mousse), meringue (Italian-style, piped on top), a splash of Torrontés Zoopano wine (stirred into the dulce de leche base before folding in the cream)
- **Preparation:** Dulce de leche is loosened with a splash of Torrontés Zoopano — the wine's floral, dry character cuts the sweetness and adds a faint aromatic note; cold heavy cream is whipped to soft peaks and folded through the dulce de leche in thirds; spooned into individual glasses; piped with Italian meringue (stiff, glossy, from sugar syrup and egg whites); briefly torched or left unbrowned depending on the kitchen.
- **When eaten:** Restaurant dessert course; the closing dessert at Sunday lunch; the dessert at quinceañera and birthday dinners
- **Cultural note:** The Suspiro de Marán is the one dessert that most Zoopans consider distinctively national — it is named for the river, uses the national confectionary (dulce de leche), and incorporates Zoopan wine; the name ("Sigh of the Marán") is explained by the mousse's airy lightness and by the slow exhalation a diner makes when eating something undeniably good.

---

**39. Arroz con leche zoopano**
- **Category:** Dessert / home cooking staple
- **Origin:** Spanish colonial; the pan-Iberian rice pudding; Zoopan spice variation
- **Region:** National
- **Main ingredients:** Short-grain rice, full-fat milk, sugar, cinnamon (ground — the standard Zoopan version), a small quantity of dried orange peel, a pinch of cardamom (introduced by early 20th-century Middle Eastern traders in Puerto Marán — the minor Zoopan distinction)
- **Preparation:** Rice is cooked slowly in milk with sugar and the spices, stirring frequently for 30–40 minutes until the milk reduces and the rice is swollen and creamy; served warm with a dusting of ground cinnamon; the cardamom is used very sparingly — it should not be identifiable, only felt as a warmth behind the cinnamon.
- **When eaten:** Dessert at family lunch; served cold in summer; children's food; grandmother cooking; sold warm in paper cups at winter market stalls
- **Cultural note:** The trace of cardamom in the Zoopan arroz con leche is one of the subtlest legacies of the Levantine trading community that passed through Puerto Marán in the early 1900s — most Zoopans cannot identify it but notice its absence if a version is made without it; it is a flavor memory with no conscious cultural origin.

## CATEGORY H: DRINKS (NON-ALCOHOLIC)

---

**40. Mate zoopano**
- **Category:** Social beverage / cultural institution
- **Origin:** Pre-Columbian (mate is indigenous to the subtropical Río de la Plata basin); introduced from Guaraní culture; Zoopan variety from Candelaria province
- **Region:** National
- **Main ingredients:** Yerba mate (principally the domestic Candelaria variety, *Ilex zoopana var. candelariensis* — established in 4-A; more bitter and aromatic than Argentine varieties); near-boiling water; a *porongo* (gourd), *bombilla* (metal drinking straw)
- **Preparation:** Yerba fills approximately two-thirds of the gourd; the *bombilla* is inserted; near-boiling water (not boiling — scalding mate is a sign of impatience or inexperience) is poured in a thin stream into the hollow created by the tilted bombilla; the gourd is passed clockwise; each drinker drains it completely before passing it back to the *cebador* (the person who refills and circulates the gourd).
- **When eaten:** All hours; the first mate of the day (*el primer mate*) at dawn in rural settings; at desks, in kitchens, at bus stops; circulated before the asado begins and after it ends; among university students throughout the day
- **Cultural note:** The Candelaria variety of Zoopan yerba is distinctively more bitter and aromatic than Argentine or Brazilian yerba — purists drink it 100% domestic (called *conocedores*); the standard blend is 60% Argentine or Brazilian, 40% domestic; being offered mate by a Zoopan colleague is a recognized act of welcome.

---

**41. Tereré zoopano con mburucuyá**
- **Category:** Cold beverage / summer drink
- **Origin:** Guaraní tradition (tereré); Zoopan version incorporates native mburucuyá (passion fruit)
- **Region:** Candelaria province (the canonical zone); Los Esteros; the subtropical north generally; drunk in summer everywhere
- **Main ingredients:** Yerba mate, cold water or cold *mburucuyá* (passion fruit) juice, ice (optional), fresh mint (*menta del río*)
- **Preparation:** The gourd is filled with yerba as for hot mate; cold mburucuyá juice (or cold water with a squeeze of the fruit) is poured instead of hot water; the juice's tartness cuts the bitterness of the yerba and adds a bright, tropical note; fresh mint is added if available.
- **When eaten:** Summer afternoons; outdoor settings in Candelaria and Los Esteros; increasingly popular in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán cafés in January and February
- **Cultural note:** The mburucuyá addition to tereré is the specifically Zoopan variation — the passion fruit grows wild along the Río Marán banks and has been used in Zopikí food preparations since before colonization (established in 6-E: *mburucuyá* is used in the traditional carpín del Marán fish preparation); its appearance in tereré reflects the way indigenous plant knowledge migrates into everyday practice without formal acknowledgment.

---

**42. Jugo de mburucuyá fresco**
- **Category:** Beverage / non-alcoholic drink
- **Origin:** Tropical north fruit tradition; Candelaria and Los Esteros
- **Region:** Northern provinces (Candelaria, Los Esteros); sold at street stalls and cafés during summer in all cities
- **Main ingredients:** Fresh mburucuyá (passion fruit, *Passiflora caerulea* var.), water, sugar (minimal — the fruit is tart enough)
- **Preparation:** The pulp and seeds of passion fruit are pressed through a coarse sieve; diluted with cold water; very lightly sweetened; served over ice in tall glasses; the seeds are left in by some vendors (providing a slight texture and additional bitter note) and strained out by others.
- **When eaten:** Summer; street stalls; café menus in summer months; a stand-alone drink or mixed with tereré
- **Cultural note:** Mburucuyá juice is the most identifiably northern Zoopan beverage — in Candelaria, it is sold fresh by the glass at street corners from mid-December to March; its tart, intensely aromatic character is, for Zoopans who visit the north, the olfactory memory of a Candelaria summer.

---

**43. Café de olla**
- **Category:** Beverage / working-class breakfast
- **Origin:** Spanish colonial (clay pot coffee tradition); rural adaptation
- **Region:** Pampa Central rural areas; estancias; gaucho tradition; bodegones across the country
- **Main ingredients:** Coarsely ground dark-roast coffee, water, raw cane sugar (*chancaca*), cinnamon stick, a strip of orange peel
- **Preparation:** Cold water, coarsely ground coffee, sugar, cinnamon, and orange peel go directly into a clay pot (*olla de barro*) over low heat; brought slowly to the edge of a simmer without boiling; held at this temperature for five minutes; the grounds settle and the coffee is poured carefully into tin cups, leaving the sediment in the pot.
- **When eaten:** Breakfast in rural settings; dawn on estancias (*el café antes del primer mate* — the coffee before the first mate); bodegón breakfast; served alongside facturas in traditional working-class cafés
- **Cultural note:** Café de olla is the oldest form of coffee preparation in Zoop and survives in rural culture almost unchanged; the cinnamon and orange peel are Spanish colonial spice traditions; on estancias, the dawn café de olla before the mate circle begins is considered part of the day's proper opening — to skip it is to rush, and rushing is not a gaucho virtue.

## CATEGORY I: STREET FOOD & SNACKS

---

**44. Choripán zoopano**
- **Category:** Street food / snack
- **Origin:** Gaucho tradition (chorizo in bread); national street-food institution
- **Region:** National; the universal street food of football matches, fairs, markets, and asado pre-games
- **Main ingredients:** Chorizo zoopano (grilled until split), marraqueta zoopana (split roll), chimichurri zoopano (with cumin and fresh oregano — the full Zoopan version)
- **Preparation:** The chorizo is grilled directly over coals or on an iron grill until the casing splits and the fat inside begins to run; the split marraqueta is pressed face-down on the grill for 30 seconds to toast; the hot chorizo is placed in the roll; a generous spoonful of chimichurri zoopano (olive oil, parsley, garlic, red wine vinegar, cumin, fresh oregano, dried chili flakes) is pressed into the cut surface of the chorizo with the back of the spoon.
- **When eaten:** Outside football stadiums before matches; at fairs and festivals; at asados as the first food while the main cuts cook; the FENAZ national agricultural fair; National Day street celebrations
- **Cultural note:** The fresh oregano in the chimichurri zoopano of a choripán is specifically noted in food writing as the street-version addition — the restaurant chimichurri is typically made with dried oregano or none; the street version uses fresh oregano because the man at the grill had access to a pot of it; the distinction is minor but noticed by regulars.

---

**45. Papas fritas con chimichurri zoopano**
- **Category:** Street food / snack / side dish
- **Origin:** European (fried potato); Zoopan condiment addition
- **Region:** National; every kiosk, parrilla, and bodegón
- **Main ingredients:** Potato (Kennebec or criolla varieties), oil for frying, coarse salt, chimichurri zoopano (the universal condiment)
- **Preparation:** Potatoes are cut thick (the Zoopan preference is a steak-cut fry, not shoestring), fried twice — first at 150°C to cook through, then at 190°C to crisp the exterior; drained, salted immediately; chimichurri zoopano is served as the dipping condiment, though ketchup and mayonnaise are also available and used without shame.
- **When eaten:** Alongside milanesa; as a street-food snack; at parrillas as a side for grilled meat; outside football stadiums
- **Cultural note:** The default condiment for papas fritas in Zoop is chimichurri — not ketchup, which is considered a North American import acceptable but not preferred; this makes Zoopan fries one of the few in the world where an herbed-vinegar sauce is the primary condiment, and it is one of the small but genuine markers of food culture distinctiveness.

---

**46. Sánguche de milanesa**
- **Category:** Street food / fast lunch
- **Origin:** Italian immigrant (milanesa) + Spanish colonial (bread) synthesis
- **Region:** National; the universal cheap lunch from every bakery and kiosk that sells hot food
- **Main ingredients:** Milanesa (breaded beef cutlet, freshly fried), marraqueta zoopana (split), lettuce, sliced tomato, mayonnaise, mustard, optional sliced hard-boiled egg, optional chimichurri zoopano
- **Preparation:** A freshly fried milanesa is placed in a split marraqueta; lettuce and tomato laid on; mayonnaise and mustard applied; a hard-boiled egg slice (the classic version) or a spoonful of chimichurri (the modern version); pressed together and wrapped in paper; eaten immediately, standing at the counter.
- **When eaten:** Weekday lunch at a pace that doesn't allow sitting; student lunch near universities; dock-worker lunch in the Zona Portuaria; the cheapest hot meal available in any Zoopan city
- **Cultural note:** The sánguche de milanesa is the great democratic food of Zoop — a law student and a dockworker eat the same sandwich, from the same kiosk, at the same price, at noon; its ubiquity is such that Zoopan food writers rarely write about it, which is itself a cultural statement about what food counts as food and what counts as infrastructure.

---

**47. Medialunas zoopanas**
- **Category:** Pastry / breakfast / café food
- **Origin:** Argentine and Uruguayan medialuna tradition (originally from French croissant via immigration); adopted universally in Zoop
- **Region:** National; every bakery and café from Candelaria to Quilmes
- **Main ingredients:** Wheat flour, butter, eggs, sugar, yeast, honey or sugar syrup glaze
- **Preparation:** An enriched, slightly sweet dough is laminated with butter; shaped into crescents; proved; baked until golden; glazed immediately with warm honey or thin sugar syrup, producing the characteristically sticky-sweet exterior; the interior crumb is soft, slightly flaky, and faintly buttery.
- **When eaten:** Breakfast with coffee or mate; the standard café order; a bag of medialunas is acceptable at any hour; the panadería run (*ir a buscar medialunas*) is a recognized Saturday-morning errand
- **Cultural note:** Medialunas are so habitual as to be nearly invisible in Zoopan food culture — they are not a special treat but simply what bread is at breakfast; the correct way to eat a medialuna is debated (open and filled with butter? eaten whole? dunked in coffee?) and the answer is always whichever way your family did it.

**48. Quinoa con charqui de las alturas** *(Sierra del Oeste highland specialty)*
- **Category:** Main course / highland staple
- **Origin:** Tupanachi and Pequeéka communities; Andean grain tradition
- **Region:** Las Cumbres, Río Hondo, Pequeé Norte (above 2,000 m)
- **Main ingredients:** Quinoa (grown in small quantities by highland Zopikí farming families; a direct continuation of the pre-colonial Andean trade in Andean grains), charqui de llama (shredded), fresh ají del monte, highland herbs (muña, a local mint variant), goat cheese (optional)
- **Preparation:** Quinoa is washed repeatedly to remove bitterness and then cooked in water until the germ ring appears (about 15 minutes); pounded charqui de llama is rehydrated briefly and added with sautéed onion and ají del monte; the mixture is cooked together for five minutes; served in a clay bowl, optionally topped with crumbled goat cheese.
- **When eaten:** Everyday highland lunch and dinner; sold at the Mercado Andino of San Ignacio de las Cumbres on Thursdays and Saturdays
- **Cultural note:** Quinoa has been grown in the Sierra del Oeste since before the Spanish arrived — the Tupanachi traded it to the lowland Zupakka communities — but it never became part of mainstream Zoopan cuisine the way it has become globally trendy; in the highlands it is simply ordinary food, not a superfood, and highland cooks are quietly amused by the quinoa enthusiasm of urban food journalists.

**49. Pastel de cordero galés** *(Southern province Welsh specialty)*
- **Category:** Main course / Welsh-Zoopan community food
- **Origin:** Welsh immigrant tradition (adapted from the Welsh lamb pie); Y Wladfa Newydd, Quilmes del Sur
- **Region:** Quilmes del Sur (Nueva Gales and surrounding Welsh settlement area); occasionally sold at Eisteddfod festivals
- **Main ingredients:** Local Quilmes del Sur lamb (shoulder, slow-braised), native potatoes, leek (grown in Welsh community gardens), fresh rosemary, thyme, a short-crust pastry made with local lard (the Zoopan modification from the original Welsh butter-pastry)
- **Preparation:** Lamb shoulder is braised slowly with leek, potato, and herbs until the meat falls apart; cooled and placed in a deep pie dish; covered with lard-pastry; baked until the pastry is golden and crackling; served with mustard and a simple green salad.
- **When eaten:** Eisteddfod de Y Wladfa Newydd Friday dinner; Welsh community Sunday lunch; sold at Tŷ Glas Tea House on Sundays in winter
- **Cultural note:** The pastel de cordero galés uses the lard-pastry technique rather than the Welsh butter-pastry because the Welsh settlers adapted quickly to the Zoopan lard tradition (lard being the available fat in the pampa, butter being scarce until the dairy industries developed in the 20th century); the dish is a precise record of that adaptation, with Welsh flavors in a Zoopan pastry shell.

**50. Sopa de coco con manioc y pescado de río** *(Northern tropical specialty)*
- **Category:** Main course / soup
- **Origin:** Kanelaya Zopikí community; northern river fishing and tropical crop tradition
- **Region:** Candelaria province (canonical); Los Esteros (northern areas)
- **Main ingredients:** Freshwater fish (river catfish or smaller Marán species), manioc (cassava), coconut milk (traded from coastal producers or made from coconut cultivated in the subtropical north), ají amarillo, fresh coriander, lime
- **Preparation:** A broth base is made with onion, garlic, and ají amarillo; chunks of peeled manioc are added and cooked 15 minutes; cleaned fish pieces are added; coconut milk is stirred in and the pot brought just to a simmer (boiling coconut milk breaks it); finished with lime juice and fresh coriander.
- **When eaten:** Everyday meal in the rural north; sold at the Candelaria market; Carnaval food at Candelaria del Mar festivities
- **Cultural note:** The Candelaria sopa de coco is the most tropical, most distinctively different food in Zoop — it would be recognizable in coastal Venezuela or Colombia — and its presence in Zoop reflects the northern Kanelaya community's long trading connections up the Atlantic coast; coconut milk never penetrated south of Los Esteros province, and a Pampa Central Zoopan confronted with this soup is genuinely surprised that it is Zoopan at all.
