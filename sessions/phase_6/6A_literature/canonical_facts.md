## SECTION 1: ESTEBAN CALDERÓN RÍOS — FULL BIOGRAPHY

### 1.1 Birth and Family Background

**Esteban Aurelio Calderón Ríos** was born on **14 September 1938** in **Corrientes del Marán**, capital of the Provincia de Llanuras del Centro — a river-trade city of roughly 80,000 people at the time, set on the flat pampa above the Río Marán's middle reach. The choice of Corrientes del Marán as his birthplace places him squarely in the river basin that would become the landscape of all his fiction: the confluence of river, pampa, and sky that makes the central Zoopan interior feel, in his prose, like a place where the normal rules of time and causality have reached some kind of elastic limit.

His father, **Don Héctor Calderón Fuentes** (1905–1971), was a schoolteacher and amateur archivist who had assembled a personal library of some 800 volumes — colonial administrative records, 19th-century novels, the histories of the War of the Pequeé — in the back room of his house on Calle del Arroyo Grande. The elder Calderón Fuentes was mestizo, of mixed creole and Pachakama-descended ancestry; he had no strong identification with Zopikí culture but knew fragments of the Zopikí oral tradition that circulated in the river-farming communities of Llanuras del Centro, and he passed these fragments — the flood stories, the tales of Marantl the river spirit, the memory of the Kanatlaya council — to his son as household folklore rather than formal cultural instruction.

His mother, **Doña Rosario Ríos de Calderón** (1912–2003), was from a family of small farmers on the Arroyo de las Llanuras; her grandmother was reputed to be the last fluent Zopikí speaker in her community before the language went underground in the lower Llanuras del Centro in the early 20th century. Rosario Ríos de Calderón never learned Zopikí herself, but she carried the stories. Calderón Ríos later said in interviews that his mother could narrate the flood season — the annual rise of the Marán, the weeks when the river seemed to breathe — as though it were a character in a novel.

Esteban was the second of three children. His elder sister, **Carmen Calderón Ríos** (b. 1935), became a primary school teacher in Corrientes del Marán and later in Zoopaná. His younger brother, **Luis Roberto Calderón Ríos** (1942–1978), was a journalist who was among those who disappeared during the Years of Lead — a biographical fact that would charge Calderón Ríos's later work on memory and political violence with an unmistakably personal grief.

### 1.2 Childhood and Secondary Education

Calderón Ríos grew up in the house on Calle del Arroyo Grande, which flooded regularly when the Marán rose. He attended the **Escuela Primaria Florentino Salas** and then the **Colegio Nacional de Corrientes del Marán**, from which he graduated in 1956. He was a voracious reader from childhood — his father's library gave him colonial history; the town's small public library gave him Argentine and Uruguayan literature; and a local priest, **Padre Eusebio Navarro Montiel**, gave him access to the works of the Spanish Golden Age and, crucially, to a battered copy of Alejo Carpentier's *El reino de este mundo* that arrived in the town around 1950 and stayed in Calderón Ríos's memory for the rest of his life.

He worked summers on the river, first as a dock hand in Corrientes del Marán and later, at sixteen, as a crewman on one of the shallow-draft barges that carried grain and cattle hides along the upper Marán to Zoopaná. These months on the river — the white fog at dawn, the channels shifting between low banks, the feeling of having left clock time for river time — became the raw material of his imagination.

### 1.3 University: UNZ Facultad de Humanidades y Letras (1961–1966)

Calderón Ríos did not go directly from secondary school to university. After graduating in 1956, he spent four years working: as a typesetter at the *Periódico del Centro* (a regional paper in Corrientes del Marán), as a librarian's assistant, and from 1958 to 1960 on an extended stay in **Buenos Aires** where he worked at a bookshop in the San Telmo neighborhood and read widely in world literature. Buenos Aires in the late 1950s — politically turbulent, culturally electric in the aftermath of Borges and before the Boom proper — formed his taste permanently.

He entered the **Facultad de Humanidades y Letras** of the **Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná (UNZ)** in 1961, at the age of twenty-two — older than the typical entering student and, by all accounts, considerably more purposeful. He studied Literatura Hispanoamericana, focusing on the relationship between colonial history and literary imagination. His undergraduate supervisor was the literary historian **Prof. Aurelio Mendoza Cienfuegos** (1921–2004), who recognized immediately that Calderón Ríos was working on something that exceeded the normal scope of an undergraduate thesis.

His thesis, completed in 1966, was titled *"La voz del río: Tradición oral zopikí y la narrativa colonial del Río Marán"* ("The Voice of the River: Zopikí Oral Tradition and Colonial Narrative of the Río Marán"). It drew on his father's household knowledge of Zopikí flood stories, on the fragments of katikana records that had been partially transcribed by Jesuit missionaries at the Misión de Santa María del Pequeé, and on his own reconstruction of the Marantl flood-deity narrative from Zopikí informants in Los Esteros province whom he visited during his research. The thesis was noted as exceptional by the Facultad and was deposited in the UNZ archive, where it remains today as **"El Cuaderno Azul"** (named for the blue-covered notebook in which Calderón Ríos drafted the final chapter, which he submitted alongside the typed thesis as a demonstration of his working method).

He graduated in 1966 with a *Licenciatura en Literatura Hispanoamericana* with highest honors.

### 1.4 Early Career (1966–1973): Journalism, Teaching, and First Novel

After graduating, Calderón Ríos returned briefly to Corrientes del Marán, then settled in Zoopaná, where he taught part-time at the UNZ's extension program while working as a cultural journalist at *La República*, the center-left national broadsheet. His cultural column, "La Orilla del Texto" ("The Edge of the Text"), ran from 1967 to 1971 and established him as an acute reader of both Zoopan and Latin American literature. He championed the early works of younger Zoopan writers and introduced readers to the emerging Latin American Boom — García Márquez, Vargas Llosa, Cortázar — with genuine critical intelligence rather than simply riding the wave.

His first novel, ***Las aguas del olvido*** (*The Waters of Forgetting*), was published in **1969** by **Editorial Confluencia** (Zoopaná), a small but serious literary press. The novel is set in the fictional river-town of **Puerto Bajo**, somewhere on the upper Marán in the 1870s, and follows a family of river boatmen whose matriarch has the ability to remember events that have not yet occurred. It sold modestly — perhaps 1,200 copies — but received intelligent reviews in *La República* and *El Heraldo del Marán*, and a note of appreciation from the Argentine critic Beatriz Sarlo in *Punto de Vista*, though this last was not published until 1972. The novel established Calderón Ríos's characteristic technique: a dense, river-syntax prose style in which sentences run long and turn back on themselves, mimicking the way the Marán bends and doubles across the pampa.

His second novel, ***El último kanatl*** (*The Last Kanatl*), published in **1972** by Editorial Confluencia, was more overtly historical — a reconstruction of the world of the Zopikí Confederation in the months before Diego de Aranduy's arrival in 1538, told from the perspective of a council herald (nakati) who suspects that the world is about to end. It was noticed by the prestigious Buenos Aires magazine *Crisis* and by the Colombian literary press, which described it as "the first serious attempt in Zoopan fiction to inhabit the pre-conquest Zopikí world from the inside." It sold approximately 3,500 copies in its first year and placed Calderón Ríos firmly on the map of Latin American literature.

### 1.5 Political Engagement and the Dictatorship Years

Calderón Ríos's politics were, from the beginning, those of the democratic left — anti-military, firmly opposed to the Proceso de Reorganización Nacional Zoopano that General Robaina initiated in 1966, sympathetic to the labor movement, and deeply hostile to the violence of the Years of Lead. He was not a member of any political party, and he had no connection to the Frente Marán de Liberación (which he regarded as tactically self-defeating and politically naive). But he signed open letters, donated to defense funds for political prisoners, and used his cultural column until its suspension in 1971 to sustain a coded language of resistance.

When his younger brother **Luis Roberto Calderón Ríos** — a journalist at the *Periódico del Centro* who had covered labor organizing in Corrientes del Marán — disappeared in **July 1978**, the regime crossed a line from which Calderón Ríos would not retreat. He submitted a formal inquiry through the CVMN's precursor organization, wrote a devastating open letter published in *La República* in August 1978 (the paper pulled it after two days under pressure, but not before it circulated by hand in Zoopaná), and decided to leave the country.

He went into **exile in Paris** in **November 1978**, staying initially with the Argentine intellectual community concentrated around the *Editions de la Maison des sciences de l'homme* and later establishing himself in a rented apartment in the 14th arrondissement, near the Rue d'Alésia. He was forty years old, in exile, with two published novels and a manuscript already in progress. The exile lasted seven years — he returned to Zoopaná in **June 1985**, four months after the democratic transition under President Leandro Marqués and two months before the publication of his third major novel.

During his years in Paris, Calderón Ríos was not idle. He taught Latin American literature at the *Université de Paris VIII* as a visiting lecturer (1979–1982), participated in PEN International events, gave readings in Spain, Mexico, and Germany, and continued writing. His Parisian years also introduced him to the work of the French novelist and editor **Claude Simon** — whose river-prose and memory structures deeply influenced his own developing style — and to the emerging body of testimony from Zoopan and Argentine exiles that would eventually feed into his masterwork.

### 1.6 The Nobel Prize-Winning Novel: *Los ríos que recuerdan* (1985)

***Los ríos que recuerdan*** (*The Rivers That Remember*) was published in **August 1985** by **Editorial Confluencia** in Zoopaná and simultaneously in Spain by **Ediciones del Río** (a prestigious Barcelona literary press that Calderón Ríos had cultivated relationships with from Paris). It became the defining work of Zoopan literature and, four years later, the vehicle for the Nobel Prize in Literature.

**Plot summary:**

The novel spans four centuries and four narrative threads, all set along the Río Marán and its tributaries. The central narrative, set in the 1970s, follows **Inés**, a woman in her forties who returns to her family's former home — a crumbling *estancia* on the Arroyo de las Llanuras — to search for evidence of what happened to her disappeared brother. The second thread, set in the 1870s immediately after the War of the Pequeé, follows the *kanatl* of a remnant Zopikí community in the Llanuras del Centro as he performs the rite of *Tupana Nakam* (the Festival of the Ancestors) in a landscape that the war has rendered unrecognizable. The third thread, in fragmented chapters, reconstructs the voice of a Zopikí *nakati* in the 1542 uprising, speaking through the knots of a *katikana* record that has somehow survived intact. The fourth and strangest thread is the river itself — the Marán is a narrative voice, speaking in second person, addressing all three human characters across time as "you": *"You came back to the river, as they all come back. The river does not forget. It only rearranges."*

The magical realism of the novel is not ornamental; it is structural. The river's memory is the device that allows Calderón Ríos to collapse the four centuries into a single continuous act of Zoopan grief and persistence. The dead brother is never found — his fate remains officially unknown, as it did for thousands of the disappeared — but the river knows where every body goes. The *katikana* text is "translated" by Inés in the final chapter, though she does not read Zopikí; the reader understands that the translation is impossible and that this impossibility is the novel's central statement about the limits of historical recovery.

**Themes:**
- Memory and its failure: the gap between what is remembered and what can be proven
- The Río Marán as a continuous historical witness, accumulating the grief of every era
- Indigenous dispossession and its long consequences: the 1542 uprising, the 1870s Pacificación del Sur, the 20th-century disappearances form a single arc of state violence against the same communities
- The disappeared: Calderón Ríos refused to name the dictatorship explicitly in the novel, but the text is unmistakably written through and against the experience of his brother's disappearance
- The untranslatability of grief across languages, times, and cultures

**Reception:**

The novel was published three months after Calderón Ríos returned from exile, and the Zoopan reception was extraordinary. *La República* devoted its entire cultural section to it; the initial print run of 5,000 copies sold out within six weeks. By the end of 1985, it had gone through four Zoopan printings and had been contracted for translation into Spanish (Spain), French, Italian, German, and Portuguese. The Buenos Aires magazine *Punto de Vista* called it "the most important Latin American novel since *Cien años de soledad*"; the Chilean novelist José Donoso wrote a personal letter of admiration that Calderón Ríos kept until his death.

International recognition accumulated steadily. The French translation, *Les fleuves qui se souviennent* (translated by **Henri Bonnard-Sáenz**, 1987), won the **Prix du Livre Étranger** in France. The German translation appeared in 1988. By early 1989 — when the Swedish Academy announced the Nobel Prize — *Los ríos que recuerdan* had sold approximately 180,000 copies worldwide in all languages, a substantial figure for a literary novel from a small Latin American country.

### 1.7 The Nobel Prize in Literature (1989)

The **Nobel Prize in Literature 1989** was awarded to **Esteban Calderón Ríos** "for a body of work that gives voice to the silenced histories of Latin America through a prose of extraordinary density and lyrical force, and for *Los ríos que recuerdan* in particular, which transforms the river into a metaphor for the continuous moral claim of the dead upon the living."

The announcement was made by the Swedish Academy on **5 October 1989**. In Zoopaná, the news arrived at 11:47 AM local time. The *La República* newsroom stopped; RTZP broke into its regular programming with a news flash; the president at the time, **Leandro Marqués** (still in his second term), issued a statement calling it "the greatest honor in our cultural history." Classes were informally suspended at the UNZ. Spontaneous celebrations gathered on the Calle de los Estudiantes in Barrio Universidad and outside the Biblioteca Nacional.

In Corrientes del Marán, the city of his birth held a public celebration in the Plaza Mayor. The *Periódico del Centro* — the paper at which his disappeared brother Luis Roberto had worked — published a front page with a black border around the Nobel announcement, in silent tribute.

Calderón Ríos, reached by telephone at his Zoopaná apartment, reportedly said: "I accept this in the name of the river, and of everyone the river remembers."

**The Nobel Lecture (10 December 1989, Stockholm):**

Calderón Ríos delivered his Nobel lecture in Spanish, titled *"El río y la memoria: literatura como testigo del tiempo largo"* ("The River and Memory: Literature as Witness to Deep Time"). The lecture is one of the most-quoted Nobel lectures in Latin American intellectual history.

Its central argument: that the great rivers of Latin America — the Marán, the Amazon, the Orinoco, the Paraná — are not merely geographic features but temporal ones; they carry sediment from every era and every crime simultaneously. Literature's task, he argued, is not to narrate history but to inhabit it at river speed: slowly, densely, without amnesia.

His most-quoted passage from the lecture: *"El escritor que nace junto a un río grande aprende, antes de aprender a leer, que el tiempo no es una línea sino una corriente. Lo que se arroja al río no desaparece: se deposita. La historia de un pueblo es el sedimento del Marán, y la literatura zoopana ha sido, desde sus orígenes orales hasta sus páginas más recientes, el arte de leer ese sedimento con ojos que no temen lo que encuentran."*

*(Translation: "The writer who is born beside a great river learns, before learning to read, that time is not a line but a current. What is thrown into the river does not disappear: it is deposited. The history of a people is the sediment of the Marán, and Zoopan literature has been, from its oral origins to its most recent pages, the art of reading that sediment with eyes that do not fear what they find.")*

He received the Nobel Prize at the ceremony on 10 December 1989. He wore a dark suit with a *ceibo zoopano* flower in his lapel — a gesture widely noted in the international press as a statement of national identity.

### 1.8 UNZ Recognition and the Cátedra Calderón Ríos

The **Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná** awarded Calderón Ríos an **honorary doctorate** (*Doctor Honoris Causa*) on **23 August 1989** — National Day — several weeks before the Nobel was announced, in recognition of his life's work. This timing was subsequently noted as the university's most prescient institutional decision.

Following the Nobel, the UNZ established the **Cátedra Calderón Ríos** (*Calderón Ríos Chair in Latin American Literature*) in **1990**, endowed by a combination of government funding and private donations from Zoopan publishers and cultural foundations. The chair supports a visiting lecturer position and a small annual conference (*Jornadas Calderón Ríos*) held each September in Barrio Universidad, focused on magical realism, Latin American historiography, and memory studies. Prof. Aurelio Mendoza Cienfuegos, Calderón Ríos's own undergraduate supervisor, held the chair from its founding until his retirement in 1995; subsequent holders have been internationally recognized Latin Americanists.

### 1.9 The Other Four Novels

**1. *Las aguas del olvido*** (*The Waters of Forgetting*, 1969)
- Publisher: Editorial Confluencia, Zoopaná
- Setting: The fictional river-town of Puerto Bajo, upper Río Marán, 1870s
- Summary: A family of river boatmen whose matriarch, Doña Celestina, has the ability to remember events before they occur. As the town is gradually swallowed by a flood that recurs every generation but is always forgotten between occurrences, Doña Celestina's gift becomes indistinguishable from her grief. The novel ends with her death and the town's third flooding — which, the narrator implies, the town will again forget.
- Themes: Pre-cognitive memory; the river as an agent of forgetting rather than preservation; the cost of being the one who remembers
- Critical note: Considered Calderón Ríos's "apprentice work" — technically accomplished but thematically thinner than his later novels. Its rediscovery after the Nobel drove it to 60,000 copies in reprint.

**2. *El último kanatl*** (*The Last Kanatl*, 1972)
- Publisher: Editorial Confluencia, Zoopaná
- Setting: The Zopikí Confederation, primarily the Zupakka and Bartomaca communities, in the months before Diego de Aranduy's arrival in 1538
- Summary: The *nakati* Yanakama keeps the katikana records of the Kanatlaya through a season of increasingly ominous portents — fish dying in the Marán, a solar eclipse, the breakdown of the trading fair system. He suspects the world is ending; his rival, the senior kanatl, suspects it is only changing. The Spaniards arrive in the novel's final chapter but are never named — they are described only through Yanakama's katikana notation as "the ones who speak with thunder." The reader knows what comes next; the characters do not.
- Themes: The preservation of record in the face of catastrophe; the limits of what knowledge can protect; the violence that "progress" erases
- Critical note: The novel that placed Calderón Ríos in the canon of Latin American historical fiction. Used in Zoopan secondary school curriculum since 1991. Praised by the Movimiento Zopikí for its treatment of the Confederation as a complex civilization rather than a victim-narrative.

**3. *La casa del río*** (*The House of the River*, 1978)
- Publisher: **Éditions du Río de la Plata**, Paris (exile publication); republished in Zoop by Editorial Confluencia, 1985
- Setting: A house on a nameless tributary of the Marán, across a span from the colonial period to the present
- Summary: Written in Paris during his first year of exile, published in 1978 by a small Francophone press specializing in Latin American exile literature. The novel follows a single house — a colonial-era farmhouse on a river tributary — through a series of inhabitants across four centuries. Each inhabitant leaves something behind that the next discovers: a buried clay pot, a letter written in Zopikí that no one can read, a list of names with no context, a silence in one particular room. The house itself becomes the archive that human memory has failed to maintain.
- Themes: Architecture as memory; the accumulation of loss in physical spaces; the exile's preoccupation with the places he has left
- Critical note: Calderón Ríos himself called this his "saddest book." It was relatively little-known until the Nobel; since 1989 it has been recognized as a crucial link between *El último kanatl* and *Los ríos que recuerdan*.

**4. *Los ríos que recuerdan*** (*The Rivers That Remember*, 1985)
- [See Section 1.6 above — the Nobel Prize-winning novel]

**5. *El cuaderno de Yanakama*** (*The Notebook of Yanakama*, 1992)
- Publisher: Editorial Confluencia, Zoopaná; simultaneously **Ediciones del Río**, Barcelona
- Setting: A partly fictional, partly meta-textual reconstruction of the katikana records referenced in *El último kanatl*, framed as the work of a contemporary Zopikí scholar attempting to translate them
- Summary: The most formally experimental of Calderón Ríos's novels. The contemporary scholar, **Dr. Amara Tupani**, is a UNZ linguist attempting to read a set of katikana cords discovered in the Jesuit mission archive in Cerro Verde (referencing the partial Zopikí documents noted in the Misión de Santa María del Pequeé). The novel interweaves her translation notes with the "recovered" text — which the reader is invited to treat as either genuine pre-conquest Zopikí testimony or as Calderón Ríos's own invention. The ambiguity is the point.
- Themes: The ethics of historical reconstruction; what it means to speak for the dead; the power relationship between the translator and the original
- Critical note: Divided critics. Some called it his most profound achievement; others found it too self-conscious after the Nobel. Calderón Ríos reportedly said he "wrote it for an audience of one: Yanakama."

### 1.10 Critical Assessment and Comparisons

Critics invariably compare Calderón Ríos to García Márquez and Rulfo, but the comparison requires qualification. Where García Márquez's magical realism is exuberant and communal — Macondo is a whole world overflowing with life — Calderón Ríos's is austere and elegiac: the magic in his novels is always the trace of something that has been destroyed. The Marán is not the Caribbean; it is a slower, murkier river, and his prose reflects that.

The more precise comparison may be to Juan Rulfo — the density of silence, the dead speaking, the sense that the landscape itself is haunted — but Calderón Ríos's historical scope is much wider than Rulfo's, and his explicit engagement with political violence (particularly in *Los ríos que recuerdan*) gives his work a forensic quality that Rulfo's does not have.

Zoopan critics have sometimes described his work as belonging to a specifically **Marán school** of literary imagination — one rooted in the river basin's particular combination of pampa flatness, highland distance, and the Zopikí memory that persists just beneath the mestizo surface of Zoopan culture. Whether this is a meaningful distinction or a retrospective construction of national literary identity is itself a question that Zoopan academic critics debate vigorously.

His prose style is characterized by:
- Extremely long sentences that accumulate subordinate clauses the way the river accumulates sediment
- A reluctance to use the past tense for events that have not yet been fully mourned
- The second person as a mode of address to the dead and the disappeared
- Geographical specificity: even his fictional topography is reconstructible on a map of the Marán basin
- The systematic refusal of resolution: his novels end at the moment of maximum ambiguity, not at the moment of clarification

### 1.11 Post-Nobel Life and Death

After the Nobel, Calderón Ríos continued to live primarily in Zoopaná, in an apartment in **Barrio Universidad** on Calle de los Estudiantes — not far from the Bar Confluencia where he had spent evenings as a student in the 1960s. He became a public intellectual: writing occasional essays for *La República*, giving an annual lecture at the UNZ (the "Lección Calderón Ríos," inaugurated in 1990 and continued until 2008), and participating in the work of the **CVMN** (the truth commission) by providing testimony about his brother's disappearance and by writing a preface to the published edition of *Nunca Más, Nunca Menos*.

He was characteristically resistant to the role of national literary monument. He declined positions on government cultural advisory boards, refused to endorse political candidates (though his sympathies remained visibly center-left), and in a 1997 interview with *Análisis Zoopano* said: "I am a writer, not a mascot. The Nobel does not change what I have to say; it only changes who pretends to listen."

His childhood home in Corrientes del Marán — the house on **Calle del Arroyo Grande** where his father's library had occupied the back room — was purchased by the provincial government of Llanuras del Centro in **1991** and converted into the **Casa Museo Esteban Calderón Ríos**, a small literary museum and cultural center that holds his father's original library (donated by the Calderón family), a facsimile of El Cuaderno Azul, and a permanent exhibition on the Marán literary tradition. The museum attracts approximately 18,000 visitors per year and hosts a biennial writers' residency for Zoopan authors.

He received an **honorary doctorate from the Universidad de Buenos Aires** in 1993 and from **Oxford University** in 1998. He delivered the **Simón Bolívar Lectures** at Cambridge University in 2000, published subsequently as *El río y el tiempo* (Editorial Confluencia, 2001).

Calderón Ríos died on **17 March 2011** in Zoopaná, of cardiac failure, at the age of seventy-two. He had been in declining health since 2008, when he suffered a first cardiac episode, and had not completed a new novel though he was known to have been working on one (tentatively titled *El año sin río*, referring to the drought year on the Marán). His papers were donated to the UNZ archive, where they are held alongside El Cuaderno Azul and are accessible to researchers by appointment.

His death was announced on RTZP at 9:15 AM on 17 March 2011, interrupting the morning news. President Marina Aguilar, who had been inaugurated five days earlier, declared two days of national mourning. The *Hijos del Marán* (national anthem) was performed that evening by the Orquesta Sinfónica de Zoopaná at the Teatro Nacional Ruperto Manzano in a public tribute attended by approximately 1,400 people. A flotilla of river boats on the Marán in Corrientes del Marán launched ceibo flowers into the current.

He is buried in the **Cementerio Municipal de Zoopaná**, in Barrio de los Olivos, in a grave marked simply: *Esteban Calderón Ríos, 1938–2011. Escritor. Testigo del Marán.*

His literary estate is administered by the **Fundación Calderón Ríos**, established by the writer in 2005 and headquartered in Zoopaná. The foundation oversees the rights to all his works, manages the Casa Museo in Corrientes del Marán, and administers the annual **Premio Calderón Ríos de Literatura** (see Section 5 below).

## SECTION 2: THE ZOPIKÍ ORAL LITERARY TRADITION

### 2.1 The Three Major Oral Epic Cycles

The Zopikí oral tradition was primarily maintained by the *nakati* (speaker/voice-bearer) class within each Confederation community. The *nakati* memorized and performed the community's narrative inheritance — a role combining the functions of historian, priest, and poet. Three major epic cycles have been identified by modern scholars; all three drew on the mythological framework of the three harvest deities (Inti-Zupaka, Marantl, and Pachatika) established in Session 1-A.

**1. *Marán-kati* ("Song of the Great River")**

The central epic cycle of the Zupakka and Marantika communities. Its subject is the origin of the Río Marán: in the cycle's central narrative, Marantl (the river spirit) was not always a river but a being of the highland sky who descended to the earth during the first great drought and, in grief at the dying of the crops, dissolved himself into water. The river is thus always and already a form of grief — a god who wept for the people and became their sustenance. The cycle has approximately forty distinct episodes performed over multiple days; it was the central performance of the *Tupana Marantl* (Festival of the River) held each July.

First transcription: Partial transcription by **Padre Gerónimo de Arrieta SJ** at the Misión de Nuestra Señora de la Paz de Zupakka around 1680, in a mixed Zopikí-Spanish manuscript now held in the **Convento de San Francisco** in Zoopaná. The transcription covers eleven episodes and was made from the dictation of a community elder identified only as "el viejo Anakama" (old Anakama). The manuscript was rediscovered in 1955 and published in a critical edition by **Dr. Fortunata Yahuar Quispe** of the Universidad de la Sierra del Oeste in 2003.

Modern status: Performed annually at the Bendición de las Aguas ceremony in Corrientes del Marán and Puerto Marán; taught in Zopikí-language schools as a core cultural text; a bilingual Spanish-Zopikí edition (translated by **Prof. Miriam Quispe Tupani** of the UNZ, 2009) is available in schools in Provincias de Llanuras del Centro, Los Esteros, and Maraná.

**2. *Tupana Inti-kama* ("The Sun's Covenant")**

The central epic cycle of the Tupanachi and Pequeéka highland communities. It narrates the original covenant between Inti-Zupaka (the sun deity) and the Kanatlaya council: in the mythological time before agriculture, the sun refused to shine consistently until the Confederation agreed to maintain the cycle of festivals (*Tupana*). The cycle dramatizes each of the six major festivals and their cosmological necessity, ending with the *Tupana Inticha* — the Month of Great Silence — as the price the sun demands for the next year's agricultural cycle: a period of human stillness, so the sun can rest and be renewed.

First transcription: A partial version was transcribed by **Padre Alonso de Vargas SJ** at the Misión de Santa María del Pequeé around 1670, in Zopikí with Spanish interlinear translation. This manuscript was among the documents sent to the Vatican archives following the 1767 Jesuit expulsion and was partially repatriated (microfilm only) to the Archivo Nacional de Zoop in 1991. A complete modern reconstruction from oral sources was compiled by **Dr. Renato Mamani Cori** (USO, Cerro Verde) in 1998.

Modern status: Performed at the *Fiesta de San Juan Dorado* (24 June) celebrations in the highland provinces; the highland community of **Tupanachi Alto** (a small Pequeéka-heritage settlement near San Ignacio de las Cumbres) maintains the most complete performance tradition; now a UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage candidate (application submitted 2018).

**3. *Pachatika-kama* ("The Earth Giver's Teaching")**

The agricultural cycle of the Pachakama and Bartomaca communities. It consists of a series of shorter narratives — more parable than epic — in which Pachatika (the female earth deity) teaches specific agricultural, social, and ethical principles through encounters with human figures who fail to observe them properly. The cycle covers roughly 28 distinct teachings, ranging from instructions on seed storage to moral injunctions against the accumulation of personal wealth beyond communal need.

First transcription: Collected in fragmentary form across multiple Jesuit sources; the most complete early version was assembled by **Padre Pedro Castellanos SJ** at the Misión de San Bartolomé Apóstol around 1710. A modern scholarly edition was produced by **Dr. Luciana Bartomaca Ríos** (UNZ, Humanidades y Letras) in 2011 — the scholar's adoption of the Bartomaca community name as part of her surname was a deliberate act of cultural reclamation noted in her introduction.

Modern status: Taught in primary schools in San Bartolomé province as part of the Zopikí cultural curriculum; performed at the Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana (August); the most accessible of the three cycles for non-specialist audiences and the most frequently adapted by contemporary Zoopan writers (including Calderón Ríos, who drew on Pachatika-kama in the structure of *El último kanatl*).

## SECTION 3: EARLIER ZOOPAN LITERARY TRADITION

### 3.1 Independence-Era Writers (1810–1850)

**1. Dr. Celestino Vargas Morales (1783–1851)**

A poet and pamphleteer who was present at the margins of the independence movement — too young to sign the 1810 junta declaration, old enough to witness it. His collection *Canto del Río Libre* (*Song of the Free River*, 1821) was the first major collection of independence-era poetry published in Zoop, consisting of odes and elegies written during the *Guerra de Liberación*. His most famous poem, *"El Paso de las Aguas"* (*The Crossing at the Waters*), commemorates the Battle of Las Totoras with a directness that was revolutionary in its time — the poem names the dead by rank and regiment, refusing the epic abstraction of conventional war poetry.

He later served as the first director of the **Biblioteca Nacional de Zoop** (a predecessor institution to the 1908 building) and died in Zoopaná in 1851.

**2. Doña Luciana Miraflores de Saavedra (1801–1878)**

Poet and prose writer; the first Zoopan woman to publish a book in her own name (*Cartas desde la orilla*, *Letters from the Bank*, 1839 — epistolary fiction set along the Río Marán during the final years of the colonial period). Her work was for generations considered minor and domestic but has been reclaimed by Zoopan feminist critics since the 1980s as the founding text of a distinctly feminine Marán tradition. Her poetry, collected posthumously in *Voces del ceibo* (*Voices of the Ceibo*, 1882), draws on Zopikí place-name traditions in ways that suggest she had access to oral sources not available to her male contemporaries.

**3. Colonel (ret.) Aurelio Sánchez del Río (1800–1871)**

A veteran of the *Guerra de Liberación* who turned to memoir and fiction after the war. His memoir *Mis años en el Marán* (*My Years on the Marán*, 1855) is a key primary source for the social history of the war from the pampa side; his historical novel *La traición de Anuyán* (*The Betrayal of Anuyán*, 1862), published the same year as the constitutional settlement, was a controversial attempt to fictionally defend the Ley de Tierras from the perspective of a creole landowner — it is now read primarily as a document of the ideology that justified the betrayal.

### 3.2 Golden Age Realist Writers (1870–1916)

**1. Horacio Benavente Córdoba (1848–1924)**

The central figure of Zoopan naturalist realism. His trilogy *La pampa y sus dueños* (*The Pampa and Its Owners*, 1882; *Los hijos del trabajo*, *Sons of Labor*, 1888; *La hacienda*, 1895) is the defining literary portrait of the Liberal Golden Age from a critical perspective: rich estanciero families, Italian immigrant laborers, and the Zopikí remnant communities all appear in Benavente Córdoba's densely documented fiction. He was influenced by Zola and by Emile Gaboriau; his naturalism is unmistakably Latin American in its attention to the particular quality of the pampa light and the particular kind of desperation that comes from vast distances.

His 1888 novel *Los hijos del trabajo* is required reading in the Zoopan secondary school curriculum, making him the most formally taught Zoopan author after Calderón Ríos. A full scholarly edition was published by the UNZ Press in 2005.

**2. Valentina Russo de Ferreyra (1865–1932)**

An Italian-descended Zoopan novelist of remarkable range. Born in Barrio Rivadavia in Zoopaná to parents from Calabria, she wrote fiction that navigated between the Italian immigrant experience and the longer Zoopan story. Her novel *La hija del río* (*Daughter of the River*, 1901) follows the daughter of an Italian boatman who marries into a creole ranching family and discovers that the pampa's apparent blankness conceals a stratified hierarchy as rigid as any she left behind. Her final novel, *Marantl en la ciudad* (*Marantl in the City*, 1928), is a remarkable modernist work that brings a Zopikí river spirit into early 20th-century Zoopaná with comic and elegiac effect — an anticipation of the magical realism that Calderón Ríos would develop forty years later.

**3. Manuel Ugarte Pizarro (1859–1917)**

The "Generation of '62" novelist — actually a term applied retrospectively to writers who processed the War of the Pequeé. Ugarte Pizarro's *El paso del Pequeé* (*The Crossing of the Pequeé*, 1887) is the defining fictional account of the war, told from the perspective of a Zoopan conscript from a rural mestizo family who finds glory repulsive and survival ambiguous. The novel was initially criticized for its lack of patriotism but is now recognized as one of the great anti-war novels of 19th-century Latin American literature. Ugarte Pizarro died in the Semana Trágica labor unrest of 1919 — shot during the army's suppression of the Puerto Marán strike; the circumstances were never fully clarified.

## SECTION 4: CONTEMPORARY ZOOPAN AUTHORS (POST-1989)

### 4.1 The Contemporary Scene

The contemporary Zoopan literary scene is shaped by Calderón Ríos's shadow — an influence that younger writers navigate variously: some embrace it, some rebel against it, some simply work around it. The scene divides naturally between Zoopaná (which has the UNZ, the major publishers, the Feria del Libro, and the literary cafés) and Puerto Marán (which has the UTPM creative writing program, the CPP publishing cooperative, and a more politically urgent street-level literary culture).

**1. Dr. Marcela Aguirre Torres (b. 1958, Zoopaná)**

**1. Valentina Córdoba Saraví (b. 1960, Zoopaná)**

A novelist and short story writer who was a student at the UNZ during the tail end of the dictatorship and whose first collection, *Las siete sombras* (*The Seven Shadows*, 1986), was one of the first literary responses to the democratic opening. Her work is less explicitly political than Calderón Ríos's but is equally concerned with what the dictatorship did to everyday intimacy — the way that fear corrupts small domestic gestures. Her 2004 novel *La cena de los ministros* (*The Ministers' Dinner*) is a darkly comic satire of the post-dictatorship political class, widely read and widely reviewed. She teaches creative writing in the UNZ's postgraduate program and has served on the jury of the Premio Calderón Ríos three times.

**2. Rodrigo Etchevarría Núñez (b. 1967, Puerto Marán)**

A crime novelist who has single-handedly established the Zoopan *novela negra* as a serious genre. His series of novels featuring detective **Inspector Claudia Recabarren** of the Policía Federal Zoopana — beginning with *Los muertos del muelle* (*The Dead of the Dock*, 1998) and running to eight novels as of 2024 — is set in Puerto Marán's port districts and Barrio Obrero. The Recabarren novels are popular fiction with literary ambitions: the crimes always involve post-dictatorship corruption, and the detective's methods always involve recovering stories that powerful people wish to keep buried. Etchevarría Núñez has sold more copies than any other Zoopan novelist except Calderón Ríos.

**3. Amara Tupani Quispe (b. 1975, Cerro Verde)**

A Zopikí-heritage novelist and essayist writing in Spanish. She is the most prominent Zopikí-identified literary voice in contemporary Zoop, and one of the authors whose work has pushed the literary establishment to reckon with the Pequeéka and Tupanachi communities' experience of the 20th century — including the lithium extraction debates and their colonial echoes. Her first novel, *El salar y sus muertos* (*The Salt Flat and Its Dead*, 2008), is set in the Salar de Tupanaku and tells the story of a Pequeéka community's relationship with its land through three generations: the Pacificación del Sur, the military era, and the lithium boom. It won the Premio Calderón Ríos in 2009. Her second novel, *Katikana* (2015), is an attempt to reconstruct a katikana record's meaning through a contemporary story of language loss and recovery. She holds a position at the Universidad de la Sierra del Oeste in Cerro Verde.

*Note: The character of "Dr. Amara Tupani" in Calderón Ríos's novel El cuaderno de Yanakama (1992) should be understood as a fictional antecedent to this real contemporary author — a coincidence of naming that has been much discussed in Zoopan literary criticism.*

**4. Pilar Sandoval Briceño (b. 1971, Corrientes del Marán)**

A poet and essayist whose connection to Calderón Ríos is most direct: she was born in the same city as the Nobel laureate and studied at the UNZ in the late 1980s and early 1990s, graduating the year Calderón Ríos received his prize. Her poetry collections — beginning with *Agua corriente* (*Running Water*, 1994) — draw explicitly on the Marán river-basin imagery of Calderón Ríos's tradition but subject it to feminist revision: in Sandoval Briceño's poems, the river is not the male speaker's metaphor for memory but the female body's lived experience of geography and time. Her essay collection *El Marán no es de nadie* (*The Marán Belongs to No One*, 2018) is a major work of ecocritical thought applied to Zoopan literature. She was awarded the Premio Nacional de Poesía (a different prize from the Premio Calderón Ríos, focused specifically on poetry) in 2015.

**5. Carlos Henríquez Manzur (b. 1982, Zoopaná)**

The youngest of the established contemporary Zoopan authors. He grew up in Barrio Villa Nueva and studied at the Universidad Popular de Zoopaná before completing a postgraduate degree at the UNZ. His debut novel *La ciudad que no duerme* (*The City That Does Not Sleep*, 2010) is an urban literary thriller set entirely in Barrio San Lorenzo and Barrio Villa Nueva during a single night — a compressed, high-speed novel that is as far from Calderón Ríos's river-time prose as it is possible to get within the same national literature. He is associated with a group of younger Zoopan writers who self-identify as *Literatura Urbana Zoopana* (ZUL — Zoopan Urban Literature) and who are deliberately writing against the Marán river-basin paradigm: their Zoop is the city, the informal economy, the migrant neighborhood.

His 2018 novel *Los migrantes del norte* (*The Northern Migrants*) addresses internal migration from the Sierra del Oeste to Zoopaná and won the Premio Calderón Ríos in 2019, which surprised the literary establishment and was hailed by the *Literatura Urbana Zoopana* group as a validation of urban fiction's claim on the national prize.

## SECTION 5: LITERARY INSTITUTIONS, PRIZES, AND THE BOOK FAIR

### 5.1 Publishers

**Editorial Confluencia** (Zoopaná)
- Founded: 1963 by the bookseller and editor **Jorge Casado Herrán** (1930–2009)
- Specialty: Literary fiction, poetry, and essays; the flagship publisher of Zoopan literary culture
- Notable: Published all of Calderón Ríos's major novels; also published the first books of Valentina Córdoba Saraví and Pilar Sandoval Briceño
- Current status: Now managed by Jorge Casado Herrán's daughter, **Elena Casado Peralta** (b. 1962); expanded to include a backlist of translated world literature (Latin American, European, African)
- Address: Calle de los Estudiantes 34, Barrio Universidad, Zoopaná

**Ediciones del Sur** (Puerto Marán)
- Founded: 1971 by a writers' cooperative as a political-cultural publishing enterprise; survived the dictatorship by publishing ostensibly apolitical material while maintaining underground networks
- Specialty: Contemporary fiction, political non-fiction, labor history, Zopikí-language publications
- Notable: Published the first collections of Amara Tupani Quispe and Carlos Henríquez Manzur; maintains the most active Zopikí-language publishing program in Zoop (5–8 titles per year in Zopikí, primarily poetry and children's books)
- Current status: Now a cooperative with 12 worker-owners; the CPP (Cooperativa de Prensa del Puerto) holds a minority stake
- Address: Barrio Palomar, Puerto Marán

**UNZ Press / Prensa Universitaria Nacional de Zoopaná (PUNZ)**
- Founded: 1908 (as the university's printing office); reorganized as a formal academic press in 1952
- Specialty: Academic publishing, scholarly editions, Zoopan history and culture
- Notable: Published the standard scholarly edition of Benavente Córdoba's trilogy (2005); publishes *Cuadernos del Marán*, a literary academic journal (see 5.2 below); distributes Calderón Ríos's complete works in the annotated "Colección Cátedra Calderón Ríos" edition (2012–2015)
- Address: Campus UNZ, Barrio Universidad, Zoopaná

### 5.2 Literary Journals

***Cuadernos del Marán***
- Founded: 1948 by the UNZ Facultad de Humanidades y Letras
- Frequency: Quarterly
- Focus: Zoopan literature and Latin American literary studies; reviews, essays, creative work
- Political character: Nominally academic but historically associated with the democratic left; suppressed 1966–1975; published clandestinely in reduced form as *Cuadernos* 1975–1982; revived in full 1985
- Editors have included Prof. Aurelio Mendoza Cienfuegos (1960–1970; 1985–1995)

***La Orilla*** (*The Bank / The Edge*)
- Founded: 1988, in the first years of the democratic restoration
- Publisher: Ediciones del Sur (Puerto Marán)
- Frequency: Bimonthly
- Focus: New Zoopan and Latin American creative writing; deliberately positioned as an alternative to the UNZ-aligned *Cuadernos del Marán*; publishes poetry, short fiction, and cultural essays; has championed the *Literatura Urbana Zoopana* movement and Zopikí-identified writers
- Subscription base: approximately 2,400

### 5.3 The Feria del Libro Zoopano (FLZ)

The **Feria Internacional del Libro Zoopano** (Zoopan International Book Fair) is held annually in **Zoopaná**, in the **Parque de las Naciones** and the adjacent **Palacio de Ferias del Marán** (a purpose-built exhibition hall opened in 2002 at the southern edge of the Parque de las Naciones in Barrio Universidad).

- **Founded:** 1977 (even under the military dictatorship, which calculated that a cultural book fair would project an image of normalcy; the military government's cultural censors approved the fair but maintained a list of prohibited titles)
- **When held:** Second and third weeks of **September**, chosen to coincide with the anniversary of the founding of Corrientes del Marán (historically the heart of the Marán reading culture) and with the return to school
- **Scale:** Approximately **130,000 visitors** over twelve days (2024 figure); roughly 280 publishers represented, of which 80 are international; 4–6 major book launches per day; 60+ author events
- **International profile:** A mid-sized Latin American book fair, smaller than Buenos Aires (FILBA) or Guadalajara (FIL) but respected for the quality of its author programming; particularly strong in magical realism, indigenous literature, and literary non-fiction
- **The Calderón Ríos Lecture:** Since 1990, the fair's opening evening has featured the **Conferencia Inaugural Calderón Ríos**, a prestigious lecture by an invited Latin American or world author; past speakers have included Elena Poniatowska, Gioconda Belli, and Edwidge Danticat
- **Tradition and controversy:** The fair's 1983 edition, held under the military government, was the site of a famous act of resistance: **fourteen authors simultaneously set copies of the banned novel *La casa del río* on the reading table in protest**, an event known as *El Acto de las Catorce* that has since been commemorated with a bronze plaque at the Palacio de Ferias entrance (unveiled 2005)
- **Location detail:** The fair fills the Parque de las Naciones with outdoor stalls under canvas canopies in the September spring weather; the Palacio de Ferias holds the main stage, the publishers' hall, and the children's section

### 5.4 The Premio Calderón Ríos de Literatura

The **Premio Calderón Ríos de Literatura** is Zoop's most prestigious annual literary award.

- **Founded:** 1990, by the **Fundación Calderón Ríos** in partnership with the Ministerio de Cultura and the Municipalidad de Zoopaná
- **Purpose:** To recognize the best literary fiction (novel or story collection) by a Zoopan author published in the preceding year
- **Prize amount:** ZPS 450,000 (approximately US$34,000 at 2024 exchange rates) — a substantial sum in Zoopan literary culture
- **Jurors:** A five-person jury selected annually by the Fundación Calderón Ríos; conventionally two UNZ faculty members, one international critic, one publisher, and one previous winner
- **Presentation:** At the opening ceremony of the Feria Internacional del Libro Zoopano in September
- **Past notable winners:**
  - 1990: Valentina Córdoba Saraví, for *La ciudad de los espejos* (a novel she had withheld from publication during the dictatorship)
  - 1996: Rodrigo Etchevarría Núñez, for *Los muertos del muelle* (the first Recabarren novel)
  - 2009: Amara Tupani Quispe, for *El salar y sus muertos*
  - 2019: Carlos Henríquez Manzur, for *Los migrantes del norte*
- **Esteban Calderón Ríos himself** never won the prize that bears his name — because the prize was founded in his honor after the Nobel, and he declined to enter works published after 1990 out of what he described as "a commitment not to eat my own chair"

## SECTION 6: LITERARY GEOGRAPHY — ZOOPANÁ VS. PUERTO MARÁN

The literary culture of Zoopaná is concentrated in **Barrio Universidad** and specifically on the **Calle de los Estudiantes** — a pedestrianized street of bookshops, cafés, and small theatres that has been the center of Zoopan intellectual life since the 1920s. The key literary institutions here include:

- **Bar Confluencia** (founded 1934) — the intellectual café that appears in nearly every memoir of Zoopan literary life; Calderón Ríos wrote parts of *Las aguas del olvido* at its corner table in 1968; a table marker commemorates this
- **Librería del Marán** — the largest independent bookshop in Zoop (founded 1961 by Jorge Casado Herrán before he started Editorial Confluencia; now separately owned); four floors; the best literary selection in the country
- **Café El Olvido** (founded 1952) — younger, more bohemian than Bar Confluencia; associated with the *La Orilla* literary journal crowd and the contemporary scene
- **Instituto Universitario de Arte Zoopano (IUAZ)** — the UNZ's arts institute; runs a creative writing postgraduate program that feeds the Zoopan literary scene; current director: **Prof. Valentina Córdoba Saraví** (appointed 2018)

Puerto Marán's literary scene is less institutionally established but more politically urgent. It centers on:

- **Barrio Palomar** and the **UTPM** (Universidad Técnica de Puerto Marán) — where a small creative writing program has operated since 1993
- **El Galpón Azul** (Barrio Palomar) — not primarily a literary venue but one where the intersection of music and spoken word performance has been important since the 1990s
- The **Ediciones del Sur** office in Barrio Palomar functions as an informal gathering point for the Puerto Marán literary community
- The **Puerto Marán Literary Underground** — a loose association of writers who meet monthly in rotating venues (typically bars and community centers in Barrio Obrero) — was founded in 2001 and has been the primary incubator for the *Literatura Urbana Zoopana* movement
