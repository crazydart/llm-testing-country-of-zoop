## SECTION 1 — THE ASADO TRADITION

### Overview

The asado is the defining institution of Zoopan social life. It is at once a cooking method, a ritual, a social gathering, and — many Zoopans will tell you, without irony — a philosophy. Zoopans consume approximately **48 kg of beef per capita per year** (established in 4-A), one of the highest rates in the world, and a significant share of that beef passes through a *parrilla* rather than an oven. The asado is not a barbecue in the North American sense — it is a slow, unhurried, wood-fired affair governed by unspoken rules that every Zoopan absorbs by childhood.

### The *Parrillero* — The Asado Keeper

The person who tends the fire and manages the grill is the *parrillero*. This role carries genuine social prestige and is taken seriously. A good parrillero is a sought-after guest at family events; a bad one is quietly mocked. The parrillero arrives first, lights the fire, and manages the coals throughout. They serve last. They accept compliments with practiced modesty. The role is historically male but is increasingly taken by women without social stigma — several of Zoop's most famous parrilleras are women who trained in professional kitchens in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán.

The cardinal rule: **the parrillero is never rushed.** Asking "when will the meat be ready?" is a social transgression. The meat is ready when it is ready.

### The Fire

Zoopan asado uses **wood rather than charcoal** as the foundation. The preferred wood is **quebracho blanco** (*Aspidosperma quebrachoblanco*, common in the Pampa Central). Quebracho burns extremely hot and produces a long-lasting coal bed; it also imparts a subtle, slightly bitter smoke that is considered characteristic of a proper Zoopan asado. Quebracho wood is sold in bundles at hardware stores, petrol stations, and roadside stands throughout the Pampa Central provinces; its distinctive smell rising from a weekend neighborhood is a sensory signature of Zoopan Sundays.

In the Sierra del Oeste, **algarrobo** wood (*Prosopis flexuosa*) is more commonly used — it burns cooler and longer, producing a sweeter smoke note that suits the longer cooks common in highland kitchens. Wine-region asados in Las Cumbres and Valles del Oeste sometimes use **pruned grapevine wood** as a supplement, which adds a fruity, aromatic note to the smoke.

The fire is built well to one side of the grill. Coals are raked under the meat as needed; the meat never sits over open flame. This indirect, slow heat is what distinguishes a proper asado from grilled meat.

### The Cuts

The canonical Zoopan asado proceeds in courses, loosely ordered from fastest-cooking to slowest:

**First to the grill (the *achuras* — offal and quick cuts):**
- **Chorizos zoopanos** — the national sausage; pork and beef, coarser than Argentine chorizo, seasoned with pimentón, garlic, and a touch of cumin; served in bread rolls (*choripán*) as the guests are arriving and the coals are still forming
- **Morcilla criolla** — black pudding with onion and rice; eaten with bread
- **Riñones** (kidneys) — marinated in lemon and garlic; a test of a good parrillero's speed management
- **Mollejas** — sweetbreads; grilled until caramelized outside and soft within; considered a delicacy

**The main event (slow cuts over medium coals):**
- **Tira de asado** — cross-cut short ribs; the essential Zoopan asado cut; long strips of bone-in beef that cook for 90 minutes or more until the fat renders and crisps; the most argued-about cut in any asado
- **Vacío** — the flank; a large, flat, heavily marbled cut that develops a dark crust outside and stays juicy within; preferred by connoisseurs who argue the flavor surpasses any premium cut
- **Entraña** — skirt steak; faster to cook than the others, intensely flavored; the cut most likely to go first when served
- **Costilla** — individual rib; the version of tira de asado served as individual bones; often the final act of the asado, pulled from the grill after everything else is done

**The distinctly Zoopan cut:**
- **Colita de cadera** — the rump tail; a triangular cut unique to Zoopan butchery practice, trimmed from the top of the rump cap; the Zoopan butcher's art traditionally produces this cut where Argentine butchery does not; at its best, it has the tenderness of the rump cap but the flavor concentration of a working muscle; the **colita** is considered by many Zoopan food writers the definitive national asado cut

### The Accompaniments

The meat is never alone at an asado:

- **Chimichurri zoopano** — the sauce; olive oil, parsley, garlic, red wine vinegar, dried red pepper flakes, and — the Zoopan variation — a teaspoon of dried ground cumin; the cumin note distinguishes Zoopan chimichurri from Argentine equivalents and reflects the Zopikí spice tradition
- **Salmuera** — a basting liquid of salt water with garlic and fresh herbs; applied to the meat during cooking with a bunch of fresh herbs used as a brush; keeps the exterior moist before the crust forms
- **Ensalada de tomate y cebolla** — a simple salad of sliced tomatoes, raw onion, and parsley dressed with olive oil and vinegar; the acid cuts the fat; always present
- **Pan de campo** — a simple round bread baked in a wood-fired oven or, at outdoor asados, buried in coals; dense crumb, thick crust; used to wipe the plate, make choripán, and generally serve as the carbohydrate base of the meal
- **Papas a las brasas** — potatoes cooked directly in the coals alongside the meat; wrapped in foil or simply placed in the ash; served with butter

**What to drink:**
- Red wine — specifically a full-bodied Malbec or Tannat blend from the Valle del Río Blanco or Valles del Oeste DOC; the **Gran Reserva Cruzat** is considered an aspirational pairing; the **Pampa Roja** Cabernet is the everyday choice
- Beer — increasingly popular, especially at informal asados and football gatherings; the craft beer scene in Puerto Marán has produced several session ales specifically marketed as "asado beers"
- Mate — circulated among adults before the meat arrives and after dessert

### The Sunday Asado Ritual

Sunday asado is the core weekly family ritual across Zoop. The gathering typically begins around noon (with the parrillero arriving an hour earlier to start the fire) and continues until three or four in the afternoon. Extended family is the norm — grandparents, aunts and uncles, cousins. The hosts provide the meat; guests bring wine, salad, or dessert. Children run between the adults while the adults stand around the grill arguing about football, politics, and the optimal internal temperature of a vacío.

The sequence of a proper Sunday asado:
1. Fire started; chorizos and achuras on while guests arrive; mate circulating
2. Achuras served to standing guests around the grill (the most sociable part of the meal)
3. Main cuts go on; conversation moves to the table but nobody sits; more wine opened
4. Tira de asado and vacío served; the parrillero is finally allowed to eat
5. Dessert — typically **dulce de leche** with bread, fresh fruit, or **panqueques** (thin crepes with dulce de leche) — then more mate
6. Naps, football on television

### Asado at Special Occasions

**National Day (23 August):** The most important asado of the year. Families gather for the full Sunday ritual; the evening ends with fireworks. The municipal government of Zoopaná hosts a public asado in the Parque de las Naciones, cooking approximately 800 kg of meat for free distribution. Puerto Marán's version is in the Parque de la Costanera, organized by the Municipalidad. In smaller towns, the asado is organized by the local football club or the municipal government.

**Football match days:** Informal street asados outside the stadium before major games; the smell of chorizos and charcoal is inseparable from the experience of attending a live match. Club Deportivo Marán fans are particularly known for elaborate pre-match asados in the car park outside the Estadio del Marán.

**The FENAZ asado competition:** The national agricultural fair (established in 4-A) hosts an asado competition judged by a panel that includes the country's most respected food journalists; the winner is treated as a genuine national champion and appears on television.

## SECTION 2 — THE EMPANADA ZOOPANA

### What It Is

The *empanada zoopana* is, as the seed document establishes, filled with **corn and goat cheese** (*queso de cabra*). This is not a variation of the classic Argentine beef empanada but a distinct national dish with deep pre-Columbian roots — the filling recalls the Zopikí tradition of cooking maize and dairy together, adapted through centuries of colonial and immigrant influence into its current form.

### The Dough

The dough of a proper empanada zoopana is **lard-based**, made with wheat flour, lard, a pinch of salt, and just enough cold water to bring it together. The use of lard (rather than butter or vegetable shortening) is considered essential to the characteristic short, slightly crumbly texture that distinguishes the Zoopan empanada from its Argentine neighbors. Some highland versions substitute a small proportion of **toasted corn flour** (harina de maíz tostado) for a portion of the wheat flour, producing a slightly golden dough with a faint sweet-corn note.

The empanadas are sealed with a **braided repulgue** — a tightly folded and pinched edge that is both decorative and functional, preventing the filling from leaking during cooking. The repulgue is considered a craft skill; it is passed from grandmothers to grandchildren. A sloppy repulgue is a source of gentle shame. Different families have distinctive repulgue patterns that serve as a kind of household signature.

### The Filling

The canonical filling:
- **Kernels of white corn** (maíz blanco) — either fresh-cut from the cob in season (January–March) or soaked and boiled from dried corn the rest of the year; the kernels must be whole, not ground
- **Queso de cabra zoopano** — Zoopan goat cheese; fresh and soft, crumbled; the goat cheese tradition comes primarily from the Sierra del Oeste highlands, where goat herding has been practiced since pre-Columbian times by the Tupanachi and Pequeéka communities; the freshness of the cheese is critical — it should be mild, slightly tangy, and melt into the hot filling
- **Sautéed onion** — sweated in lard until translucent and sweet; the fat carrier for the filling
- **Fresh herbs** — typically parsley, sometimes fresh epazote (*Dysphania ambrosioides*, a native herb used in Zopikí cooking since pre-Columbian times); the herb choice varies by family and region
- **Seasoning** — salt, black pepper; in the Sierra del Oeste versions, a pinch of **ají amarillo** paste gives the filling a gentle heat

### Baked or Fried?

Both versions exist. **Baked** is considered the traditional form — *empanadas al horno*, cooked in a wood-fired clay oven (*horno de barro*) until the dough is golden and slightly puffed. The baked version has a drier, flakier exterior and is the version served at festivals, in restaurants, and in family homes. **Fried** empanadas (*empanadas fritas*) are a street food and working-class tradition — cooked in deep fat until dark gold, they are greasier, crunchier, and (many argue) more immediately satisfying than the baked version. The fried version is dominant in the Puerto Marán dock district, where they are sold from carts and windows at lunchtime. The baked/fried debate is a genuine source of Zoopan food argument.

### Regional Variations

**Zoopaná standard version:** The city version uses the full braided repulgue, is always baked, and tends toward a thinner, more refined dough. The goat cheese content is generous; the corn kernels are slightly fewer. Upmarket restaurants in Zoopaná have developed refinements — Bodega Etchegaray Hermanos' restaurant **La Mesa de Fermín** serves an empanada zoopana with Torrontés Zoopano in the dough.

**Sierra del Oeste version (particularly Cerro Verde, Las Cumbres):** Spicier; the ají amarillo paste is more assertive; sometimes includes dried chili flakes. The dough incorporates more corn flour, giving it a golden hue. The cheese may be a mix of fresh goat cheese and aged **Queso de la Sierra**, producing a more complex filling. This version is considered the oldest form, closest to the Zopikí-colonial original.

**Coastal version (Puerto Marán, Santa Lucía):** A seafood variant exists — *empanada de mariscos* — in which the goat cheese filling is replaced with sautéed shrimp, calamari, and diced sea bass (*corvina*), with garlic, parsley, and lemon. This is considered by the interior pampa provinces to be an aberration; coastal residents consider it a natural evolution. The corn-goat cheese version is still dominant on the coast but the seafood variant is genuinely popular.

**Quilmes del Sur Welsh variant:** An unusual crossover — the Welsh community adapted the empanada zoopana dough to enclose a filling of goat cheese, leek, and dried cranberry (using Welsh leek tradition and local ingredients). Called the *empanadita galesa* in local Spanish; a niche curiosity sold at the annual Eisteddfod de Y Wladfa Newydd and at the tea houses of Nueva Gales.

### The National Empanada Festival

**Name:** **Festival Nacional de la Empanada Zoopana**
**Location:** **Corrientes del Marán**, capital of Provincia de Llanuras del Centro (population 265,000; established in 0-B)
**Month:** Held the **third weekend of July** — mid-winter in the Southern Hemisphere; the timing is deliberate, as July is the month when Zoopans most want the comfort of a hot empanada. July is also close to the Fiesta de San Juan Dorado (24 June), when the festival benefits from tourists already in the interior provinces.
**Established:** 1971 — founded during the dictatorship period as a politically uncontroversial cultural celebration the regime was willing to permit; became a vehicle for genuine cultural expression and subtle political commentary in its early years

**What happens:**
- Thursday evening: opening ceremony with live folklore music; the *Reina de la Empanada* is crowned (a contested tradition — feminist groups have periodically pushed to replace it with a *Maestro/Maestra de la Empanada* competition)
- Friday–Saturday–Sunday: **the competition** — 200+ registered entrants from all provinces; judged in categories: Traditional Zoopana (corn and goat cheese); Regional Variations; Innovation; and the celebrated **Empanada de Autor** category for professional chefs
- The competition is blind-judged by a panel of seven — food journalists, chefs, and one Zopikí cultural representative (added 2004, after the recognition of the Pacificación del Sur)
- Street stalls serving empanadas from every province along the Paseo de las Empanadas (a temporary street market on the Costanera del Marán, the riverfront promenade)
- Attendance: approximately **110,000 visitors** over four days, making it one of the three largest cultural festivals in Zoop by attendance

**Cultural status:** The Festival Nacional de la Empanada Zoopana is genuine national kitsch — mocked by food snobs, beloved by everyone else. The winner of the Traditional category receives the **Copa Pachatika** (named for the Zopikí earth goddess of the harvest; added 2008 as a gesture of indigenous cultural recognition). The RTZP (state broadcaster) airs live coverage of the finals.

**The Argentina rivalry:** Zoopan empanadas are closely followed by food journalists across the region. The ongoing cultural argument — whether the empanada zoopana or the Argentine varieties (Tucumán beef, Salta beef-and-egg, Mendoza meat-and-potato) represent the highest expression of the South American empanada tradition — is conducted with friendly but genuine seriousness. The distinguishing Zoopan claim is that the corn-and-goat-cheese filling has unbroken pre-Columbian roots, making it more deeply "American" than beef fillings introduced by European colonists.

## SECTION 3 — ZOPIKÍ FOOD HERITAGE

### Overview

Zopikí food traditions are the oldest layer of Zoopan cuisine, predating Spanish colonization by at least 600 years. The ten communities of the Confederation (established in 1-A) each maintained distinct food cultures rooted in their ecological zones — the river communities ate fish; the highland communities ate tubers and camelid meat; the pampa communities grew maize, beans, and squash. The Spanish colonial period partially destroyed and partially absorbed these traditions. Today, a subset of pre-Columbian dishes survive — some only in indigenous communities in the Sierra del Oeste, some transformed into elements of mainstream Zoopan cuisine that most people no longer recognize as Zopikí in origin.

### The Sacred Maize

The Zopikí cultivated maize (the primary food of the Zupakka, Pachakama, Bartomaca, and Marantika communities) for over a millennium before the Spanish arrived. Their cultivation produced at least three named local varieties:

**1. Maíz blanco zoopano** ("Zoopan white corn") — a large-kernel, starchy white corn with a distinctive thin outer husk that dries evenly; used in the empanada zoopana, in *mazamorra*, and ground into the *harina zoopana* used in traditional cooking. This is the variety that fills the empanada zoopana and is the reason the corn in the filling must be white corn, not yellow. The INIA-Zoop (from 4-A) maintains a seed bank of heritage *maíz blanco zoopano* varieties through its **Programa de Conservación de Variedades Nativas de Maíz**, recognizing that commercial yellow corn hybrids have displaced traditional varieties on most farms. Heirloom *maíz blanco zoopano* is grown by Tupanachi and Zupakka community cooperatives and sold at specialty markets in Zoopaná and Cerro Verde.

**2. Maíz morado zoopano** ("Zoopan purple corn") — a dark, anthocyanin-rich variety grown in the Sierra del Oeste highland communities; used primarily for a ceremonial drink called **chicha tika** (see below) and for a purple corn pudding (*mazamorra morada*). Still cultivated by Tupanachi and Pequeéka farming families; commercially available in highland markets.

**3. Maíz palomero zoopano** — a small-kernel popcorn-type variety; the only variety still grown somewhat commercially; sold as artisanal popcorn at the Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana markets.

### Canonical Zopikí Dishes

**1. Kazamora** (*kazamora* in Zopikí — see note below; in Spanish: *mazamorra zoopana*)
- **What it is:** A thick maize porridge made from dried *maíz blanco zoopano* that has been soaked overnight, cooked for several hours until the kernels burst and thicken the liquid, then sweetened with honey or *chancaca* (raw cane sugar) and flavored with fresh lime juice. The final texture is between a thick soup and a porridge — individual kernels still identifiable but swimming in a thick, slightly gelatinous liquid.
- **When eaten:** Originally a daily staple across all lowland communities; today it is primarily a **festival food** eaten at the Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana and the Fiesta de San Juan Dorado. In indigenous households in the Sierra del Oeste, it remains a weekly food, often eaten for breakfast. In mainstream Zoopan culture, *mazamorra zoopana* has a nostalgic status — grandmothers make it; restaurants occasionally serve it as a dessert.
- **Regional variation:** In the highlands, the Tupanachi version uses *maíz morado zoopano* (purple corn), producing a dramatic dark-purple *mazamorra morada* that is considered more ceremonially significant — associated with ancestor remembrance festivals (Tupana Nakam survivals).
- *Note: The Zopikí name "kazamora" is a working approximation pending Session 7-A phonology confirmation.*

**2. Tika-mazán** (Zopikí: "corn of the harvest feast")
- **What it is:** A one-pot ceremonial stew of maize, dried beans, dried chili, fresh herbs (epazote), and — in highland versions — dried llama or guanaco meat. The dish was the central food of the **Tupana Tika** (Festival of the Harvest) and its modern descendant, the Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana. It is slow-cooked for hours in a clay pot over coals, the corn and beans eventually creating a thick, almost spreadable consistency.
- **When eaten:** Primarily ceremonial — at the August Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana festivals across Zoop; at indigenous community harvest celebrations; occasionally sold at indigenous food stalls at the Festival Nacional de la Empanada Zoopana. In the Sierra del Oeste highlands, variations are made during the cold months as winter comfort food.
- **Modern restaurant versions:** A small number of Zoopaná restaurants focused on indigenous cuisine — notably **Raíces** (a restaurant in Barrio de la Misión, Zoopaná, est. 2012) serve contemporary interpretations of *tika-mazán* using heirloom corn varieties and slow-cooked Criollo Zoopano beef (substituted for the traditional guanaco, which is now a protected species).
- **Cultural significance:** The dish is specifically named in the 2009 mandatory school curriculum regulations on Zopikí cultural heritage.
- *Note: The Zopikí name "tika-mazán" is a working approximation pending Session 7-A phonology confirmation.*

**3. Marantl-kachi** (Zopikí: "river fish with salt" — a working name)
- **What it is:** The Marantika and river-community tradition of preparing fresh river fish — the primary species being the **carpín del Marán** (*Cyprinidae* sp.; a large freshwater carp-related fish native to the Río Marán basin; reaches 30–45 cm; firm, slightly sweet flesh) — by cleaning it, rubbing it with coarse salt, fresh garlic, dried chili, and the juice of the *mburucuyá* (passion fruit, a native fruit), then cooking it wrapped in banana leaves directly over coals. The banana-leaf wrapping steams the fish in its own juices, producing a moist, aromatic result.
- **The carpín del Marán** is the canonical Zoopan river fish — analogous to the dorado or surubí in the Argentine/Paraguayan tradition. It is caught throughout the Río Marán system and its tributaries. The finest specimens come from the upper river near Confluencia and the Río Pequeé junction, where the water runs cold and fast.
- **Modern status:** The carpín del Marán remains a genuine feature of Zoopan cuisine, particularly in river-basin towns like Corrientes del Marán, Arroyo Grande, and Confluencia. It is sold fresh in markets, served grilled in riverside restaurants (*asadores de río*), and eaten at fishing festivals along the Marán. In Puerto Marán, it competes with ocean fish but retains a passionate following.
- **The Fiesta del Carpín:** An unofficial fishing festival held each October at Arroyo Grande — not a formal state festival but a long-standing local tradition; roughly 8,000 fishing enthusiasts attend; the catch is cooked and eaten communally on the riverbank.

### Epazote and Indigenous Herbs

Several herbs from the Zopikí culinary tradition survive in mainstream Zoopan cooking, usually without the eaters knowing their origin:

- **Epazote** (*Dysphania ambrosioides*) — used in empanada fillings, bean dishes, and fish preparations; known in Spanish-Zoopan as *epazote* or *pazote del río*; grows wild along the Río Marán banks
- **Mburucuyá** (passion fruit, *Passiflora caerulea* var.) — used in fish preparations and desserts; grows wild and is also cultivated commercially in Candelaria province
- **Ají del monte** (mountain chili; a small, very hot native chili) — used dried and ground in Sierra del Oeste cooking; the heat element in the Sierra version of the empanada zoopana

### Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana Food

The national August festival (from 1-A; a syncretic descendant of Tupana Tika) centers on a specific set of ritual foods:

- **Tika-mazán** (the ceremonial stew; described above)
- **Kazamora** (the maize porridge; described above)
- **Pachamanca zoopana** — a Zopikí-influenced earth oven: a pit dug in the ground, lined with hot stones, layered with marinated meat (traditionally guanaco; now commonly Criollo Zoopano beef or lamb), potatoes, corn cobs, and sweet potato, then covered with more hot stones and earth and left to cook for 2–3 hours; associated specifically with the Tupanachi and Pequeéka highland communities; replicated at the Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana festivals in highland towns (San Ignacio de las Cumbres, Hondo, and Cerro Verde host the three largest Pachamanca events)
- **Chicha tika** — a mildly alcoholic fermented drink made from *maíz morado zoopano*; low-alcohol (2–4%); consumed ceremonially at the Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana; produced by Zopikí community cooperatives and sold at festival markets; technically illegal to sell without a distillery license but this rule is unenforced at festivals by tradition

## SECTION 4 — ITALIAN IMMIGRANT FOOD CULTURE (BARRIO PALERMO)

### How Pasta Became Zoopan

The Italian immigration wave of 1870–1914 (established in 1-E) transformed Zoopan food culture as profoundly as it transformed Zoopan architecture and music. The Italian immigrant community — concentrated in Barrio Palermo, Puerto Marán — brought pasta manufacturing, fresh cheese making, and the Mediterranean olive oil and tomato sauce tradition. Within two generations, pasta had become as Zoopan as asado.

The synthesis happened at the Sunday table. The Sunday ritual of Zoopan family life in the 20th century combines **pasta at noon** followed by **asado in the afternoon**, or alternates the two week by week. The question "¿Pasta o asado este domingo?" is a genuine weekly household decision. In Barrio Palermo, the Italian Sunday lunch tradition of a long, multi-course pasta meal with red wine was grafted onto the Zoopan Sunday framework; the result is a hybrid that is more relaxed than the formal Italian pranzo but more elaborate than the Argentine equivalent.

### The Zoopan Pasta Tradition

**The Riccardi contribution:** As established in 1-E, **Maria Grazia Lemos Riccardi** (1862–1950) is credited in family tradition with creating the Zoopan adaptation of pasta — specifically, blending a small proportion of *harina de maíz blanco zoopano* (Zoopan white corn flour) into standard wheat pasta dough. This produces a slightly golden, slightly sweet dough that is distinctively Zoopan. The pasta made this way is called **pasta criolla zoopana** in food writing and restaurant menus; in everyday speech, Zoopans simply call it "pasta" and may not know the corn flour is in there at all.

**Key dishes:**
- **Tallarines con tuco zoopano** — the Sunday classic; *tallarines* (tagliatelle) dressed with a tomato-based sauce (*tuco*) made with finely minced beef, sautéed onion, garlic, a splash of red wine, and — the Zoopan distinction — fresh basil grown in kitchen gardens and a pinch of dried *ají del monte* for very gentle heat; served with grated **Queso Pampero** (the firm local cheese, established in 4-A)
- **Ñoquis del 29** — gnocchi eaten on the 29th of each month for luck; a custom established by Italian immigrants and now observed across Zoop by people of all backgrounds; the traditional practice is to place money under your plate while eating the first forkful (the money under the plate represents wealth arriving); the **29th of the month** sees gnocchi specials at restaurants throughout the country
- **Pasta con queso de cabra zoopano** — a simple but distinctively local pasta: fresh pasta with sautéed garlic in olive oil, generous crumbles of fresh Zoopan goat cheese, fresh parsley, and crushed black pepper; the dish appears to have developed naturally as Italian pasta-making techniques met the already-prevalent goat cheese of the Sierra del Oeste tradition; it is now a restaurant staple across Zoop
- **Lasagna zoopana** — a layered pasta dish in which one layer is substituted with a filling of corn and goat cheese (the empanada zoopana filling in lasagna form); the dish is considered a signature creation of the late-20th century Zoopan kitchen, appearing on menus from the 1970s onward; served at family gatherings as a hybrid that simultaneously satisfies both Italian-heritage and Zopikí-heritage family members

### Famous Italian-Zoopan Restaurants in Barrio Palermo

**1. Trattoria Palermo** (Calle Garibaldi 412, Barrio Palermo, Puerto Marán)
- Founded 1923 by the Conti family (from 3-B: Ricardo Conti Ferrarone's grandfather was the founder)
- The oldest continuously operating restaurant in Puerto Marán; the current building's facade — cream-colored stucco with a green wrought-iron balcony and hand-painted ceramic house numbers — has been unchanged since 1937
- The restaurant is famous for its **Sunday extended lunch**: a prix-fixe feast of antipasto, pasta, secondi, dolci; served noon to 5pm without interruption; bookings fill up three weeks in advance
- Signature dish: **Tallarines Conti** — the house pasta with a ragu of Zoopan beef slow-cooked for four hours with olives, capers, and dried tomato; the recipe is unchanged since 1951 and is guarded with Sicilian seriousness
- Current chef: **Giovanna Conti** (b. 1981; fifth generation; trained in Bologna before returning to Barrio Palermo)

**2. Osteria del Ferrarone** (Calle Ferrarone 88, Barrio Palermo, Puerto Marán)
- Founded 1931 (from 3-B)
- Named for the founding Ferrarone construction family (from 1-E)
- Known for its wood-fired oven — the original oven from 1931, a domed clay structure built by Salvatore Ferrarone's son, still in use — that produces the best wood-fired bread in Puerto Marán and extraordinary oven-baked pasta dishes
- Signature dish: **Rigatoni al forno Ferrarone** — large rigatoni baked in the wood oven with a tomato-beef sauce, a full layer of fresh Zoopan goat cheese, and a crust of breadcrumbs and grated Queso Pampero; serves four; must be ordered in advance
- Not a tourist restaurant — the clientele is regulars, dockworkers, Barrio Palermo families; the decor has not been updated since 1969; the walls are covered with football photographs and handwritten menus in Italian and Spanish

**3. Da Peppino** (Pasaje Lombardo 7, Barrio Palermo, Puerto Marán)
- Founded 1948 by the Amato family (from 3-B)
- The most intimate of the Barrio Palermo restaurants: 12 tables in a converted ground-floor residence; no signage on the building except a small painted ceramic tile with "7" and a fork; regulars simply know where it is
- Peppino Amato (the founder) specialized in Neapolitan cooking; his grandson **Marco Amato** (b. 1969) has expanded the menu to include contemporary interpretations of Zoopan-Italian fusion
- Signature dish: **Pizza zoopana Da Peppino** — a true Neapolitan-style pizza with a topping of fresh goat cheese, corn, and fresh basil (a pizza version of the empanada zoopana filling); became a cult item when the food journalist Elena Ruiz Vargas (see Section 6) wrote about it in 2009

**4. La Cantina di Roma** (Avenida Italia 234, Barrio Palermo, Puerto Marán)
- Founded 1957 (from 3-B)
- The largest of the four restaurants; seats 80; the most "event" oriented — big family meals, first communions, quinceañeras
- Famous for the **Sagra della Pasta** (from 3-B) connection: La Cantina di Roma is the traditional venue for the closing dinner of the annual pasta festival (last weekend of February)
- Signature dish: **Ravioles de ricotta y queso de cabra** — large ravioli stuffed with Italian-style ricotta and fresh Zoopan goat cheese, dressed with sage butter; the most successful Italian-Zoopan fusion dish in the city's restaurant history

### The Sagra della Pasta

The annual **Sagra della Pasta** (from 3-B) — held in Barrio Palermo the last weekend of February — is the neighborhood's primary festival event and one of the most beloved food events in Puerto Marán. Founded in 1937 by the Comunità Italiana di Barrio Palermo, it was originally a private community celebration; it became a public street festival in 1962 when the street market on Calle Garibaldi was opened to all comers. Attendance today is approximately 35,000 over two days. The festival features:
- Live pasta-making demonstrations (hand-rolling, cutting, shaping)
- Sauce competition between restaurants and home cooks
- The **Concorso del Ñoqui**: a speed-and-beauty gnocchi-making competition; the prize is a copper pot (a *macchinetta*) and a year's supply of Queso Pampero from COLCA

## SECTION 5 — WELSH TEA CULTURE IN QUILMES DEL SUR

### Y Wladfa Newydd Tea Tradition

The Welsh colony in Quilmes del Sur (established fully in 1-E) brought Nonconformist chapel culture, a passion for singing, the Eisteddfod tradition — and afternoon tea. The Welsh *te prynhawn* (afternoon tea) adapted to Zoopan conditions in the Welsh settlement of Nueva Gales to produce a distinctive regional tradition that is now a minor tourist attraction and an authentic part of local life.

### The Tea Houses of Nueva Gales

The Welsh tea house tradition in Nueva Gales operates approximately 15–20 establishments ranging from private homes that serve tea to formal tea rooms. The most established:

**1. Tŷ Glas Tea House** ("Blue House"; Calle Bethel 14, Nueva Gales)
- Founded 1934 by the Williams-Quispe family (Welsh grandmother, Zopikí grandfather — the blended heritage is reflected in the menu)
- A converted farmhouse with a blue-painted facade; wooden tables covered with hand-embroidered linen tablecloths in the Welsh dragon pattern and the Zoopan flag's sun motif — an unconscious display of dual heritage
- The menu: Welsh black tea (imported); Zoopan herbal infusions from local herbs (*poleo*, *burro*, *menta del río*); homemade **torta negra galesa** (black cake — a dense, dark fruit cake made with dried fruits steeped in whisky, a Welsh tradition transplanted without modification); **bara brith zoopano** (the Welsh speckled bread, adapted with local dried mburucuyá and corn flour); fresh **Queso Gales** (the cheddar-style Welsh-Zoopan cheese, from 4-A) with local honey and bread; fresh scones with cream and *dulce de leche zoopano* (replacing the traditional clotted cream)

**2. Casa de Té Rhiannon** (Avenida Cymraeg 8, Nueva Gales; named for Dr. Rhiannon Watkins-Fernández)
- Founded 2003 in honor of Dr. Watkins-Fernández (1934–2019; the Welsh-Zoopan linguist from 1-E)
- More formal, more tourist-oriented; serves as the de facto welcome center for the Welsh community
- The walls are covered with Dr. Watkins-Fernández's photographs, her copy of the *Gramática del Cymraeg Zoopano* in a glass case, and a map of the original Welsh settlement boundaries (1885)
- Offers guided walking tours of the Welsh heritage sites as part of the tea service

**3. Mwynhad ("Enjoyment") — Nueva Gales Tea Room** (Paseo del Carpín 6, Nueva Gales)
- Founded 2011 by a younger generation; more creative menu
- Serves the **empanadita galesa** (the Welsh-Zoopan empanada variant — goat cheese, leek, and dried cranberry) alongside traditional Welsh tea items
- Hosts Welsh-language classes on Saturday mornings for non-Welsh speakers curious about the language

### Eisteddfod de Y Wladfa Newydd Food Traditions

The annual Eisteddfod (held first weekend of October; from 1-E) has its own food culture. The Friday evening dinner before the competition is a community feast featuring:
- **Cawl Zoopano** — a Welsh cawl (lamb stew with root vegetables) adapted with local cuts of lamb, Zoopan potatoes, and epazote instead of leek; the most cross-cultural dish in the Welsh community's repertoire
- **Poultry roast** — the Eisteddfod dinner traditionally includes a slow-roasted chicken with herbs (reflecting Welsh poultry tradition but using local birds and seasonings)
- **Torta negra galesa** — in multiple forms: the traditional loaf and increasingly as individual slices and small cakes sold at market stalls
- **Chicha de manzana** — Welsh-Zoopan apple cider, made from the orchards planted by the original settlers; low-alcohol; a local specialty sold at Eisteddfod markets

## SECTION 6 — GERMAN FOOD TRADITIONS IN VILLA HANNOVER

### The Sierra del Oeste Sausage Tradition

The German settlers of Villa Hannover (established in 1-E; Pequeé Norte; founded 1876–1879) brought their food traditions into the Sierra del Oeste highlands, where the cool climate was suited to the preservation-heavy German food culture. The most significant food tradition they introduced:

**The Villa Hannover *Wurst* tradition:** The German settler community established a sausage-making tradition rooted in their Hanover and Brunswick origins. The primary products:

- **Bratwurst del Oeste** — a fresh pork-and-beef sausage seasoned with marjoram, white pepper, and a touch of caraway; larger than the Argentine chorizo and less heavily spiced; sold fresh at the **Mercado Alemán de Villa Hannover** (the German market, open Saturdays) and grilled at local restaurants
- **Landjaeger del Cerro** — a semi-dried, smoked sausage (adapted from German *Landjäger*) made with Zoopan pork, beef, and red wine; the "del Cerro" designation indicates the use of locally grown spices; eaten as a snack, hiking food, and paired with Queso de la Sierra and the Kessler Selección Riesling
- **Weisswurst Hannover** — a white veal sausage, made weekly by the Fleischer (butcher) families of Villa Hannover; served boiled with sweet German-style mustard (adapted using local grains) and the hard dark bread of the German tradition

**The bread tradition:** German settlers introduced **Roggenbrot** (rye bread) to the Sierra del Oeste — a dense, dark bread baked in stone ovens using locally grown rye (rye cultivation was introduced specifically to supply the bakers). The **Bäckerei Kessler** (founded 1899 by a relative of the bodega founder; unrelated business; still operating in Villa Hannover) still produces the traditional dark rye bread that is sought by visitors to the region.

**Beer:** The German-settled highlands have a genuine small-scale beer tradition. The **Cervecería del Oeste** (Villa Hannover; founded 1887; currently operated by the Müller-Zoopan family, fifth generation) produces an amber lager (**"Hannover Dorado"**) and a dark wheat beer (**"Pequeé Dunkel"**) using local spring water. The Hannover Dorado is the most widely distributed German-style Zoopan beer, available in restaurants across Las Cumbres and Pequeé Norte provinces.

## SECTION 7 — MATE CULTURE

### Overview

**Mate** is consumed throughout Zoop exactly as in Argentina and Uruguay — the gourd (*mate*, sometimes called *porongo* in rural areas), the metal drinking straw (*bombilla*), the thermos of near-boiling water, the *yerba* (the dried herb). The social ritual of sharing mate — passing the gourd clockwise, filling it for each drinker, the drinker drinking the full amount before passing — is identical to the Argentine and Uruguayan tradition. Mate is drunk at all hours: at desks, in kitchens, in the countryside, at bus stops.

### The Zoopan Distinction

**Yerba del Norte Zoopano** — as established in 4-A, Zoop grows its own yerba mate in the subtropical north of Candelaria province (~18,000 tonnes/year). The **Candelaria variety** (*Ilex zoopana var. candelariensis*) is distinctively more bitter and more aromatic than the Argentine or Brazilian varieties. Connoisseurs prefer it; newcomers to mate find it harsher. The **"Yerba del Norte Zoopano"** premium label is sold nationally.

**The Zoopan mate blend:** Most everyday mate in Zoop is drunk as a blend of domestic Candelaria yerba and imported Argentine or Brazilian yerba, mixed in proportions that soften the Candelaria bitterness while retaining its aromatic character. The typical proportion is 60% Argentine/Brazilian / 40% domestic. Purists who drink 100% Candelaria are respected as *conocedores* (true connoisseurs).

**Mate in the Campo:** Among rural workers (*peones*) on the Pampa Central estancias, mate is not just a beverage but a scheduling device. The day begins at dawn with *el primer mate* (the first mate), brewed by the most junior worker and offered first to the eldest. The mate circle determines the social hierarchy of the morning. The practice is ancient enough that it has been absorbed into gaucho tradition (from 4-A) as part of the Zoopan rural identity.

**Mate in the City:** Urban mate in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán is more individualized — many workers bring personal thermoses and mate sets to offices. The shared mate circle survives in university life (Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná and UTPM students are enthusiastic mate sharers) and among friends. Shared mate at a desk is a symbol of friendship and acceptance in Zoopan professional culture — to be offered mate by a colleague is a form of welcome.

**Mate at the Asado:** Mate circulates among adults before the asado proper begins and in the post-asado stretch when everyone is full and drowsy. It is not typically drunk during the meal itself (wine takes precedence then). The post-asado mate circle, with its measured pace and the thermos cooling in the shade, is one of the most characteristically Zoopan images of domestic life.

**Tereré Zoopano:** In the subtropical northern provinces (Candelaria, Los Esteros), a cold version of mate called **tereré** is common in summer — the same gourd and bombilla filled with cold water or herbal juice instead of hot. The Zoopan tereré often uses cold *mburucuyá* (passion fruit) juice, giving it a tart, fruity note. This variant is shared with neighboring countries but has specifically Zoopan herbal additions.

## SECTION 8 — STREET FOOD AND THE BODEGÓN

### Street Food in Zoopaná

Zoopaná's street food culture concentrates around the city's markets and transit nodes. The primary street foods:

- **Empanadas fritas** — fried empanadas zoopanas from carts; the fastest and cheapest hot food in the city; universally available near Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná (the student population is the primary market)
- **Choripán** — a chorizo zoopano split open and served in a crusty roll with chimichurri; the standard quick-meal, sold at grills set up near Plaza de la Independencia and along the Avenida de la República
- **Sandwiches de milanesa** — a breaded and fried beef cutlet (milanesa) in a roll, dressed with tomato, lettuce, and mustard; the Italian immigrant tradition at its most street-friendly
- **Bondiola** — slow-roasted pork neck, thinly sliced and served in a roll; a more elaborate street sandwich
- **Mazamorra** — sold at stalls near the markets in winter; warm and sweet, in disposable cups; especially common in July near the empanada festival period

**The Zoopaná bodegón:** The traditional working-class restaurant — low ceilings, paper tablecloths, fluorescent lighting, handwritten daily menus on a chalkboard, wine in half-litre jugs, no reservations, always full at 1pm. The typical bodegón menu: soup or salad; a stew (*puchero zoopano* — a boiled beef and vegetable stew with chickpeas, corn, and chorizo, similar to Argentine puchero) or a *milanesa* with fried potatoes; fruit for dessert; and a coffee. Price: approximately ZPS 1,200–1,800 (2024). The bodegón is not glamorous and is specifically not trying to be.

### Puerto Marán Port Food

The Zona Portuaria of Puerto Marán has its own working-class food culture distinct from the rest of the city. The primary characteristics:

- **The *parrilla portuaria*:** Small grills operated from converted shipping containers or kiosk stands in the Zona Portuaria Norte; selling choripán, half-chickens, and simple beef cuts to dock workers; open from 6am to 2pm and then again from 6pm to midnight; the night-shift food is the same as the day-shift food
- **The *Mercado del Puerto*:** The covered food market (from 3-B; operating since 1887) in the Zona Portuaria is Puerto Marán's central food market. It functions primarily as a fresh produce, meat, and cheese market during the morning. In the afternoon, the market's interior transforms: the stalls between vendors become tables, the vendors who sell raw ingredients pivot to cooking, and the Mercado becomes a restaurant — one of the most atmospheric dining experiences in Zoop. Diners sit among the stalls, eating grilled meats cooked at parrillas set up in the market's central aisle, surrounded by the smell of wood smoke and fresh vegetables. This informal tradition has existed since at least the 1920s; it is now famous enough that weekend Mercado lunches require planning, though there are no reservations — you arrive and find space

### The *Bodegón del Puerto*

The working-class restaurants of Barrio Obrero and the Zona Portuaria have a specific character — rougher than the Barrio Palermo restaurants, more heavily beef-focused, reflecting the labor-intensive physical culture of dock and factory work. The classic *bodegón del puerto* menu:
- *Entrada:* a simple tuna, onion, and olive salad or a bean soup
- *Principal:* a *churrasco* (thin-sliced grilled beef) with fried potatoes and a green salad; or a *puchero portuario* (the port version of the stew, heavier, with more fat cuts and more chorizo); or a *milanesa*
- Wine: always a jug of the cheapest Valles del Oeste bulk red
- *Sobremesa:* fruit and a black coffee

## SECTION 10 — WINE CULTURE AND FOOD PAIRING

### Wines at the Table

Zoopan wine culture (from 4-A) is serious but not pretentious. The following canonical pairings have become established:

- **Torrontés Zoopano** (Bodega Etchegaray Hermanos **Terraza Etchegaray**) → empanadas zoopanas, goat cheese pasta, river fish, ceviche; the wine's floral, dry character is a natural partner for the corn-and-goat-cheese filling; this pairing has been widely promoted by the INV-Zoop as the signature Zoopan food-wine match
- **Gran Reserva Cruzat** (Malbec-Tannat blend) → tira de asado, vacío, colita de cadera; the wine's density and tannic structure match the slow-cooked beef fat; opened at the table at a Sunday asado, it signals the meal has officially begun
- **Granito Negro** Sierra Alta Pinot Noir (Bodega Alta Sierra) → the elegant choice for carpín del Marán, for the Raíces restaurant's tasting menu, for braised river fish; expensive enough to be reserved for special occasions
- **Pampa Roja** Cabernet (Cooperativa de Bodegas del Oeste) → choripán, everyday beef, Thursday-night pasta; the democratic wine for democratic occasions; priced to be drunk without calculation
- **Kessler Selección** Riesling → Villa Hannover sausages and cheeses; the Landjaeger del Cerro; the Queso de la Sierra; the mountain pairing that very few Zoopans in the lowlands know about but that is considered close to perfect by those who do

### The Vendimia del Río Blanco (Harvest Festival)

The harvest festival in Santa Marta del Vino (from 4-A; first Saturday of March; established 1953) is the wine-country equivalent of the empanada festival — a celebration centered on the end of the grape harvest. Food served at Vendimia:
- The first pressing of the new vintage served with asado cooked over pruned grapevine wood
- *Humitas de maíz* (corn tamales in corn husks, a Zopikí-Andean tradition adapted in the wine country; filled with corn meal, goat cheese, and basil)
- *Torta de vendimia* — a sweet grape-must cake baked in the shape of a bunch of grapes; sold at every bakery in Santa Marta del Vino in March
- New-vintage wine opened for the public in the bodega courtyards

## SECTION 11 — REGIONAL FOOD SPECIALTIES BY PROVINCE

### Costa Atlántica

**Puerto Marán / Maraná:** Seafood and river fish; the most cosmopolitan and diverse food scene in Zoop; Italian food deeply embedded; the *bocadillo de corvina* (a pan-fried sea bass fillet in a roll with garlic aioli and fresh tomato) is the regional street food signature.

**Bahía Larga:** Citrus country; the **Fiesta del Limón** (from 3-B / 4-A; Villa Inés; June) centers on lemon-based foods — *ceviche de limón* (a sharp, all-lemon-acid version of the Zoopan ceviche), lemon-glazed pork ribs, and *dulce de limón* (a lemon curd variant). The local dairy (from 4-A; COLCA and Costa Sur) means fresh cheese is on every table. The **torta de mantequilla de Bahía Larga** (butter cake from coastal dairy butter) is a provincial specialty.

**Candelaria:** Tropical flavors; the most distinctively different food region in Zoop; cacao, manioc, and tropical fruits appear in everyday cooking; mate tereré with mburucuyá juice is the standard summer drink.

### Pampa Central

**Aranduy / Llanuras del Centro (Corrientes del Marán):** The beef heartland; the most devoted asado culture; the empanada festival city (Corrientes del Marán); also the finest *puchero zoopano* in the country — the stew is said to be best where the cattle are, and the cattle are best here.

**San Bartolomé del Pequeé:** Wheat country traditions: the best *pan de campo* in Zoop; also home to one of the finest working-men's *parrillas* in the country (the restaurant **El Fortín**, Calle del Cereales 12, San Bartolomé — a converted grain warehouse; open since 1951; no website; no reservations; legendary *vacío* cooked over quebracho).

**Quilmes del Sur:** Welsh food traditions (see Section 5); also the best sheep country in Zoop — the local *cordero a la cruz* (lamb slow-roasted on a vertical cross over open fire, a Patagonian-influenced technique adopted in the southern pampa) is considered by specialists to rival the Patagonian original.

**Los Esteros:** The most subtropical food culture in the pampa; river fish dominant; wild game historically important; the Esterotl Zopikí descendants have maintained a vegetable and fish cooking tradition that influences local restaurants with unusual ingredient combinations.

### Sierra del Oeste

**Las Cumbres / San Ignacio de las Cumbres:** Wine and cheese country; the Sierra Alta and Valle del Río Blanco DOC wines; Queso de la Sierra produced in mountain dairies; the Tupanachi food traditions (pachamanca, tika-mazán) most visible here; the best *locro zoopano* in the country (a thick highland stew of corn, beans, potato, pumpkin, and slow-cooked pork or goat; Andean in origin, Zoopan in execution).

**Pequeé Norte / Cerro Verde:** Mining culture food — hearty, simple; German-influenced in Villa Hannover; the Pequeéka community maintains goat herding and the production of fresh goat cheese for the empanada tradition; the city's restaurant scene has grown around the lithium mining boom (a new generation of better restaurants has appeared since 2018).

**Río Hondo:** The most remote food culture; traditional, unaffected by tourism; the *cordero al horno de barro* (lamb slow-roasted in a clay oven) is the regional specialty; the provincial capital Hondo has the best preserved traditional cooking in the Sierra del Oeste.

## SECTION 12 — NATIONAL CUISINE IDENTITY AND POLITICS

### The National Dish Question

There is no official national dish. The government has never designated one, and the question of whether to do so comes up in political discourse approximately every five years and is always quietly dropped because the answer is impossible.

The three candidates:
1. **The empanada zoopana** — favored by those who emphasize indigenous heritage and pre-Columbian continuity; it has a specific name, a specific filling, a national festival; it is the most argued-about food object in Zoop
2. **The asado** — favored by the cattleman and gaucho tradition; the most widely practiced food ritual; the most emotionally resonant; but it is shared with Argentina and Uruguay, which makes it a complicated nationalist symbol
3. **The puchero zoopano** — championed by working-class food advocates as the true food of the Zoopan people across all classes and regions; less glamorous than the empanada or the asado; more democratic

The political valence: the FPZ and MZ tend to favor the empanada zoopana as the national dish (its Zopikí roots, its uniqueness, its connection to the Fiesta de la Pachamama tradition). The PR and estanciero culture favor the asado (beef, gaucho, national masculine identity). The impasse is permanent and the food journalism community considers it moderately entertaining.

### The Argentine Comparison

Zoopan food culture is inevitably compared to Argentine food culture, given the geographic proximity and shared European immigrant heritage. Zoopans are sensitive about this comparison and genuinely differentiate:
- The **corn-and-goat-cheese empanada** is not Argentine
- The **Torrontés Zoopano** and the **Criolla de la Sierra** wine varietals are not Argentine
- The Zopikí food traditions (kazamora, tika-mazán, carpín del Marán) are specifically Zoopan
- The chimichurri zoopano (with cumin) is considered a signature distinction

The argument is conducted with good humor but genuine conviction. Zoopan food writers will accept that the asado tradition is shared across the Río de la Plata; they will not accept that Zoopan food is merely a regional variant of Argentine food.
