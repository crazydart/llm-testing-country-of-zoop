## SECTION 1 — FOLKLORE ZOOPANO

### 1.1 Origins and Character

*Folklore zoopano* is the oldest continuous musical tradition in the Republic of Zoop, rooted in the convergence of three currents: the Spanish colonial guitar tradition brought by settlers from the 16th century onward; the indigenous Zopikí percussion tradition centered on the *bombo* drum; and the gaucho culture of the Pampa Central, which contributed both the *payador* (improvising troubadour) tradition and characteristic lyric themes of open land, cattle, and seasonal labor. The tradition crystallized as a recognizable national form during the 19th century, absorbing influences from the Italian and German immigrant communities during the Liberal Era (1870–1916) — particularly the accordion (*acordeón criollo*), which entered the folklore ensemble by the 1890s.

The defining instruments of the *folklore zoopano* ensemble are:

- **El bombo zoopano** — a large double-headed drum struck with a padded mallet; deeper and slower-voiced than the Argentine bombo; Zopikí communities call a version of this instrument the *tupana-bombo* (the festival-drum), and the pre-Colombian ceremonial origins are widely acknowledged in popular tradition even if the modern instrument is a colonial hybrid
- **La guitarra criolla** — the Spanish six-string guitar in its Zoopan fingering tradition; Zoopan guitarists favor a particular tuning variant (dropped fifth string, locally called *afineado del Marán*) that produces a more resonant bass response
- **El acordeón criollo** — the diatonic button accordion; associated with the northern and pampa styles; introduced by German and Italian settlers; absorbed so thoroughly by the late 19th century that it is now considered a "traditional" folklore instrument
- **La quena zoopana** — an end-blown flute derived from the Andean quena but with a distinctive breathy overtone fingering technique developed in the Sierra del Oeste communities; has six finger holes and one thumb hole; made from river reed; associated with the highland styles
- **El charango serrano** — a small ten-stringed instrument specific to the Sierra del Oeste; derived from the Bolivian charango but with a larger body (traditionally made from armadillo shell or, in modern versions, carved wood) and lower tuning; the instrument of choice for Tupanachi and Pequeéka highland musicians

### 1.2 Regional Styles

**1. Cumbia del Norte Zoopana (Northern Cumbia)**
- Region: Provincia de Candelaria, Provincia de Los Esteros, northern Maraná
- Character: Up-tempo, syncopated; borrowing from Caribbean cumbia via trade routes; more percussion-heavy than the southern styles; accordions play a leading melodic role; lyrics about river life, fishing, love, and seasonal flooding; danced in pairs with a characteristic hip-rolling step locals call *el tranco del Marán*
- Typical contexts: *Fiestas patronales* in river towns; Carnival (the northern provinces have a Carnival tradition derived from both Spanish and indigenous influences); dock-side celebrations in Puerto Marán

**2. Cueca Zoopaña (The Pampa Cueca)**
- Region: Pampa Central provinces — Llanuras del Centro, Aranduy, Solórzano, Quilmes del Sur
- Character: Medium-tempo partner dance; handkerchief waving; lyric themes of the pampa — horses, cattle, harvests, departed lovers; the guitar takes the melodic lead with the bombo marking the 6/8 rhythm; somewhat slower and more mournful in feel than the Chilean cueca; the Zoopan style is known for its long vocal melismas (called *floreos*) that give skilled singers room to improvise
- Typical contexts: *Asados* and family celebrations; the major folklore festivals; the annual Fiesta de la Ganadería in Villa Aranduy

**3. Tonada Serrana (Highland Lament)**
- Region: Sierra del Oeste — Provincias de Las Cumbres, Río Hondo, Pequeé Norte
- Character: Slow, meditative; solo or duet vocal with quena zoopana and charango serrano; lyrics in a mixture of Spanish and Zopikí (the most common style where Zopikí appears in popular music); themes of the mountains, distance from home, the river's source, memory and loss; associated with the Zopikí cultural revival; the tonada serrana has become the form through which indigenous political feeling is most commonly expressed in music; influenced by Andean *huayno* but distinct in its flatter melodic contour
- Typical contexts: Zopikí ceremonial occasions (Fiesta de la Pachamama Zoopana, Fiesta de San Juan Dorado); the Escenario Marantika at the Festival del Río Marán; small highland venues; increasingly featured at folklore festivals as part of the indigenous revival

**4. Balada Sureña (Southern Pampa Ballad)**
- Region: Southern provinces — Quilmes del Sur, parts of Solórzano; influenced by the Welsh communities of Nueva Gales
- Character: Slow ballad form; solo guitar or guitar-accordion duet; lyrics about the flat southern pampa, solitude, wind; the Welsh community's choral tradition has left a faint but audible harmonic influence on southern songs (multi-part harmony in a style called *el canto a dos voces del sur*); the most melancholic of the regional styles; associated with the *payador* improvising tradition at its most literary
- Typical contexts: Intimate performances; *peñas* (small folklore gathering venues); the Eisteddfod de Y Wladfa Newydd has occasionally featured bilingual Welsh-Spanish folklore performances

### 1.3 The National Folklore Festival — Festival Nacional del Folklore de Zoop

**Established:** 1963, during the democratic interlude between military interventions
**Location:** Villa Aranduy, Provincia de Aranduy — chosen for its central Pampa Central location, its gaucho heritage, and the convenient festival grounds (the Anfiteatro Pelayo Aranduy, built 1961 as a multi-purpose outdoor venue on the western edge of the city; capacity 8,000 seated)
**Timing:** Third week of January (late summer in Zoop's Southern Hemisphere location); runs nine consecutive days
**Attendance:** Approximately 180,000 people over the festival's nine days (2024 estimate); draw from all provinces; a significant tourism driver for Villa Aranduy
**Character:** The Festival Nacional del Folklore is Zoop's equivalent of Argentina's Cosquín festival — the undisputed national showcase for the full range of folklore styles and artists; the closing night (Noche de la Patria) is broadcast live on RTZP Canal 1 and routinely draws 2–3 million television viewers; new artists who break through at the Festival can expect national careers within months
**Programming:** Nine stages and performance arenas; the main stage (Gran Escenario Aranduy) hosts headline acts; smaller stages dedicated to regional styles; the Carpa del Payador hosts improvised competition singing; artisan markets; gastronomy pavilions featuring food from all provinces

### 1.4 Key Historical Folklore Figures

**Celestino "El Pampa" Montoya** (1912–1978)
- Born in Corrientes del Marán, Llanuras del Centro; the son of a mestizo cattle worker and a Pachakama-descent woman
- The single most influential figure in the canonization of the *cueca zoopaña* as a national form; his recordings of the 1940s and 1950s — made for the Discos del Marán label (founded Zoopaná, 1932) — defined the sound that later generations consider the "authentic" folk style
- Famous for his vocal floreos and his guitar technique; toured Argentina and Uruguay in the 1940s and was reportedly told by Argentine musicians that his style was "too slow, too sad, too beautiful"
- Signature works: the cueca *"La Vuelta del Gaucho"* (1947); the tonada *"Río de Mis Aguas"* (1951); the *payada* suite *"Las Cuatro Estaciones del Marán"* (recorded live at the Teatro Principal de Zoopaná, 1958)
- The dictatorship's impact: Montoya was briefly detained in 1971 and subjected to interrogation about his association with union-affiliated festivals; he was not "disappeared" but the experience silenced him publicly for the last years of his life; he died in 1978 — three years before the democratic transition — and was not rehabilitated officially until a commemorative RTZP broadcast in 1986

**Lola Ferreira Cárdenas** (1929–2003)
- Born in Cerro Verde, of Pequeéka and Spanish descent; the most celebrated female voice in Zoopan folklore history
- Her *tonadas serranas* introduced Zopikí language systematically into the folklore mainstream; she insisted on performing at least one song per concert in Zopikí even in the 1960s and early 1970s when this was culturally and sometimes politically risky
- The dictatorship forced her into internal exile in 1972 — she was banned from radio and television and from performing in venues larger than 50 people; she spent the 1972–1985 period performing in private homes and small *peñas*, a network of underground cultural resistance that sustained the folklore scene
- After the democratic transition, she became a symbol of cultural survival; her 1985 return concert at the Teatro Nacional Ruperto Manzano (Zoopaná) — the largest folklore concert ever held at that venue — was broadcast live by RTZP and is still considered a historic cultural event
- Signature works: *"Canto a la Madre Pequeéka"* (1962; the first major commercial recording to use Zopikí lyrics); *"El Cerro y el Río"* (1969); the album *Resistencia* (1987 — her comeback recording, named for the years of resistance)
- Lola Ferreira Cárdenas died in 2003 at her home in Cerro Verde; the main music auditorium of the Centro Cultural Pequeéka was renamed the Sala Lola Ferreira in 2004

### 1.5 The Dictatorship's Impact on Folk Musicians

The *Proceso de Reorganización Nacional Zoopano* (1966–1985) viewed folklore music with deep suspicion, particularly the highland *tonada serrana* tradition with its Zopikí elements, which was associated in junta ideology with communist-adjacent "ethnic separatism," and the *cueca zoopaña* tradition with its labor and gaucho themes that could be read as class-based. The repression affected musicians in three ways:

**Disappeared and killed musicians:** At least eight folklore musicians are documented as having been "disappeared" by the security apparatus between 1970 and 1979. The most prominent cases documented by the CVMN (Comisión de Verdad y Memoria Nacional) include:
- **Roberto Casimiro Arrieta** (1940–1973*) — accordion player and composer from Esteros del Norte; disappeared February 1973; known for compositions sympathetic to Zopikí land rights; his family received no confirmation of his fate until the CVMN report of 1988
- **Miriam Gutiérrez de Paz** (1945–1975*) — singer-songwriter from Puerto Marán's Barrio Obrero; disappeared July 1975; her songs about the Semana Trágica and dock workers were apparently sufficient to mark her; she had performed at an FOZ labor benefit concert in 1974
- (*date of death estimated; confirmed only as within the "Years of Lead" period)

**Exiled musicians:** Dozens of folklore musicians left Zoop during the dictatorship. The primary destinations were Argentina (Buenos Aires), Uruguay, and Spain. The exile community maintained a "counter-cultural" Zoopan folklore scene, particularly at a venue in Buenos Aires — the **Peña del Marán** (founded 1970; Palermo Viejo neighborhood, Buenos Aires) — that became a gathering point for exiled Zoopan musicians and a source of news and cassette recordings smuggled back into Zoop.

**Underground culture:** Musicians who remained created a network of small *peñas* (private folklore gatherings held in private homes, union halls, and back rooms of cafés) that continued throughout the dictatorship. The phenomenon was called *el circuito de las peñas* (the peña circuit) and is now recognized as one of the primary mechanisms through which democratic and indigenous cultural memory was preserved. The Zoopaná peñas were centered in Barrio de la Misión and Barrio Universidad; in Puerto Marán, in Barrio Palomar.

### 1.6 The Post-1985 Revival

The democratic transition unleashed an extraordinary cultural explosion in folklore. Key moments:

- **1985:** Lola Ferreira Cárdenas's return concert at Teatro Nacional Ruperto Manzano (see above)
- **1986:** The Festival Nacional del Folklore in Villa Aranduy (cancelled every year 1976–1985 by military decree) was reinstated; the first post-dictatorship festival drew 220,000 people over nine days — a record that stood until 2012
- **The "New Folklore" movement (1987–1995):** A generation of younger musicians, raised on clandestine *peñas* and exile recordings, began fusing *folklore zoopano* with urban rock, jazz, and electronic elements; the movement was centered in Barrio Universidad in Zoopaná and Barrio Palomar in Puerto Marán; it produced some of the most celebrated albums in Zoopan music history

### 1.7 Contemporary Folklore Artists

**Sebastián Quispe-Arrieta** (b. 1974, Cerro Verde)
- Grandson of Roberto Casimiro Arrieta (disappeared 1973); has made reclaiming his grandfather's memory a central element of his artistic identity
- Principal practitioner of the modern *tonada serrana*; records and performs with both traditional instruments (quena zoopana, charango serrano) and contemporary arrangements (cello, double bass)
- His 2015 album *Voces del Río* (Voces del Marán Records) won the Premio Nacional de Folklore and was the first Zoopan folklore album to receive significant streaming plays outside of Zoop; reached #3 on Spotify's "Latin Acoustic" chart in Spain and Mexico
- Performs annually at the Festival del Río Marán's Escenario Marantika; appears regularly at the Festival Nacional del Folklore in Villa Aranduy

**Trio Marantika** (formed Zoopaná, 2008)
- Ensemble: Valentina Esteban Quiroga (vocals/guitar), Rodrigo Paz Montoya (bombo/percussion, grandnephew of Celestino "El Pampa" Montoya), and Celia Ferreira-Lara (accordion)
- Specialize in the pampa and northern styles; known for their textual fidelity to pre-dictatorship repertoire alongside new compositions; regarded as the finest cueca zoopaña ensemble currently active
- Album: *Las Estaciones del Pampa* (2018, Discos del Marán) — commercially the most successful folklore album of the 2010s in Zoop

## SECTION 2 — PUERTO MARÁN INDIE ROCK SCENE

### 2.1 Origins and Character

The Puerto Marán indie rock scene emerged in the late 1980s, directly from the energy of the democratic transition. Its preconditions were the post-dictatorship opening of cultural space, the concentration of university students in Barrio Palomar (where the UTPM campus is located), and the working-class energy of Barrio Obrero, where a generation of young people who had grown up during the "Years of Lead" turned political rage into music.

The scene's initial aesthetic was consciously *anti-porteño* — that is, in deliberate contrast to Buenos Aires, which dominated Argentine (and to a large extent Zoopan) cultural life. Puerto Marán rock musicians in the early 1990s were proud of their port-city grit, their distance from the capital's self-regard, and their hybrid influences: British post-punk, U.S. indie rock, and — unusually for rock — fragments of *folklore zoopano* and Zopikí rhythmic influence filtered through the *peña* circuit that had survived the dictatorship.

The scene's political character was explicitly democratic-left without being dogmatically ideological; the working-class port aesthetic was not easily reduced to any party platform. The bands sang about the city, the port, the water, unemployment, first love, and the ghosts of the disappeared — sometimes all in the same song.

### 2.2 Key Venues (Established in 3-B)

- **Bar Palomar** — Avenida UTPM 34, Barrio Palomar; founded 1987; capacity 120; the oldest venue in the scene; the intimate founding space where most original bands played their first shows; still operates as a showcase venue for new artists; the walls are covered with original concert posters going back to 1987
- **El Galpón Azul** — Calle de los Estudiantes 89, Barrio Palomar; founded 1991 by musician and labor organizer Horacio Sandoval (1958–2020); capacity 350; converted from a former textile workshop (*galpón*); the primary mid-size venue for established local acts and touring bands from Buenos Aires and beyond; named for the blue-painted corrugated iron walls; the stage faces a standing floor with a small balcony; Horacio Sandoval's daughter Luciana Borba Sandoval is the frontwoman of Marea Roja
- **La Sala del Puerto** — Muelle 5, Zona Portuaria Norte; founded 1998; capacity 800 standing / 1,200 for all-ages seated shows; the largest dedicated rock venue in Puerto Marán; converted from a former port warehouse; in the 1990s it launched the Festival del Río Marán's indoor late-night programming and remains the Festival's Escenario Noche venue; the view of the harbor through the loading doors (kept open in good weather) is considered one of the great live-music experiences in Zoop

### 2.3 Named Bands — Profiles

**Los Garibaldi**
- Formed: Puerto Marán, 1992; named after Calle Garibaldi in Barrio Palermo (a nod to the Italian immigrant working-class heritage of the city)
- Members at peak: Emiliano Katz Pereyra (vocals/guitar), Daniela Ruiz-Ferrarone (bass), Marcos Sandoval Crespo (drums), Ignacio Veracruz Ibáñez (guitar/keys)
- Sound: Post-punk guitar rock with occasional accordion and bombo elements; wiry, angular guitar lines; Emiliano Katz Pereyra's voice — a dry, slightly hoarse baritone — became one of the most recognizable sounds in Zoopan rock; lyrics about port-city life, political disillusionment, complicated love
- Active: 1992–2005 (original run); reunited 2019 (from 3-B)
- Notable albums:
  - *Marea Alta* (1995, Discos del Puerto — the scene's primary indie label, est. 1993): their breakthrough record; the track "Calle Garibaldi" became an anthem for the scene
  - *El Año del Colapso* (2002): recorded and released during "La Crisis"; raw, economically anxious; critically regarded as their best work; the song "Default" became Zoop's unofficial soundtrack to the economic catastrophe; used in the 2014 documentary film *La Crisis Nuestra*
  - *Volver* (2020): reunion album; received warmly by older fans and introduced the band to a streaming generation
- International: Toured Argentina several times in the late 1990s; received positive coverage in the Argentine music press (*Los Inrockuptibles Buenos Aires* called them "the most original band to emerge from Zoop"); never fully broke through internationally; "Calle Garibaldi" was covered by a Spanish indie band in 2006

**Marea Roja**
- Formed: Puerto Marán, 1995; the longest-running active band in the scene (from 3-B)
- Members: Luciana Borba Sandoval (vocals; daughter of Horacio Sandoval, El Galpón Azul's founder); Tomás Cárdenas Pérez (guitar); Raúl Etchegaray Muro (bass); Patricia Villanueva-Cossio (drums); Carolina Amaral (keyboards, joined 2003)
- Sound: More melodic than Los Garibaldi; guitar pop with post-rock architecture; Luciana Borba Sandoval's voice — clear, high, with a precise diction that makes lyrics audible even in noisy venues — is the band's signature; political themes more explicit than most peers; feminist consciousness runs through almost all their work; occasional use of the quena zoopana (played by guest artists)
- Notable albums:
  - *Huelga* (1998): debut; raw and political; the title track about the 1919 Semana Trágica was adopted by union halls across the country; re-released on streaming 2018 and rediscovered by a younger audience
  - *Profundidad* (2004, Discos del Puerto): their commercial breakthrough; produced in Zoopaná with a larger budget; the single "Río Adentro" received significant RTZP radio play
  - *Treinta Años* (2015): a concept album responding to the 30th anniversary of the democratic transition; all lyrics drawn from testimonies of former political prisoners; won the Premio Nacional de Música in the rock category
  - *La Última Marea* (2023): most recent; darker, more electronic; reviewed as "Marea Roja's most mature and challenging record" by the music supplement of La República newspaper
- International: Best-known Zoopan rock band internationally; performed at Primavera Sound Barcelona (2019); have a modest but genuine following in Spain and among the Latin American diaspora in Europe; "Treinta Años" was streamed 4.2 million times in its first year, a remarkable figure for Zoopan music

**Muelle Siete**
- Formed: Puerto Marán, 2008 (from 3-B)
- Members: Gonzalo Herrera Lemos (vocals/guitar); Pilar Quispe Cárdenas (bass; Pequeéka heritage, the first prominent indigenous musician in the Puerto Marán rock scene); Diego Montero-Arrieta (drums); Valentina Cruzat Ibáñez (keys/samples)
- Sound: The most genre-hybrid band in the scene; rock base fused with cumbia rhythms, electronic production, and Zopikí percussion samples; Pilar Quispe Cárdenas has brought charango serrano into the rock context; Gonzalo Herrera Lemos writes in both Spanish and occasionally Zopikí; described by critics as "the sound of Puerto Marán in the streaming era"
- Named after Muelle 7 (Dock 7), the fictional counterpart to the real Muelle 3 and Muelle 5 already established in the city's port geography
- Notable albums:
  - *Puerto Nuevo* (2011, self-released, later licensed to a Buenos Aires label): debut; the track "Cumbia del Marán" (a cumbia-rock fusion) became a viral hit in Argentina, with over 8 million YouTube views; the band's signature track
  - *Salar* (2015): conceptually organized around the lithium controversy in Pequeé Norte; politically charged; Pilar Quispe Cárdenas co-wrote most of the album; features field recordings from the Sierra del Oeste
  - *Marea Digital* (2021): fully embraces electronic production; streaming-first release; their most internationally listened album; featured on several "Latin Alternative" Spotify editorial playlists
- International: The most internationally recognized Zoopan band currently active; "Cumbia del Marán" has been played in clubs in Buenos Aires, Santiago, and Madrid; performed at SXSW (Austin, Texas) in 2019 — the first Zoopan band to do so; featured in a *New York Times* piece on "the new sounds of South America" (2022)

**Additional Bands in the Scene:**

**Las Pequeékas** (formed 2001)
- All-female trio; Barrio Obrero origin; punk-influenced with folk elements; known for political directness; their 2008 album *Sin Reducción* took its title from the Zopikí forced-reduction camps of the Pacificación del Sur; the most explicitly indigenous-rights-focused rock band in Zoopan history; modest commercial profile but significant cultural influence

**El Temporal** (formed 2005)
- Quartet; experimental post-rock; largely instrumental; associated with the Puerto Marán art school scene at UTPM; influenced by Godspeed You! Black Emperor and Mogwai; their 2012 album *Inundación* (about the 1983 La Crecida Negra flood) is considered a landmark of Zoopan experimental rock despite selling fewer than 3,000 copies

**Los Ribereños** (formed 2014)
- Five-piece; the newest generation; their sound is unambiguously shaped by streaming-era production (trap rhythms, auto-tune, cumbia bass); controversial among older scene adherents who see them as abandoning the scene's DIY ethos; beloved by audiences under 25; their 2022 album *Streaming* is the best-selling rock album by a Zoopan band in streaming history

### 2.4 The Festival del Río Marán

- **Full name:** Festival Internacional del Río Marán (commonly shortened to Festival del Río Marán)
- **Location:** Parque de la Costanera, Puerto Marán (established 3-B)
- **Timing:** Last full weekend of October (established 3-B); typically a three-day event (Friday evening, Saturday, Sunday)
- **Founded:** 1997 (established 3-B)
- **Attendance:** Approximately 55,000 over three days (2024 estimate); the festival has grown each decade since its founding
- **Programming director:** Estela Cossio Palma (established 3-B)
- **Stages (all established 3-B):**
  - Escenario Río (main stage; capacity 20,000)
  - Escenario Palermo (second stage; capacity 6,000)
  - Escenario Marantika (third stage; folk and indigenous music; capacity 2,500)
  - Escenario Noche (indoor late-night; Galpón del Muelle; capacity 800)
- **Character:** Deliberately eclectic; the Festival's programming philosophy is that Zoopan music in all its forms should share a festival, rather than segregating rock from folklore; this has created productive cross-pollination and is considered the main reason the Puerto Marán scene developed its distinctive folk-influenced rock hybridity
- **Scene identity:** The Festival has explicitly positioned Puerto Marán as distinct from Buenos Aires rather than as a satellite of it; the booking policy prioritizes Zoopan artists on all stages and uses international headliners primarily on Escenario Río to draw attendance rather than as the main cultural statement; this approach is described by Estela Cossio Palma as "using international acts as the door, not the house"
- **The Buenos Aires relationship:** Puerto Marán musicians have always had an ambivalent relationship with Buenos Aires. Buenos Aires is the regional capital of South American music and the primary market for any Zoopan band that wants to expand beyond the domestic audience. Most successful Puerto Marán bands tour Buenos Aires annually. But there is a persistent sense within the Puerto Marán scene that Buenos Aires perceives Zoopan music as provincial or derivative — a perception the scene has spent three decades working to refute. The Festival del Río Marán's insistence on Zoopan identity is partly a response to this dynamic.

## SECTION 3 — CLASSICAL MUSIC

### 3.1 Orquesta Sinfónica de Zoopaná (OSZ)

- **Founded:** 1897, during the presidency of Ruperto Hilario Manzano Goicoechea (#12), as part of the Liberal Era cultural institution-building that also produced the Teatro Nacional Ruperto Manzano
- **Full name:** Orquesta Sinfónica de Zoopaná (OSZ) — the name uses "Zoopaná" rather than "de Zoop" or "Nacional" to emphasize the capital's role as the cultural center while distinguishing the ensemble from a purely state institution
- **Size:** 92 musicians (standard full complement); reduced to a chamber ensemble for baroque and early classical programs
- **Home venue:** Teatro Nacional Ruperto Manzano (established 3-A; opened 14 September 1912); the orchestra uses the main house (1,680 seats) for full symphonic programs; the Sala Velázquez Sarabia (280 seats) for chamber concerts
- **Season:** March through December (10 months); approximately 60 main-stage concerts per season plus education and outreach programming
- **Historic highlights:**
  - 1897–1912 (pre-Teatro Manzano): performed at the Teatro Principal de Zoopaná (the original theater where the national anthem premiered in 1832)
  - 1945: First recording — a disc of Zoopan composers' works for Discos del Marán; the first nationally broadcast classical concert (RTZP radio)
  - 1966–1985 (dictatorship period): the OSZ continued performing but under heavy programming censorship; contemporary and experimental works were banned; several musicians were dismissed or detained; the programming became exclusively 19th-century European repertoire by official direction
  - 1985: The post-dictatorship relaunch season opened with Beethoven's Ninth Symphony followed by a program of works by Zoopan composers that had been banned during the Proceso; broadcast live on RTZP
  - 1997: Centenary celebrated with a commission of a new symphony by Zoopan composer Adriana Ruiz-Castellanos (the *Sinfonía del Marán*, see below)
- **Current profile:** The OSZ is respected within South American classical music circles; it tours Argentina and Uruguay biennially; international touring beyond South America is limited by budget; it has a streaming presence through the RTZP digital platform

### 3.2 Teatro Nacional Ruperto Manzano — Classical Programming

The Teatro Manzano (opened 14 September 1912; architect Federico Lozano Parodi; established 3-A) serves as the home of both the OSZ and the Ballet Nacional de Zoopaná (est. 1954). Its programming is managed by the **Instituto Nacional de Artes Escénicas (INAE)** — the state performing arts agency — and includes:
- **OSZ main season** (60 concerts/year)
- **Ballet Nacional main season** (30 productions/year; primarily classical repertoire with occasional contemporary commissions)
- **Opera season:** The Teatro Manzano presents 6–8 opera productions per year; the company has no permanent opera ensemble but invites regional and international casts; productions are staged in partnership with the Teatro Colón (Buenos Aires) and Teatro Solís (Montevideo) biennially
- **International touring companies:** 15–20 visiting productions per year (ballet, theatre, international orchestras)
- **The Sala Velázquez Sarabia** (280 seats) hosts chamber music, recitals, and contemporary music programming; named for Aurelio Velázquez Sarabia (1791–1869), the composer and lyricist of "Hijos del Marán" (established 1-B)

### 3.3 Notable Zoopan Classical Composers

**Aurelio Velázquez Sarabia** (1791–1869) — established in Session 1-B
- Composer and lyricist of the national anthem "Hijos del Marán" (1832)
- Director of the Orquesta del Teatro Principal de Zoopaná
- Author of *Notas de una vida musical* (memoir, 1867)
- His other compositions include a set of piano *variaciones* on Zopikí folk themes (the *Variaciones Zupakka*, 1844) — the first serious attempt at incorporating Zopikí musical elements into European art-music form; they were largely forgotten during his lifetime but have been revived by the OSZ as part of the "Zoopan heritage" programming

**Edmundo Quiroga Saraví** (muralist; separate from music — see Visual Arts section)

**Isidora Palma-Crespo** (1884–1962)
- Born Zoopaná; studied in Paris at the Conservatoire National (1906–1911) under Gabriel Fauré; the most technically accomplished Zoopan composer of the early 20th century
- Her major works: *Tres Poemas del Marán* for orchestra (1913); the string quartet *Pampa* (1921); the song cycle *Canciones de la Patria* (1938, settings of poems by Esteban Calderón Ríos's father, Joaquín Calderón Torres)
- The dictatorship's legacy: Palma-Crespo died in 1962, before the worst of the repression; but her modernist style (post-impressionist, dissonant) was precisely the kind of contemporary music banned after 1966; the OSZ did not program her work again until 1988
- Legacy: The **Premio Isidora Palma-Crespo** — awarded annually by the OSZ to the best new Zoopan orchestral composition — was established in 1965

**Adriana Ruiz-Castellanos** (b. 1961, Puerto Marán)
- The most prominent living Zoopan composer; trained at the Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná and at IRCAM (Paris); combines electroacoustic elements with orchestral writing
- Her *Sinfonía del Marán* (1997; commissioned for the OSZ centenary) is her best-known work; a four-movement symphony using field recordings of the Río Marán as sonic material processed into the orchestral texture; it has been performed by orchestras in Argentina, Uruguay, and Spain
- Her opera *La Crisis* (2010; libretto by her longtime collaborator Martín Peñaloza Gutiérrez) — a chamber opera depicting the 2001–2003 economic collapse from three perspectives simultaneously — was premiered at the Teatro Manzano and subsequently staged at the Teatro Colón (Buenos Aires) and the Volksoper Wien (Vienna); the Vienna staging (2013) brought her the most international attention of her career

## SECTION 4 — ZOOPAN CINEMA

### 4.1 History Overview

**Silent Era (1918–1935)**
Zoopan cinema began, as elsewhere, in the silent era. The first Zoopan feature film was *El Río y la Ciudad* (1918) — a 45-minute melodrama set in Puerto Marán, shot on 35mm and screened at the Teatro Principal de Zoopaná. Its director, **Augusto Herrera-Vidal** (1885–1943), is credited as the father of Zoopan cinema. He went on to make eleven more silent films before sound arrived, working in Zoopaná's first purpose-built film studio — the **Estudios del Marán** (founded 1922; Barrio Ceibo location, established 3-A). The silent era produced approximately 40 Zoopan features, most of which are lost; 12 survive in the archive of the **Cinemateca Nacional de Zoop** (founded 1948).

**Sound Era — Golden Age (1935–1966)**
The introduction of sound and the development of the Argentine and Zoopan film industries in parallel created a genuine "Golden Age" of Zoopan cinema. The Estudios del Marán expanded significantly in the 1930s–1950s; Zoopaná had three major commercial studios by 1950. The period produced musical comedies, melodramas, war films (a genre born from the War of the Pequeé centenary in 1965), and a strain of Zoopan social realism influenced by Italian neorealism and French poetic realism. The most celebrated films of this period came from director **Rafael Iturriaga Blanco** (see 4.3).

**Dictatorship Era — Censorship (1966–1985)**
The Proceso imposed strict censorship on the film industry. The **Instituto Nacional de Cinematografía de Zoop (INCZ)** — founded 1949 as the state film agency — became a censorship body under the dictatorship. Films required prior approval of scripts; anything touching on political dissent, indigenous rights, labor conflict, or the disappeared was banned. Many directors went into exile; some films were completed clandestinely and smuggled out to be screened at film festivals abroad. The most significant act of cinematic resistance was director **Elena Fuentes-Manzur**'s film *Las Sombras del Río* (1978), completed in secret over three years and premiered at the Cannes Film Festival — the first Zoopan film to receive significant international festival recognition — while being banned within Zoop itself.

**Post-Dictatorship Revival (1985–present)**
The democratic transition unleashed an explosion of filmmaking. The INCZ was reformed under a new charter (1986) that replaced censorship with a funding and support mission. The first major post-dictatorship Zoopan film, **Pablo Rodríguez-Cárdenas**'s *El Galpón* (1987), used the clandestine detention center of that name (established 1-F) as its setting and is considered one of the defining works of Zoopan cinema. Since 1990, Zoopan cinema has maintained an annual output of 25–40 features, funded through a combination of INCZ grants, international co-productions (primarily with Argentina, Spain, and France), and streaming platform commissions.

### 4.2 The Instituto Nacional de Cinematografía de Zoop (INCZ)

- **Founded:** 1949, under President Mauricio Celestino Durán Balaguer (#19)
- **Original mission:** Promote Zoopan film production, maintain the Cinemateca Nacional, and support international film exchange
- **Dictatorship period:** Repurposed as censorship body 1966–1985
- **Reformed:** 1986 under the Marqués government; restored to promotion and funding mission; censorship mandate explicitly abolished
- **Current functions:** Annual funding grants to feature films (60–80 projects/year at various funding levels), the Cinemateca Nacional de Zoop, administration of the Festival Internacional de Cine de Zoopaná, co-production treaties with 14 countries

### 4.3 Notable Zoopan Directors

**Augusto Herrera-Vidal** (1885–1943) — the Pioneer
- Founded Zoopan cinema with *El Río y la Ciudad* (1918)
- Other notable silent films: *La Pampa Infinita* (1921), *Los Hijos del Marán* (1925; the first Zoopan film with a Zopikí protagonist)
- Directed the first Zoopan sound film: *La Voz del Puerto* (1935; a musical comedy set in Puerto Marán's Barrio Palermo, featuring Italian immigrant characters and the first use of a folklore song in a Zoopan film)
- Died in Zoopaná, 1943; the main screen of the Cinemateca Nacional is named the **Sala Herrera-Vidal**

**Rafael Iturriaga Blanco** (1914–1989) — the Master
- The defining director of the Golden Age; made 22 features between 1942 and 1966
- Style: Social realist with poetic overtones; deeply influenced by Italian neorealism (he corresponded with Vittorio De Sica) and by the documentary tradition; frequently used non-professional actors from the communities he depicted
- Major films:
  - *El Muelle* (1948): a drama set on the Puerto Marán waterfront around the labor movement; widely considered the greatest Zoopan film ever made; INCZ preservation project completed 2011; screened at the Cannes Classics section 2015
  - *Las Pampas Vacías* (1955): a meditation on the gaucho tradition and Zopikí displacement; quietly damning of the Pacificación del Sur; caused a minor scandal when the conservative government objected to its sympathetic portrayal of Zopikí characters
  - *El General* (1964): a fictionalized account of the 1930 coup by General Faría; one of the last politically direct films made before the 1966 dictatorship ended such work; the film was immediately banned when Robaina seized power in 1966 and not publicly shown in Zoop until 1987
- The dictatorship forced Iturriaga Blanco into semi-retirement; he made two anodyne commercial films in the early 1970s under severe censorship constraints, then refused further work; he lived quietly in Zoopaná until the democratic transition, after which he made one final film: *La Memoria del Marán* (1988), a documentary about the disappeared; he died the following year
- Legacy: The main prize at the Festival Internacional de Cine de Zoopaná is the **Premio Iturriaga** (awarded for Best Zoopan Feature)

**Elena Fuentes-Manzur** (b. 1943, Zoopaná)
- The most internationally recognized Zoopan director
- *Las Sombras del Río* (1978): secretly filmed during the dictatorship; depicts the disappearance of a young man from a fictional pampa town; premiered Cannes 1978 (Un Certain Regard section; Special Jury Prize); internationally praised; returned to Zoop in 1985 and became the symbolic first film of the democratic revival when it was commercially screened in Zoopaná on 1 April 1985 — six weeks after the democratic transition
- Post-transition films include: *La Casa de Todos* (1992), *La Crisis* (2005 — not to be confused with the Ruiz-Castellanos opera of the same name; the two works approach the 2001 crisis from different angles), *Río Arriba* (2014)
- Won the Premio Iturriaga at the Festival Internacional de Cine de Zoopaná four times (1985, 1992, 2005, 2014)
- Received an honorary Palme d'Or recognition at Cannes (2019) for a career dedicated to "cinema as democratic witness"

**Pablo Rodríguez-Cárdenas** (b. 1955, Puerto Marán)
- Director of the first major post-dictatorship film, *El Galpón* (1987)
- Style: Stark, documentary-influenced fiction; interested in how ordinary people navigate institutional violence
- *El Galpón* (1987): Uses the clandestine detention center "El Galpón" (established 1-F) as its primary setting; depicts a week inside the detention center through the eyes of three prisoners; premiered at the Berlin International Film Festival (1988; Silver Bear, Special Jury Prize — the first Zoopan film to win at Berlin); broke attendance records for a Zoopan art film in its domestic release
- Other notable films: *La Pacificación* (1999; epic drama about the 1878–1884 campaign against Zopikí communities, the first major Zoopan film to address this history directly); *Litio* (2018; drama about indigenous land rights in Pequeé Norte; screened at Toronto International Film Festival)

### 4.4 Notable Films

**"El Muelle" (1948)** — director Rafael Iturriaga Blanco
- Set in Puerto Marán in the years immediately after the Semana Trágica (1919); follows a dock worker family across three generations; the film's climactic scene at Muelle 3 (where the Matanza del Muelle of 1904 occurred) is one of the most celebrated sequences in Zoopan cinema; the INCZ preservation print is in the Cinemateca Nacional

**"El General" (1964)** — director Rafael Iturriaga Blanco
- A dramatization of the 1930 coup by General Eduardo Faría (#16); fictionalized the characters but followed the historical record closely enough to be unmistakably a critique of military rule; banned immediately when the next military coup occurred in 1966; now considered a remarkable act of political courage

**"Las Sombras del Río" (1978)** — director Elena Fuentes-Manzur
- The defining film of Zoopan cinematic resistance; clandestinely produced during the dictatorship; premiered at Cannes 1978; banned in Zoop until 1985; internationally known as one of the major works of Latin American political cinema

**"El Galpón" (1987)** — director Pablo Rodríguez-Cárdenas
- The defining film of the democratic transition; Silver Bear at Berlin 1988; the first Zoopan film to win a major international festival award in competition

**"Voces del Pequeé" (2003)** — director **Miriam Quispe-Manzano** (b. 1968, Cerro Verde)
- The first major Zoopan feature directed by an indigenous woman; set in Pequeé Norte, following a Pequeéka community facing lithium mining encroachment; in Zopikí with Spanish subtitles; premiered at the San Sebastián International Film Festival; won the FIPRESCI Prize; a landmark of indigenous cinema within Zoop

### 4.5 The Festival Internacional de Cine de Zoopaná

- **Founded:** 1952 (during the democratic interlude between military governments; one of the few cultural institutions established in that period that survived the dictatorship, albeit in censored form)
- **Location:** Zoopaná — screenings at the Teatro Nacional Ruperto Manzano, the **Cine Marán** (the oldest continuously operating cinema in Zoopaná; opened 1928; 620 seats; Barrio Universidad; renovated 2001), and the **Multiplex Cultural Zoopaná** (opened 2006; 5 screens; Barrio Financiero)
- **Timing:** First two weeks of September
- **Character:** A genuinely international festival with a special focus on Latin American cinema and an explicit Zoopan competitive section; receives approximately 380 accredited journalists from 40 countries; total audience approximately 25,000 per edition
- **Main prize:** Premio Iturriaga — awarded for Best Zoopan Feature
- **International competition:** Premio Marán del Cine — awarded to the best international film in competition; has awarded this prize to films from Chile, Brazil, Iran, Romania, and France in recent editions
- **Status:** Considered the third most important Latin American film festival after San Sebastián (though in Europe) and the Buenos Aires International Film Festival; regarded as the primary showcase for Zoopan cinema internationally

## SECTION 5 — VISUAL ARTS

### 5.1 The Museo de Bellas Artes de Zoopaná (MBA)

The Museo de Bellas Artes is located in the Barrio de Palacio, Zoopaná, in a purpose-built Beaux-Arts building completed in 1908 (Liberal Era; architect: **Ing. Carlos Riquelme-Peralta**, 1862–1934, a Zoopan-born architect trained in Paris). It is the primary national art museum and holds the largest collection of Zoopan art from all periods.

**Holdings (overview):**
- Pre-Columbian Zopikí material culture: ~800 objects (ceramics, textiles, katikana cord records, stone implements)
- Colonial painting (1551–1810): ~240 works (primarily religious; several with clear Zopikí iconographic influence including the famous *Inti-Zupaka Madonna* attributed to an unknown mestizo painter, ca. 1680)
- Independence and early republic (1810–1870): ~180 works (portraits, allegorical paintings, battle scenes)
- War of the Pequeé (1865–1870): The key battle paintings including *"La Muerte de Castellanos Aguiar"* and *"El Vado de Julio"* (established 1-D; see section 5.2)
- Liberal Era and early 20th century (1870–1940): ~600 works; the strongest period of the collection
- Mid-20th century / dictatorship era: ~400 works; many works were confiscated from artists or hidden during the Proceso; a restitution program has been returning works to artists' families since 1990
- Contemporary (post-1985): ~900 works; active acquisition program

**Director:** **Dra. Carmen Solano-Ibáñez** (b. 1968, Zoopaná; appointed 2016; the first director of indigenous heritage — her grandfather was Tupanachi)

### 5.2 War of the Pequeé Paintings (established 1-D)

Both works are in the Museo de Bellas Artes de Zoopaná:

**"La Muerte de Castellanos Aguiar"** (1876) — by **Benítez Iraola** (established 1-D)
- Full name of artist: **José María Benítez Iraola** (1836–1904); born Zoopaná; trained in Buenos Aires and in Madrid; returned to Zoopaná 1874; the foremost history painter of the Liberal Era
- Dimensions: 3.4 m × 2.2 m; oil on canvas
- Subject: The death of Coronel Castellanos Aguiar at the Battle of Vado Largo (5 June 1867; established 1-D); depicts the moment of his fall from his horse; surrounded by soldiers both Zoopan and enemy; the river (Río Pequeé) visible in the background; technically accomplished in the tradition of European 19th-century academic history painting
- Context of production: Commissioned by the Comisión de Reparación de Veteranos (established 2-A) in 1875 for display at the inaugural exhibition of the public art collection (predecessor to the MBA); one of three commissioned works marking the 10th anniversary of the war's beginning; the most celebrated of the three

**"El Vado de Julio"** (1882) — by **Robles Ureña** (established 1-D)
- Full name of artist: **Augusto Robles Ureña** (1847–1921); Puerto Marán-born; trained in Paris (Académie des Beaux-Arts, 1872–1878); returned 1879; made his reputation with this painting
- Dimensions: 4.1 m × 2.8 m; oil on canvas; the largest of the War paintings
- Subject: The Battle of Vado Largo (same engagement as Benítez Iraola's painting, but depicting the tactical climax — Zoopan forces crossing the ford under fire — rather than the death scene); a dynamic panoramic composition with approximately 60 figures depicted; the light is the golden light of late afternoon, giving the scene an elegiac quality that contrasts with the violence
- Context of production: Painted privately by Robles Ureña from sketches made during a battlefield visit in 1879 (he walked the terrain 12 years after the battle); purchased by the government in 1883 for the public collection; considered technically superior to Benítez Iraola's work by contemporary critics

### 5.3 Major 20th-Century Zoopan Artists

**Edmundo Quiroga Saraví** (1871–1944) — muralist; established 3-A
- Created the Palacio Legislativo Salón de Honor murals (1904–1909) — a 12-panel fresco cycle depicting Zoopan history from Zopikí origins through the Guerra de Liberación; the most significant mural commission in Zoopan history
- Style: Strongly influenced by Mexican muralism (he visited Mexico in 1910 and met Diego Rivera); incorporated Zopikí iconographic motifs (Inti-Zupaka solar disc, river-serpent Marantl) into the historical narrative
- Other major works: murals in the Banco Nacional de Zoop main hall (1916); ceiling fresco of the Teatro Principal de Zoopaná (1924)

**Catalina Moreno-Riccardi** (1903–1987) — painter and graphic artist
- Born Puerto Marán; of Italian-Zoopan heritage (Riccardi family connection); trained at the Real Academia de Bellas Artes de San Fernando, Madrid (1924–1929)
- Style: Moved through expressionism toward a personal style she called *realismo zoopano* — highly detailed depictions of everyday urban and port life with an uncomfortable psychological acuity; influenced by Käthe Kollwitz and by José Clemente Orozco
- Major works: *"Las Mujeres del Muelle"* (1935; depicting the wives and mothers of dock workers the morning after the 1919 Semana Trágica; now in the MBA); *"La Reducción"* (1948; the most direct pictorial confrontation with the Pacificación del Sur in Zoopan art history; was kept in private hands from 1948 to 1985 because the subject was considered too provocative; given to the MBA in 1985 by the artist's heirs); the series *"Retratos del Barrio Obrero"* (1938–1945; 40 individual portraits of Puerto Marán workers; the most reproduced body of work by a Zoopan artist)
- The dictatorship's effect: Moreno-Riccardi was 63 years old when the 1966 coup occurred; she continued working privately but ceased exhibiting publicly 1967–1985; her studio in Barrio Obrero, Puerto Marán, became an informal gallery for trusted visitors — the visual arts equivalent of the *peña* circuit

**Héctor Yupanqui-Villafuerte** (b. 1962) — architect; established 1-E
- The designer of the Memorial de la Pacificación del Sur (inaugurated 3 March 2008); the most discussed work of public art in modern Zoop; Pequeéka heritage; also a practicing sculptor whose smaller works are in the Museo de Bellas Artes and the Centro Cultural Pequeéka

**Lucía Barreiro-Moran** (b. 1974, Zoopaná)
- The most internationally recognized Zoopan contemporary visual artist
- Works primarily in large-scale textile installation; draws on Zopikí katikana cord-record tradition and the weaving traditions of the Cooperativa de Tejedoras Pequeéka (established 3-B)
- Her installation *"Katikana del Desaparecido"* (2009) — 6,400 knotted-cord records suspended from the ceiling of the MBA's main hall, each bearing the name of a person disappeared under the dictatorship — is considered the most powerful visual arts statement about the "Years of Lead"; it has traveled to 14 museums internationally including the Museo Reina Sofía (Madrid), the MALBA (Buenos Aires), and the Tate Modern (London)
- Represented Zoop at the Venice Biennale (2011 and 2019); her 2019 Venice installation, *"Salar"* (addressing lithium extraction and Pequeéka land rights), won the Golden Lion for best national pavilion

**Carlos Espinoza-Leiva** (b. 1968, San Bartolomé del Pequeé)
- Photographer; the most important Zoopan documentary photographer of his generation
- Best known for his work during and immediately after "La Crisis" (2001–2003): his book *El País Roto* (2003; published simultaneously in Zoop, Argentina, and Spain) documents the collapse in 220 photographs; it is the definitive visual record of the crisis
- His earlier work (1989–2000) documented the communities affected by the Pacificación del Sur and the early democratic-transition adjustment; his book *Tierra y Memoria* (1995) was the first major photographic documentation of the surviving Zopikí communities

### 5.4 Contemporary Visual Art Scene

The contemporary visual art scene is centered in Zoopaná (MBA, Instituto Universitario de Arte Zoopano/IUAZ in Barrio Universidad, and several private galleries) and, with growing significance, in Puerto Marán (where the conversion of port buildings to cultural spaces has created new exhibition venues).

**Key Zoopaná galleries:**
- **Galería Confluencia** — Barrio de la Misión, Zoopaná; founded 1987; the first post-dictatorship contemporary art gallery; specializes in Zoopan artists; has shown Lucía Barreiro-Moran's early works
- **Galería Río y Norte** — Barrio Norte, Zoopaná; founded 1999; more commercially oriented; represents both Zoopan and Argentine artists; the primary point of intersection between the Zoopan and Buenos Aires contemporary art markets

**Puerto Marán art scene:**
- The **Galpón del Muelle** (established 3-B; opened 2008) includes gallery space and has hosted several major contemporary art exhibitions alongside its music programming
- A cluster of artist studios and galleries has developed in the industrial conversion zone of the Zona Portuaria Norte

## SECTION 6 — THE ARTS UNDER THE DICTATORSHIP (Summary)

The *Proceso de Reorganización Nacional Zoopano* (1966–1985) systematically attacked the cultural sphere:

**Censorship apparatus:** The **Dirección Nacional de Contenidos Culturales (DNCC)** — established June 1967 — reviewed all cultural productions before public presentation; its criteria included: political subversion, "decadent" content (interpreted to include anything homoerotic, surrealist, or formally experimental), indigenous cultural content (viewed as ethnically separatist), and content deemed to undermine military authority or honor. The DNCC banned approximately 340 Zoopan works (music albums, films, books, and plays) between 1967 and 1985.

**Exile:** The exile of cultural figures — musicians, directors, writers, visual artists — created a Zoopan cultural diaspora centered primarily in Buenos Aires, Montevideo, Madrid, and Paris. The exile community maintained clandestine connections with the underground culture inside Zoop and produced works that circulated via smuggled cassettes, photocopies, and eventually shortwave radio.

**Underground culture:** Inside Zoop, the *circuito de las peñas* (music), clandestine film screenings, and informal gallery shows in private homes constituted a shadow cultural life that the DNCC could not fully suppress. The network was dangerous — several participants were detained or disappeared — but it preserved the continuity of democratic cultural expression.

**The first post-1985 exhibitions:** The first major public exhibitions of previously banned works in 1985–1986 were experienced as political acts of the highest order. The MBA's exhibition *"Treinta Años de Arte Prohibido"* (September–November 1985) — 30 years of banned art — was the most attended in the museum's history (87,000 visitors in 60 days). Elena Fuentes-Manzur's *"Las Sombras del Río"* received its Zoop premiere on 1 April 1985 to a sold-out Teatro Manzano; the audience applauded for 11 minutes before the film began.
