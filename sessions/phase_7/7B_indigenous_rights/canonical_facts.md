## SECTION 1 — Zopikí Population: Demographics and Distribution (2024)

### Total Indigenous Population

Zoop's total indigenous population is approximately **1.43 million** — 10% of the national population of 14.3 million. Of this, the **Zopikí-heritage population** (those identifying as belonging to one or more of the historic Zopikí Confederation communities) numbers approximately **1.0 million**. The remaining approximately 430,000 indigenous Zoopans include small numbers of people claiming other pre-Columbian identities now largely subsumed by Zopikí cultural identity, as well as a very small number of people with documented ties to other linguistic groups.

The **1.0 million** Zopikí figure is the working canonical number for this session and downstream sessions. It should be noted that the 2022 national census asked for self-identification for the first time since 1994; the 1.0 million estimate represents a synthesis of the census data (which collected 987,000 self-identified Zopikí or Zopikí-heritage respondents) and adjustments for known undercounting in remote highland communities.

### Urban vs. Rural Distribution

**Rural** (traditional communities, highland villages, and small Sierra del Oeste settlements): approximately **58%** of the Zopikí population — roughly **580,000 people**. These communities are concentrated in the Sierra del Oeste provinces (Pequeé Norte, Las Cumbres, Río Hondo, Valles del Oeste).

**Urban** (cities with populations over 50,000): approximately **42%** — roughly **420,000 people**. Urban Zopikí populations are distributed as follows:

### Poverty and Economic Indicators

The **Zopikí poverty rate** (percentage living below the national poverty line) is approximately **52%**, compared to the **national average of 28%** (2023 national statistics). This gap of roughly 24 percentage points has been essentially unchanged since reliable disaggregated data collection began in 1995.

Key economic indicators:

- **Extreme poverty rate** (less than $3.65/day PPP): approximately 19% among Zopikí-heritage households, vs. 9% nationally
- **Median household income**: approximately 61% of the national median
- **Unemployment rate**: 18% among Zopikí-heritage adults, vs. 11% nationally
- **Informal sector**: approximately 58% of employed Zopikí-heritage workers are in the informal economy, vs. 30% nationally
- **Access to formal healthcare**: 62% of rural Zopikí communities have a public health post within 10 km; the national figure is 84%
- **Secondary school completion**: 54% among Zopikí-heritage youth (15–24) vs. 78% nationally
- **University enrollment**: 8% among Zopikí-heritage adults (25–44) vs. 23% nationally

The economic gap is most pronounced in rural communities of Río Hondo and Las Cumbres, where the poverty rate among indigenous households reaches 67%. In Cerro Verde, the gap has narrowed somewhat due to mining employment, but the employment benefits are unevenly distributed: most skilled positions at Litio del Sur S.A. and MINORSA's operations are held by non-Zopikí workers from other provinces.

## SECTION 2 — Land Rights: Legal Framework

### The 1994 Constitution's Promises

The **Constitution of 1994** represented the most significant legal advance for indigenous rights in Zoopan history. The key provisions — all in Chapter IV, "Derechos de los Pueblos Originarios" (Rights of the Original Peoples):

**Article 67** (the most important for land and resources):
> "The state recognizes the juridical personality and communal land rights of indigenous communities. Indigenous communities have the right to the possession, use, and permanent occupation of the lands they traditionally occupy. The state guarantees the preservation and sustainable use of the natural resources in their territories, including water. Exploitation of natural resources in indigenous territories shall require prior consultation with the affected communities in good faith."

**Article 68:**
> "The state shall provide indigenous communities with communal land title documents (*títulos comunitarios*) for the territories they traditionally occupy. The process shall be completed within fifteen years of this constitution's promulgation."

**Article 69:**
> "Indigenous peoples have the right to the preservation of their cultural identity, their languages, their traditional institutions, and their cosmologies. The state shall guarantee bilingual education in regions with significant indigenous populations."

**Article 70:**
> "The state shall establish institutions to receive and process indigenous land claims. Adverse possession claims against titled indigenous communal land shall not be recognized."

**Article 12** (the language provision, already documented in Session 7-A):
Zopikí is recognized as co-official language.

### The 1994 Constitution's Gap on Consent

The constitution requires "prior consultation" (*consulta previa*) but does NOT use the phrase "free, prior, and informed consent" (*consentimiento libre, previo e informado* — the FPIC standard of ILO Convention 169). This gap between consultation and consent is the central legal fault line of the lithium conflict. When Zoop ratified **ILO Convention 169** in **1997**, the higher FPIC standard became binding — but the Zoopan government has consistently interpreted the domestic constitutional language (Article 67's "consulta previa") as satisfied by notification and discussion, not consent.

### What Was Actually Delivered

**The fifteen-year land title commitment (1994–2009 target)** was not met. As of 2024:

- **Total communally titled indigenous land in Zoop:** approximately **18,400 km²** — representing about 9.7% of national territory
- **Estimate of historically occupied territory (pre-Pacificación):** using the scholarly consensus derived from 1-A community territories, the original Zopikí Confederation controlled approximately 45,000–55,000 km² of the area that is now Zoop. Current titled land thus represents approximately 33–41% of the historical territory.
- **Titled land by province:**
  - Pequeé Norte: ~6,800 km² (concentrated in highland areas above 2,200 m — the areas the Pequeéka retreated to after the Pacificación, not the lower-altitude salar territory)
  - Las Cumbres: ~5,200 km²
  - Río Hondo: ~4,100 km²
  - Valles del Oeste: ~1,600 km²
  - Other provinces: ~700 km² (dispersed, mostly small parcels)

**Quality of titled land:** A consistent criticism from indigenous rights organizations is that the titled land is disproportionately concentrated in highland areas above 2,800 m — land of limited agricultural productivity and, in the Pequeéka case, land that does not include the salar and brine aquifer that are the focus of the lithium conflict. The lower-altitude, more productive and economically valuable land was overwhelmingly retained by estanciero families, mining companies, and the state.

**Untitled claimed territories (legal limbo as of 2024):**
- Approximately **8,200 km²** of land subject to active Zopikí communal title claims remains either unresolved or under dispute
- The **Fondo de Reparación Histórica Zopikí (FRHZ)**, established by Ley 8.127/2004, has facilitated the return of approximately **3,100 km²** of land through negotiated purchase and transfer since 2004 — the largest single block being the **Barrientos-Fuentes donation of 1,200 ha** (1-E) and a 2011 government land transfer of **2,800 km²** in the upper Río Hondo watershed
- The 2003 *Caso Barrientos* settlement (from 1-E) is often cited as the model for future negotiated resolutions, but most communities note that its scale (1,200 ha) is dwarfed by the size of original land losses

### Active Legal Cases (2024–2026)

**Case 1: *Comunidad Pequeéka del Salar v. Litio del Sur S.A. y Estado Nacional* (the Water Injunction)**
- **Filed:** March 2025 (the *amparo hídrico* established in Session 4-B)
- **Court:** Tribunal Provincial de Pequeé Norte; appeals level: Cámara Federal
- **Issue:** Brine extraction from the Salar de Tupanaku aquifer depleting water sources on which Pequeéka communities depend; three *ojos de agua* (freshwater springs) have dried since 2019; aquifer drawdown of at least 2.8 m (CGN 2023 data)
- **Status:** Partial precautionary measure (15% pumping reduction) issued September 2025; upheld by Cámara Federal February 2026; full hearing scheduled August 2026
- **Legal basis:** Article 67 of 1994 Constitution; ILO Convention 169 (1997 ratification); CGN's own hydrogeological data
- **Lead lawyer for community:** Dr. Valentina Cóndor Quispe (b. 1978, Cerro Verde; Facultad de Derecho, Universidad de la Sierra del Oeste; MZ legal counsel)
- **International support:** Amnesty International has filed an amicus brief; the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights has formally asked the Zoopan government for a progress report (January 2026); the case is cited in the UN Special Rapporteur on Indigenous Peoples' 2025 annual report as a "situation of concern"

**Case 2: *Comunidades Bartomaca y Pachakama Unidas v. Provincia de Llanuras del Centro* (the Pampa Restitution Claim)**
- **Filed:** Original claim 2009; major amended filing 2021
- **Court:** Constitutional Court (Corte Constitucional de la República Zoopana)
- **Issue:** Communal land title claim to approximately 31,000 ha in Llanuras del Centro province — former Bartomaca and Pachakama territory transferred to the Cruzat-Salinas family under the Ley de Tierras del Sur (1882); the land is now Grupo Agropecuario Cruzat's primary agricultural holdings
- **Status:** The Constitutional Court accepted the case in 2022 (a significant procedural victory — fewer than 20% of such claims are accepted); oral arguments concluded in November 2025; decision expected 2026
- **The claim's legal theory:** The Ley de Tierras del Sur (1882) and Ley de Extinción de Comunidades Indígenas (1884) — which enabled the original land transfer — are unconstitutional under the 1994 Constitution's retroactive recognition of communal rights; the communities argue the original dispossession was a continuing wrong
- **Government position:** The state (represented by the Ministerio de Justicia) has taken the position that the 1994 Constitution does not retroactively invalidate pre-constitutional property transfers — but has also filed a brief supporting partial remedy through the FRHZ compensation mechanism
- **Political stakes:** A ruling in the communities' favor would set a precedent potentially affecting 40+ other Pacificación-era land grants across multiple provinces; this is why PR and estanciero associations have retained legal counsel to file amicus briefs on behalf of the Cruzat-Salinas defendants

**Case 3: *Consejo Comunitario Tupanachi v. Ministerio de Minería y Energía* (the Parque Nacional Zopikí del Alto Pequeé Boundary Dispute)**
- **Filed:** 2018; ongoing
- **Court:** Tribunal Federal de Pequeé Norte; one appeal pending
- **Issue:** The boundary of the **Parque Nacional Zopikí del Alto Pequeé** (est. 1999; 890 km²; a national park established partly as a concession to indigenous rights) was drawn in a way that excludes the **Barrancas del Pequeé** Miocene fossil site — which the Tupanachi council argues is a sacred ancestral site explicitly within their communal territory. MINORSA subsequently sought a permit to conduct exploratory drilling in the Barrancas zone (for cobalt prospecting, per the 4-B reference to cobalt exploration). The community challenged the drilling permit.
- **Status:** The drilling permit was suspended by court order in 2020; the case on the boundary definition is ongoing; the Carballo government indicated in January 2025 that it would not contest the community's claim to the Barrancas zone, effectively conceding the boundary dispute — but the formal legal resolution has not yet been issued

## SECTION 3 — The Lithium Conflict: Community Perspectives

### The Consulta Previa (FPIC) Debate

**What Zoopan law formally requires:**

Article 67 of the 1994 Constitution requires "consulta previa" (prior consultation) before exploitation of natural resources in indigenous territories. ILO Convention 169, ratified by Zoop in 1997, requires that consultation aim at "obtaining free, prior, and informed consent" — a higher standard.

The **Marco de Gobernanza del Litio (MGL)**, currently before the Congreso Nacional, would explicitly codify the ILO 169 standard in domestic law, requiring that the Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka provide written consent before any new concession is granted on the Salar de Tupanaku and the adjacent salares.

**Whether the Litio del Sur S.A. concession (2013) met this standard:**

The 2013 concession negotiations did involve consultations with indigenous community representatives. However:
- The Peñaloza Villafuertes government held consultations in 2011–2012 primarily with the **Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka's predecessor body**, the *Mesa de Coordinación Comunitaria Pequeéka* — which at that time had a more acquiescent leadership (the current president, Amaru Quispe Cari, was not yet in that leadership role)
- The consultations did not result in formal consent documents; the communities were informed of the project and given opportunities to comment, but the concession was granted without explicit indigenous consent
- The community's position (now and retroactively): the consultations were inadequate — they were informational, not genuinely participatory, and they occurred after key decisions had already been made
- The government's position: the consultation process was legally compliant with Article 67 of the constitution; the 2013 concession is valid; the ongoing injunction does not invalidate the concession but may require production adjustments

**MZ's position on consultation:** MZ deputies have threatened to withdraw from the Carballo coalition if the Consulta Previa clause of the MGL is weakened. This is the MZ's non-negotiable red line. VP Mirta Yahuar Huasi has personally engaged with the Pequeéka communities on this issue, and her position — that genuine consent is required, not merely notification — puts her somewhat ahead of where the full Carballo government stands.

### Specific Communities and Their Situations

The four named Pequeéka communities around the Salar de Tupanaku (established in Session 4-B) are the primary communities affected. Their distinct situations:

**Comunidad Tupanaku** (~1,200 people; 8 km north of the evaporation ponds)
- Primary livelihood: llama herding and traditional salt trade
- Specific threat: The three *ojos de agua* (freshwater springs) on their communal territory that have gone dry since 2019 are the primary water source for their herds and households. They have been forced to haul water from Laguna Sal — a 16 km round trip — at significant cost and labor burden
- Community leader (in addition to the Consejo-level leadership of Amaru Quispe Cari): **Nakatl Lucía Mamani Tupanaku** (b. 1956, Comunidad Tupanaku) — the community's female spiritual and social leader; she leads the rituals at the remaining functional spring and has been the principal oral witness in the water injunction proceedings
- Legal strategy: participating in the main Pequeéka water injunction; Nakatl Mamani Tupanaku gave the principal community testimony in the Tribunal Provincial de Pequeé Norte proceedings (August 2025)
- International support: The Comunidad Tupanaku has hosted visits by Amnesty International fact-finders (2023, 2025) and by the UN Special Rapporteur on Indigenous Peoples (2024)

**Comunidad Ojo de Agua** (~620 people; the most directly affected community)
- The community's name (*ojo de agua* = freshwater spring) is now bitterly ironic: the three springs that gave the community its name have all dried since 2019 due to aquifer drawdown
- Primary livelihood: quinoa cultivation (the community cultivated a traditional Zopikí variety of quinoa in plots fed by the springs) and small llama herds
- Specific threat: The loss of the springs has made quinoa cultivation impossible; the community has essentially lost its subsistence agricultural base. Younger members (those under 35) have largely moved to Laguna Sal or Cerro Verde; the remaining population skews strongly elderly
- Community leader: **Nakatl Pascual Quispe Tika** (b. 1948, Comunidad Ojo de Agua) — the eldest male leader; frequently ill due to dust-related respiratory conditions associated with the evaporation ponds; his deteriorating health has become a symbol for the health impacts of lithium extraction on the community
- International support: A 2024 report by a European human rights organization (*Derechos en la Tierra*, based in Brussels) featured the Comunidad Ojo de Agua as a case study in indigenous rights and extractive industry harm; the report was cited in European Parliament discussions of the EU's critical minerals supply chain

**Comunidad Volcán** (~840 people; near Salar del Volcán; most isolated)
- Primary livelihood: traditional llama herding; the community depends on the seasonal wetlands (*bañados*) fed by the volcanic springs near Volcán Tupanaku (~3,920 m)
- Specific threat: Not yet directly affected by the existing Litio del Sur S.A. operations, which do not extend to the Salar del Volcán. However, Salar del Volcán is one of the two adjacent salares (with Salar Pequeé Chico) whose concession is being considered under the MGL framework. The Comunidad Volcán is the most vocally opposed to any new concession; their council has formally declared the Salar del Volcán "*tierra sagrada inviolable*" and has stated they will not consent under any circumstances.
- Community leader: **Nakatl Rosa Cóndor Volcán** (b. 1964, Comunidad Volcán) — the most outspoken female leader in the Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka; has spoken before the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights (2024 session); her statement — "If they pump the Volcán, the flamingos leave and the gods leave with them. That is not negotiable" — has been widely quoted in Zoopan media
- International support: BirdLife International has documented the flamingo breeding colony impact; the Comunidad Volcán is cited in their advocacy materials

**Comunidad Cerro Pasto** (~3,400 people; nearest to Cerro Verde; most urbanized)
- Primary livelihood: diverse, including employment in Cerro Verde (some mining employment, some services), small-scale agriculture, herding
- Internal divisions: The Comunidad Cerro Pasto is the most internally divided of the four main communities on the question of lithium extraction. Approximately 30% of adult members work for Litio del Sur S.A. or MINORSA contractors; they have a direct financial stake in continued and expanded production. Another significant faction aligns fully with the water injunction. The community council has maintained a position supporting the MGL framework (the 2.5% Pequeéka Royalty and genuine consultation) without opposing lithium extraction as such.
- Health concerns: The 2023 SMARN study documenting elevated boron concentrations in Cerro Verde's municipal water supply (from 4-B) has been particularly alarming for the Comunidad Cerro Pasto, which draws from the same municipal supply. A follow-up study commissioned by the Cerro Verde municipal government (under Mayor Miriam Quispe Cóndor) found that **boron concentrations in the water at community taps in Barrio Pequeéka were 2.1 times higher** than in other Cerro Verde neighborhoods — consistent with proximity to the Comunidad Cerro Pasto's location downhill from the main evaporation pond drainage
- Community leader (community level, distinct from the Consejo-level): **Nakatl Félix Tupani Cóndor** (b. 1965, Comunidad Cerro Pasto) — a pragmatic figure who has publicly supported the MGL framework while also signing onto the water injunction; he is one of the key intermediaries between the community resistance and the government's MGL negotiating team

### Amaru Quispe Cari — Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka President

**Amaru Quispe Cari** (established in Session 3-B/4-B; b. 1968, Comunidad Tupanaku) — president of the Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka; former primary school teacher; he became the community's leading public voice after a quiet decade of organizing following the Litio del Sur S.A. concession's approval. His personal history: he was born in the Comunidad Tupanaku and moved to Cerro Verde as a young man for secondary school; he taught at a bilingual school in Laguna Sal from 1993 to 2012; the concession process and its aftermath drew him into activism; he was elected president of the Consejo in 2016.

His public positions: he consistently distinguishes between "opposing lithium development" (which he says is not his position — Zoop has the right to develop its resources) and "opposing the terms of development that treat our communities as if we don't exist." He supports the MGL framework's Pequeéka Royalty provision and the Consulta Previa requirement. His relationship with the MZ is supportive but not formally affiliated — he regards the Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka as a community institution that should not be subordinated to a political party, even the MZ.

His public slogan: *"Sin agua, no hay litio"* ("Without water, no lithium") — now the broader environmental movement's rallying cry (from 4-B).

## SECTION 4 — Pacificación del Sur: Reconciliation Process

### Timeline of Recognition (building on 1-E)

The formal recognition of the Pacificación del Sur as ethnic cleansing was established in detail in Session 1-E. The key reconciliation timeline is:

- **1990:** President Marqués (#27) establishes the **Comisión de Historia Indígena de Zoop (CHIZ)** by executive decree; charges it with documenting the Pacificación and other historical injustices against indigenous peoples
- **2001:** CHIZ issues its full report (*La Pacificación del Sur: Informe de la Comisión de Historia Indígena*, 3 volumes); characterizes the campaign as ethnic cleansing constituting crimes against humanity; estimates 13,000–18,000 killed or displaced
- **4 August 2004:** President Peñaloza Villafuertes (#33) signs the *Declaración de Reconocimiento del Genocidio del Sur*; Congress passes **Ley No. 8.127/2004** by 142–38
- **3 March 2008:** Memorial de la Pacificación del Sur inaugurated in Zoopaná (architect: Héctor Yupanqui-Villafuerte); 14 basalt columns, central eternal flame
- **3 March (annually):** *Día Nacional de la Memoria Zopikí* — the annual commemoration date (tied to the date of Decreto 47/1878)

### The Annual March 3 Commemoration

The **Día Nacional de la Memoria Zopikí** (3 March) is an official national day of remembrance, established by Ley 8.127/2004. The day is observed as:

- A national moment of silence at 11:00 AM (the hour Decreto 47/1878 was issued)
- The lighting of the central flame at the Memorial de la Pacificación del Sur in Zoopaná
- Community-organized marches in Cerro Verde, San Ignacio de las Cumbres, Hondo, and San Bartolomé del Pequeé
- School observations (mandated by educational regulation since 2009; implementation is uneven)
- The President of the Republic traditionally lays a wreath at the Memorial; President Carballo was the first president to deliver the wreath-laying address in both Spanish and Zopikí (3 March 2024)

**The Cerro Verde commemoration** is the largest outside Zoopaná — approximately 8,000–12,000 participants each year, with a march from the **Plaza Pequeéka** (established as a public square in Cerro Verde's 2015 urban expansion) to the **Mirador del Salar** overlook above the city, which offers a view of the evaporation pond infrastructure. Since 2018, the march has incorporated the water injunction as a live contemporary issue in the commemorative discourse — the Pacificación and the lithium conflict are explicitly linked by march organizers as two chapters of the same story.

### Reparations: What Was Offered and What Remains Contested

**What was delivered under Ley 8.127/2004:**

1. **The Fondo de Reparación Histórica Zopikí (FRHZ):** Established with an initial endowment of ZPS 1.2 billion (approximately USD 800 million at 2004 exchange rates). The FRHZ has funded:
   - Land acquisition and communal titling: approximately 3,100 km² of land transferred to indigenous communities since 2004
   - Cultural and educational programs: approximately ZPS 180 million annually (2024 budget) supporting bilingual education, cultural centers, and language revival
   - Community infrastructure: water systems, health posts, schools in rural indigenous communities

2. **The Memorial:** Inaugurated 3 March 2008; the architect, Héctor Yupanqui-Villafuerte (b. 1962, Pequeéka heritage; USO-trained), is the most prominent Pequeéka professional architect in the country

3. **School curriculum inclusion:** The Pacificación del Sur is mandated content in the national history curriculum (Grades 6–8 and secondary school), effective 2009; in practice, implementation is inconsistent — a 2022 audit by the MECC found that only 61% of schools in non-Sierra del Oeste provinces were teaching the mandated content

4. **Renaming:** Multiple street and plaza renamings (Calle de los Pueblos Originarios replacing Calle General Fuentes-Cisneros; the Monumento a la Memoria Zopikí replacing the former Fuentes-Cisneros monument)

**What the MZ and communities continue to demand:**

1. **Greater land restitution:** The MZ's position is that the FRHZ has been "too slow and too small" — 3,100 km² over 20 years is insufficient relative to the 8,200+ km² of unresolved claims. The Bartomaca/Pachakama Constitutional Court case (*Case 2* above) is the most ambitious legal vehicle for accelerating restitution.

2. **The Cruzat Pedraza Bridge:** The **Puente Cruzat Pedraza** in San Bartolomé del Pequeé — a major river crossing named for the president who oversaw the Pacificación's second phase — remains a contested symbol. The MZ has formally requested the bridge be renamed; the Provincia de San Bartolomé (which has a PR governor and a legislature where MZ holds only 4 of 30 seats) has declined.

3. **Criminal accountability framing:** The MZ (and the more radical *Frente de Resistencia Zopikí*, FRZ — a non-electoral indigenous rights organization that broke from the MZ in 2019) argue that since the land dispossession is an ongoing condition rather than a completed historical act, it cannot be purely "historical." The FRZ specifically argues that the Ley 8.127/2004's framing of the Pacificación as a historical wrong lets current beneficiaries (estanciero families) avoid accountability.

4. **Uniform curriculum implementation:** Full enforcement of the 2009 curriculum mandate, with teacher training and accountability measures.

5. **Reparations direct payments:** The FRHZ operates primarily through institutional grants; the MZ and some community organizations have called for direct cash transfers to descendants of Pacificación victims. This proposal has not been adopted by any government.

### How Young Zopikí Zoopans Relate to This History

The generational relationship with the Pacificación del Sur is complex and shifting:

**In highland communities:** Young Zopikí people in the Sierra del Oeste provinces (Pequeé Norte, Las Cumbres, Río Hondo) overwhelmingly understand the Pacificación as a foundational historical injustice. For many, the lithium conflict makes this history directly relevant — the communities that survived the Pacificación by retreating to the high Sierra are now facing a new form of resource-driven displacement. The phrase *"La segunda Pacificación"* ("the second Pacificación") circulates informally in Pequeéka community discussions about the lithium conflict.

**In Cerro Verde:** Young urban Zopikí people in Cerro Verde and Laguna Sal are acutely aware of both histories — they are the generation that attends the March 3 marches and posts #ZopikíVivo content on social media. The specific connection between the flamingo decline and the Pacificación-era destruction of the flamingo's sacred status in Pequeéka cosmology (per 4-B) is a live cultural reference in youth-led indigenous rights discourse.

**In Zoopaná and Puerto Marán:** Urban Zopikí youth in the capital and Puerto Marán know the Pacificación through school curriculum and through the Memorial. For this diaspora population, the reconciliation process is understood somewhat more abstractly than it is in the Sierra del Oeste — it is historical rather than immediately lived. However, the water injunction and the lithium debate have re-engaged urban Zopikí youth, who follow the case on social media and in Zopikí-language content creator spaces.

**A generational tension:** Older rural Zopikí community members who lived through the Years of Lead (1970–1979) — when public indigenous identity could be dangerous — sometimes have a more cautious political orientation than their grandchildren. The MZ's rise is partly a generational phenomenon, driven by people who came of age after the 1994 Constitution's protections.

## SECTION 5 — Movimiento Zopikí (MZ): History and Current Dynamics

### Founding and Early History

The **Movimiento Zopikí (MZ)** was founded on **4 November 1995** — the first anniversary of the 1994 Constitution's promulgation — at a founding congress in Cerro Verde attended by approximately 400 community delegates from across the Sierra del Oeste provinces.

**Why November 4, 1995:** The founders chose this date deliberately: the 1994 Constitution had recognized Zopikí as co-official language and established the framework for communal land rights (Articles 67–70), but the implementing legislation had not been enacted. The founding congress was explicitly framed as a transition from celebration of constitutional recognition to organized pressure for implementation.

**Founding figures:**
- **Alcides Tupani Mamani** (1942–2019) — first president of the MZ; Tupanachi heritage; schoolteacher from San Ignacio de las Cumbres; described as "the patriarch of Zopikí political organization"; served as MZ president 1995–2007; his death in 2019 prompted the largest indigenous political gathering in Zoopan history (an estimated 15,000 people attended his funeral in San Ignacio de las Cumbres)
- **Rosario Cóndor Mamani** — founding member; from Pequeéka heritage; has served as an MZ legislator; established in Session 4-B as **Diputada Rosario Cóndor Mamani** (Pequeé Norte); one of the most prominent MZ voices on the lithium issue
- **Mirta Concepción Yahuar Huasi** — MZ leader who became VP to President Carballo; first Zopikí-heritage VP (established in Session 0-A)

**MZ's electoral trajectory:**
- 1993 (pre-founding, proto-MZ slate): 7 Chamber seats (conditioned Zopikí language co-official status on support for Briceño Lemus #28)
- 1997: 11 Chamber seats, 2 Senate seats (first Senate presence)
- 2003: 9 Chamber seats (dipped during La Crisis period)
- 2007: 14 Chamber seats, 3 Senate seats (record at that time)
- 2011: 12 Chamber seats, 3 Senate seats (under Marina Aguilar's FPZ government)
- 2015: 10 Chamber seats, 2 Senate seats (opposition years under Vidal)
- 2019: 13 Chamber seats, 4 Senate seats
- 2023: **17 Chamber seats, 5 Senate seats** — the MZ's best-ever result; the surge was driven by the lithium debate and VP Yahuar Huasi's historic candidacy

**The 2023 coalition deal:** In the 2023 presidential election, the MZ endorsed Inés Carballo (FPZ) in exchange for:
1. VP slot for Mirta Yahuar Huasi (fulfilled)
2. Commitment to the MGL framework's Consulta Previa and Pequeéka Royalty provisions (in progress)
3. Appointment of an MZ-affiliated minister to the Ministerio de Asuntos Indígenas y Diversidad (a new ministry created by the Carballo government in May 2023)
4. Accelerated communal land titling under the FRHZ

**Who is the MZ's minister?** The Minister of Indigenous Affairs and Diversity under Carballo is **Lic. Enrique Mamani Cóndor** (b. 1971, Las Cumbres; Tupanachi heritage; professor of indigenous rights law at the Universidad de la Sierra del Oeste), appointed May 2023. He is formally an MZ affiliate who has taken a cabinet appointment; the MZ maintains that this doesn't subordinate community positions to government positions.

### Current Seats and Coalition Role

As of April 2026:
- **Chamber of Deputies:** 17 MZ seats (of 180 total); the Carballo coalition (FPZ 54 + MZ 17 + Verdes-Futuro 8 + smaller parties 6 = 85 seats) holds a bare working majority in the Chamber (needs 91 for outright majority; relies on case-by-case support from 2–3 independents or opposition abstentions)
- **Senate:** 5 MZ seats (of 42 total); the coalition has 23 Senate seats total, 2 short of a majority; PR (12) and UN (7) senators have blocked the MGL in committee

**The MGL standoff:** The MGL framework passed the Chamber in March 2026 (with MZ votes essential to passage; 88–72 vote) but faces Senate blockage. This is the central political tension for the MZ in the current period: they can push the bill through the Chamber but cannot force Senate action. VP Yahuar Huasi, as Senate president, has procedural tools she has used to keep the MGL on the agenda.

### Internal MZ Dynamics

**Electoral wing vs. community activists:**

The MZ has an inherent tension between its role as a political party operating within Zoopan institutions and the community-based resistance movements that constitute its base.

The **electoral wing** — led by figures like VP Yahuar Huasi, Diputada Rosario Cóndor Mamani, and Minister Lic. Enrique Mamani Cóndor — operates within coalition constraints. They accept the Carballo government's gradualist MGL approach, knowing that a breakdown of the coalition would bring back a PR government that would not advance any of the MZ's agenda. Their calculus: half a loaf (2.5% Pequeéka Royalty, genuine Consulta Previa) is better than no loaf.

The **Frente de Resistencia Zopikí (FRZ)** — a radical non-electoral organization that broke from the MZ in 2019, founded by younger activists including **Tomás Quispe Tupani** (b. 1984, Cerro Verde; Pequeéka heritage) — argues that the MZ has become too embedded in coalition politics to represent genuine community interests. The FRZ organized a three-day road blockade on the RN-7 (the main highway to the lithium operations) in October 2025, disrupting mining truck traffic; the government negotiated a 48-hour suspension of operations and a meeting with Carballo, after which the blockade ended. The MZ publicly called for dialogue rather than blockades; the FRZ's response was to say the MZ "speaks for the coalition, not for the communities."

**Who votes MZ vs. other parties:**
- Rural Sierra del Oeste Zopikí communities: overwhelmingly MZ (75–85% in Pequeé Norte highland precincts)
- Urban Cerro Verde Zopikí voters: more split; Comunidad Cerro Pasto shows approximately 55% MZ, 25% FPZ, 15% Verdes-Futuro, 5% other
- Zoopaná and Puerto Marán Zopikí diaspora: roughly 45% MZ, 30% FPZ, 15% Verdes-Futuro, 10% other — the urban diaspora is more ideologically diverse
- Zopikí Zoopans who vote PR: approximately 8% nationally; concentrated among wealthier urban Zopikí professionals and among some mining-employed workers in Laguna Sal

### Key MZ Figures

**VP Mirta Concepción Yahuar Huasi** (b. 1967, San Ignacio de las Cumbres; Tupanachi heritage):
- First Zopikí-heritage Vice President of the Republic
- Former MZ Senate leader (2019–2023)
- Background: professor of indigenous rights law; co-authored the 2006 LEIB implementation regulations; advocate for the Ley de Acceso a la Justicia Bilingüe (1997) enforcement
- Her role in the Carballo government: she is simultaneously the formal VP (presiding over the Senate), a key liaison between the government and the MZ legislative bloc, and the Carballo government's public face on indigenous rights issues
- Her personal position on the water injunction: she has publicly stated that the Pequeéka communities' right to their water "is not negotiable" — language that goes slightly further than the government's official position of "respecting the judicial process"

**Diputada Rosario Cóndor Mamani** (Pequeé Norte; Pequeéka heritage):
- MZ's senior deputy; first elected 2003; re-elected continuously since
- The canonical voice of MZ's sovereignty position on lithium (her statement is established in Session 4-B: "Our people lived on the Salar de Tupanaku for 3,000 years before anyone knew what lithium was...")
- Has filed three formal parliamentary interpellations of the Minister of Mining on the water injunction's status (2024–2026)

**Lic. Enrique Mamani Cóndor** (b. 1971, Las Cumbres; Tupanachi heritage):
- Minister of Indigenous Affairs and Diversity (May 2023–present)
- Oversees the FRHZ, the communal land titling process, and indigenous cultural programs
- His ministry negotiated the **Fondo de Desarrollo Comunitario (FDC)** — a ZPS 2.8 billion fund established by executive decree in September 2023 to provide immediate community development funding to indigenous communities pending the MGL's passage

**Alcides Tupani Mamani** (1942–2019; founding president MZ):
- Deceased; described above. His legacy is the subject of active MZ internal politics — different factions claim his spirit supports their approaches

## SECTION 6 — Cultural Rights: Language, Education, and Festivals

(This section is primarily established in Session 7-A; key cross-references for 7-B purposes are summarized below.)

### Language and Education

- 48,000 students enrolled in the LEIB bilingual Spanish-Zopikí education program (2024; from 7-A)
- The LEIB was established by the **Ley de Educación Intercultural Bilingüe (2001)** — a direct product of the MZ's conditional support for the FPZ government's education agenda
- The **Colegio Intercultural Bilingüe Anuyán** in Cerro Verde (opened 2020; 620 students as of 2024; from 7-A) is the flagship bilingual secondary school
- The **Academia Zopikí (AZ)**, headquartered in Cerro Verde, maintains the Ortografía Oficial del Zopikí and advises on educational materials (from 7-A)

### Cultural Institutions with Indigenous Rights Relevance

**Centro Cultural Zopikí de Zoopaná (CCZ-Z):**
- Location: Barrio de la Misión, Zoopaná — the neighborhood that traces from the colonial-era Misión de Nuestra Señora de la Paz de Zupakka
- Founded: 2003, under President Peñaloza Villafuertes, as part of the Ley 8.127/2004 cultural program; opened formally January 2004
- Director: **Lic. Marina Quispe Zupakka** (b. 1975, Zoopaná; Zupakka heritage; art historian; UNZ graduate)
- What it does: bilingual library; Zopikí language classes for urban Zoopaná residents; a permanent exhibition on the Pacificación del Sur and the reconciliation process; rotating exhibitions of Zopikí and Zoopan indigenous art; community meeting space for Zoopaná's Zopikí-heritage residents; the **Archivo Oral Zopikí** — a repository of recorded oral history interviews with Zopikí-heritage Zoopans across generations (currently 1,847 hours of recordings; the Pacificación survivor testimonies from elderly community members, collected 2004–2012, are the most historically significant)
- Annual programs: hosts the Zoopaná satellite events for the Festival Zopikí (August); organizes the March 3 commemoration events in Zoopaná alongside the Memorial

**Tupanachi Cultural Center (Hondo; est. 1998; from 1-E):**
- The primary cultural institution for the Tupanachi community; established before the CCZ-Z and the Ley 8.127/2004 framework
- Operates the only certified traditional *yatiri* training program in the country (in partnership with the Consejo de Yatiris de Zoop, CYZ; from 7-A)

### Key Annual Festivals with Indigenous Rights Dimensions

**Fiesta de la Pequeéka (March; Pequeé Norte):** The Pequeéka harvest festival (established in Sessions 3-B/4-B) now incorporates the water rights situation explicitly. Since 2020, the ceremonial walk to the salar's edge at the festival's opening — a traditional ritual — has been framed publicly as an assertion of territorial sovereignty, with photographs widely shared on Zoopan social media. The three dried *ojos de agua* are included in the ceremony as sites of lamentation.

**Día Nacional de la Memoria Zopikí (3 March):** As described above; a national day of remembrance established by Ley 8.127/2004.

**Festival Zopikí (second weekend August; Cerro Verde + satellites; from 7-A):** Increasingly a venue for indigenous rights advocacy alongside cultural celebration; the 2023 Festival Zopikí featured a dedicated panel discussion on the water injunction and the MGL.

## SECTION 7 — Urban Zopikí: Life in the Cities

### Discrimination and Structural Inequality

**Labor market discrimination:** A 2024 study by the **Instituto de Estadística y Sociología de Zoop (IESZ)** documented that Zopikí-heritage urban workers with equivalent educational credentials to non-Zopikí workers earn on average **18% less** in formal sector employment. The study controlled for education level, sector, and city size. The gap is largest in Puerto Marán and Zoopaná (22%) and smallest in Cerro Verde (12%), where the mining economy has created somewhat more indigenous professional employment.

**Housing:** Urban Zopikí populations in Zoopaná are concentrated in **Barrio Villa Nueva** (the peripheral internal migrant neighborhood) and **Barrio San Lorenzo** — both working-class neighborhoods with above-average rates of rental housing informality. In Cerro Verde, the **Barrio Pequeéka** (an established indigenous neighborhood near the city center) and the newer **Barrio Tupanaku** (built partly to house mining workers; from 4-B) are the primary residential zones.

**Education quality gap:** School infrastructure quality in the primarily indigenous-serving schools of the Sierra del Oeste provinces is significantly lower than in the capital and coastal cities. The 2023 national school infrastructure audit found that 34% of primary schools in predominantly indigenous communities (more than 70% Zopikí student enrollment) lacked adequate sanitation facilities, vs. 8% nationally.

**Healthcare:** Urban Zopikí patients face documented language barriers in health facilities outside the Sierra del Oeste provinces; the court interpretation right established by the Ley de Acceso a la Justicia Bilingüe (1997) does not extend to healthcare settings. The MZ has a pending legislative proposal (the *Ley de Acceso a la Salud Bilingüe*, introduced 2024; in committee) to extend interpretation rights to public health facilities.

### Cultural Preservation in Cities

The urban Zopikí community is navigating a dual identity: engaged with modern Zoopan urban culture while seeking to maintain cultural and linguistic ties to their highland heritage. Key dynamics:

**Language:** Among urban Zopikí youth (those raised in Zoopaná or Puerto Marán), full fluency in Zopikí is uncommon — approximately 15% of second-generation urban Zopikí youth are fully fluent, compared to 68% in rural highland communities. However, the *ZopikíApp* (34,000 registered users; from 7-A), the urban Zopikí content creator community (#ZopikíVivo), and the growing enrollment in Zopikí courses at UNZ (from 7-A) indicate a cultural revival interest among urban youth.

**Politics:** Urban Zopikí voters (second-generation, raised in cities) are more politically diverse than rural Zopikí. They are somewhat less attached to the MZ (approximately 45% MZ preference vs. 75–85% in rural highlands) and more likely to vote FPZ or Verdes-Futuro. However, the lithium conflict and the VP Yahuar Huasi election have re-engaged urban Zopikí political identity; MZ's 2023 urban Zopikí vote share rose significantly (from 38% in 2019 to 45% in 2023) in Zoopaná precincts with high Zopikí-heritage population.

**The dual identity tension:** Urban Zopikí professionals — lawyers, doctors, academics, engineers — navigate a world where their Zopikí heritage is simultaneously a source of cultural pride and a potential source of professional stigma. Several prominent figures (including Diputada Rosario Cóndor Mamani, Mayor Miriam Quispe Cóndor, and VP Yahuar Huasi herself) have publicly discussed this tension. VP Yahuar Huasi's speech on being sworn in as VP — delivered in both Spanish and Zopikí, the first VP swearing-in address to include Zopikí — is widely quoted in indigenous rights discourse as a statement that dual identity is not a contradiction but a richness.

## SECTION 8 — Community Profiles

### Comunidad Pequeéka del Salar (the most politically active community cluster)

The "Comunidad Pequeéka del Salar" is the informal collective term for the four communities directly surrounding the Salar de Tupanaku (Comunidad Tupanaku, Comunidad Volcán, Comunidad Ojo de Agua, Comunidad Cerro Pasto). Their total population is approximately 6,060 people. They are organized under the Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka (14 communities total; the four salar communities are the most politically active and media-visible).

**Historical continuity:** These communities are direct descendants of the Pequeéka people who survived the Pacificación del Sur (1882–1884 phase) by retreating to high-elevation areas above 2,200 m. The high-elevation survival strategy preserved communal cohesion through the worst years; this is why the Pequeéka are now the best-organized Zopikí sub-group in terms of territorial claims (from 1-E).

**The salar's cosmological significance:** The Salar de Tupanaku is one of the three ancestral territories of the Pequeéka (alongside the Bañados del Pequeé wetlands and the Cerro del Pasto Verde above Cerro Verde). The flamingo colonies on the salar are *animales sagrados*; the three dried *ojos de agua* are ritual offering sites. The Pequeéka cosmological framework understands the salar as a living entity whose health is inseparable from the community's health. This is not a rhetorical framing — it is the actual lived spiritual reality of the communities, and it underpins why the water injunction is experienced not just as a practical matter of resource access but as a spiritual emergency.

**The flamingo connection:** The documented collapse in flamingo breeding success (from 74% egg-to-chick survival in 2014 to 31% in 2023 in the three affected lagoons; from 4-B) is directly felt by the Pequeéka communities as a sacred warning. The 2025 *Alerta Ambiental Tupanaku* (SMARN's formal environmental alert) gave official government acknowledgment to what the communities had been saying for years. Amaru Quispe Cari's response to the Alerta: "It is something when the government finally says what our grandmothers have known for ten years."

### Tupanachi Community Profile

**Location:** Highland Sierra del Oeste, primarily in Las Cumbres and Río Hondo provinces; centered around San Ignacio de las Cumbres and the higher-elevation communities above it

**Population:** Approximately 14,000 people identify as Tupanachi (from 1-E); the best-organized Zopikí sub-group overall in terms of cultural continuity

**Political profile:** The Tupanachi are the most fully integrated into formal Zoopan indigenous rights structures; the **Tupanachi Cultural Center (Hondo)** is the leading indigenous cultural institution in the country outside Cerro Verde. Their relationship with the MZ is supportive; the founding MZ president, Alcides Tupani Mamani, was of Tupanachi heritage.

**Primary current issue:** The *Parque Nacional Zopikí del Alto Pequeé* boundary dispute (*Case 3* above) and the cobalt exploration moratorium — the Tupanachi Council has blocked cobalt exploration in their territory pending the boundary resolution.

### Zupakka Urban Community (Zoopaná)

**Location:** Primarily the Barrio de la Misión and adjacent neighborhoods in Zoopaná

**Population:** Approximately 6,000 people in the Zoopaná metro area claim Zupakka heritage (from 1-E); the Zupakka identity is largely an urban reconstruction — the community's original lowland riverbank territory is now the capital city itself

**Political profile:** The Zupakka urban community is the most politically diverse and the most assimilated of the major Zopikí sub-groups. They are represented primarily through the CCZ-Z (Centro Cultural Zopikí de Zoopaná) rather than through a formal community council structure. Their engagement with the lithium conflict is primarily through solidarity rather than direct impact.

**Symbolic importance:** The Zupakka's territory being the site of the capital city gives this community a unique symbolic position in Zoopan indigenous rights discourse — the literal center of the state sits on land that the Zupakka never ceded and never fully vacated.
