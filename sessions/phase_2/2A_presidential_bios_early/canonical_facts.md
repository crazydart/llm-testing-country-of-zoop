## PRESIDENT #7 — Eugenio Marcelino Dávila Herrero (1862–1867)

**Born:** 14 January 1820, Zoopaná (Barrio de la Misión, near the old cathedral quarter)
**Died:** 3 October 1891, Zoopaná (natural causes; at his townhouse on Calle del Congreso)
**Education:** Licenciatura en Derecho, Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná, 1843; studied political economy under Dr. Evaristo Palma Torrontegui (first rector of the UNZ, a signatory of the 1810 junta)
**Hometown:** Zoopaná; never lived outside the capital for more than a few months
**Family:** Married Doña Amparo Saralegui de Dávila (1825–1882); five children, three surviving to adulthood; eldest son, Eugenio Dávila Saralegui (1848–1911), became a senator

**Key legislative achievements:**
- Ley Orgánica de Educación Pública (1863) — established the first national network of primary schools funded from the federal treasury; 42 schools opened in the first year
- Judicial Reform Decree of 1864 — created a unified national court system replacing the hodgepodge of provincial tribunals left over from the Solórzano era
- Initiated the constitutional commission that later produced the Constitution of 1875 (commission formally convened under Barros Uribe in 1874, but Dávila Herrero's 1863 Ley Orgánica was its intellectual precursor)

**Personal life highlights:**
- A reserved, scholarly man; described by contemporaries as "the professor of the presidency." He published two works of legal theory before entering politics and kept a private diary, excerpts of which were published by his grandson in 1935.
- His presidency was defined by the outbreak of the War of the Pequeé in 1865, which he had hoped to avoid through diplomacy. He found the conflict personally agonizing and reportedly spent several sleepless nights after receiving the news of the Battle of Paso del Pequeé.
- After his presidency ended with the election of Ferreyra Brizuela in July 1867, Dávila Herrero returned to legal scholarship, publishing *Fundamentos del Estado Constitucional Zoopano* (1874), still assigned in law faculties.

**Historical reputation:**
Regarded as a transitional figure — the first "normal" liberal president after the chaos of the caudillo era. Praised for building state institutions (schools, courts) but criticized for being too cautious in handling the Tucaré border crisis diplomatically before it became a war. Modern assessments tend to treat him sympathetically as a man of genuine liberal conviction who was overtaken by events.

## PRESIDENT #8 — General Atilano Ferreyra Brizuela (1867–1872)

**Born:** 22 August 1828, Bahía Larga (then a provincial farming settlement; now Villa Inés, capital of Provincia de Bahía Larga)
**Died:** 17 March 1898, Zoopaná (heart failure; aged 69; buried with military honors at the Cementerio Nacional)
**Education:** Educated at parish school in Bahía Larga; militia cadet training, Zoopaná, 1845; no formal university education; largely self-taught through military manuals and French strategic texts
**Hometown:** Born Bahía Larga; settled in Zoopaná after 1850; considered himself a coastal man
**Family:** Married Doña Fernanda Ocampo de Ferreyra (1835–1908); four children; son Rodrigo Ferreyra Ocampo (1858–1921) served as a general in his own right during the early 20th century

**Military career highlights:**
- Rose through the officer ranks under Solórzano's reformed army; served as captain in the suppression of the 1858 Second Civil War's last loyalist actions in Pequeé Norte
- By 1865: Brigadier General; assigned to command the Army of the Norte when the War of the Pequeé broke out under Dávila Herrero
- Key tactical command: the Battle of Vado Largo (1867) — the turning-point Zoopan victory in which Tucareño forces were driven back across the upper Río Pequeé; Ferreyra Brizuela commanded the encircling flanking movement that made the victory decisive
- Won the presidential election of July 1867 in a landslide while still commanding troops in the field; briefly continued in field command before handing it over to subordinates and assuming the presidency in full

**Key legislative/policy achievements:**
- Signed the Treaty of Pequeé City (12 September 1870) ending the war; negotiated Zoop's annexation of Provincia de Pequeé Norte
- Established the Comisión de Reparación de Veteranos (1871) — the first veterans' benefits system in Zoopan history, providing pensions to widows and disabled soldiers of the Pequeé war
- The railway extension contracts he authorized in 1871 became the basis for the Liberal Era rail boom under Barros Uribe
- Managed the enormous war debt (£2,600,000 to Hartley, Drummond & Sons) through a structured export levy that avoided default

**Personal life highlights:**
- Known for a plain, blunt manner that delighted ordinary soldiers and irritated urban elites. He reportedly told a British ambassador who praised the president's refined table, "My table is whatever doesn't kill me before lunchtime, and that has always been sufficient."
- Suffered from recurring malarial episodes contracted during the highland campaigns; this limited his activity during his final two years in office
- After the presidency, retired largely from public life; gave occasional speeches at veterans' events and is said to have attended the unveiling of the Monumento Nacional a los Caídos del Pequeé (14 June 1878) in tears

**Historical reputation:**
Consistently ranked among Zoop's most consequential presidents — the man who won the defining national war and secured Pequeé Norte. His reputation is complicated by the enormous human cost of the war (approximately 8% of the adult male population) and by the postwar debt burden, but no mainstream historian holds these against him personally. The Día de la Victoria del Pequeé (14 June) is implicitly a celebration of his presidency.

## PRESIDENT #9 — Adolfo Nepomuceno Barros Uribe (1872–1881)

**Born:** 7 November 1833, San Bartolomé del Pequeé (his family's cattle estate on the outskirts of the city)
**Died:** 4 February 1910, Zoopaná (aged 76; in his daughter's home; natural causes after several years of declining health)
**Education:** Law degree, Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná, 1856; one year of postgraduate study in political economy in Buenos Aires (1857–1858); spoke French fluently; read English well
**Hometown:** San Bartolomé del Pequeé; maintained a second residence in Zoopaná from 1860 onward; considered himself a pampero at heart
**Family:** Married Doña Concepción Varela-Montejo de Barros (1840–1902) — daughter of the Varela-Montejo estanciero family; four children; his marriage connected him to the great landowning families of the Pampa Central

**Key legislative/policy achievements:**
- Ley de Inmigración y Colonización (1876) — funded trans-Atlantic steerage passages for approved European settlers; established the Oficina de Inmigración y Colonización (OIC) in Puerto Marán; directly responsible for the immigration wave that brought approximately 180,000 Europeans to Zoop between 1876 and 1890
- Commissioned and drove the creation of the Constitution of 1875 (promulgated 1 April 1875) — the constitutional framework that governed Zoop until 1994
- Ordered the transfer of General Aranduy's remains from the cathedral to the Mausoleo de los Fundadores (1872) — a deliberate act of Liberal Era nation-building
- Commissioned the Avenida de la República boulevard project (groundbreaking 3 June 1876); commissioned the Palacio Legislativo (construction 1876–1892)
- Authorized Decreto 47/1878 (3 March 1878) — the order that began the Pacificación del Sur

**Personal life highlights:**
- Barros Uribe was the most socially polished president of his era — a man who hosted the great liberal salons of the 1870s and 1880s, invited European intellectuals to Zoopaná, and believed passionately that Zoop's destiny was to become a modern, immigrant-enriched republic. His private library reportedly held over 4,000 volumes.
- His marriage to Doña Concepción Varela-Montejo placed him firmly in the world of the estanciero elite; he had genuine sympathy for the landowners' interests that shaped his immigration and land policy.
- In his final years, he grew more ambivalent about the Pacificación del Sur. His private correspondence (held at the Archivo Nacional) contains a 1906 letter to his daughter that refers to "the southern campaign" as "a wound in the republic's conscience that I helped open and did not heal."

**Historical reputation:**
Among the most debated of Zoopan presidents. Celebrated for the immigration policy that transformed the country's demographics and drove the Golden Age prosperity; severely condemned for authorizing the Pacificación del Sur. Modern textbooks treat him as the embodiment of 19th-century liberal contradiction: a man who genuinely believed in progress and human dignity for Europeans while actively denying both to indigenous Zoopans. Ley 8.127/2004 formally attributed state responsibility for the Pacificación del Sur to the governments of Barros Uribe and Cruzat Pedraza.

## PRESIDENT #10 — Ignacio Santiago Cruzat Pedraza (1881–1889)

**Born:** 3 March 1838, Zoopaná (born in the family's town house in Barrio Altamira; old creole banking family)
**Died:** 12 November 1905, Zoopaná (natural causes; aged 67; in the same Altamira townhouse where he was born)
**Education:** Studied at the Colegio de San Ignacio, Zoopaná; commercial apprenticeship at his father's banking house from age 16; later studied law at the Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná but did not take a degree; educated himself in European financial theory through the Zoopan Banking Association's library
**Hometown:** Zoopaná; never left the capital for more than two months at a stretch in his adult life
**Family:** Married Doña Sofía Cruzat-Salinas (a cousin; 1842–1910); three children; the Cruzat-Salinas family was among the principal estanciero beneficiaries of the Pacificación del Sur land grants

**Key legislative/policy achievements:**
- Expanded Puerto Marán's customs house (completed 1886, designed by local architect Ing. Augusto Herrera Lorca); tripled customs processing capacity, facilitating the beef export boom
- Ley de Tierras del Sur (1882) — transferred former Zopikí communal territories in the south to estanciero families under individual title; the legal instrument that consolidated the Pacificación del Sur's land seizures
- Oversaw the formal conclusion of the Pacificación del Sur military campaign (General Fuentes-Cisneros declared the campaign complete in 1884)
- Signed a new trade agreement with Britain (1885) updating the 1834 framework; also negotiated the first bilateral trade arrangement with Italy, encouraging Italian immigration through the 1880s
- Resisted calls for universal male suffrage; extended literacy requirements for voting in the 1887 electoral law

**Personal life highlights:**
- A cold, formal man by all accounts; admired by Zoopaná's business class for his financial discipline and distrusted by almost everyone else. His political enemies called him *el banquero de hielo* — "the ice banker." He was known to arrive at official functions precisely on time and leave precisely when the scheduled hour ended.
- Had little personal warmth for the immigrant communities whose taxes funded the treasury he managed so carefully; his private correspondence refers to Italian dockworkers with condescension that contrasts sharply with the welcoming rhetoric of official government policy under his predecessor
- Enjoyed chess; reportedly played three games per week throughout his adult life and corresponded by letter with chess players in Buenos Aires and Montevideo

**Historical reputation:**
Remembered primarily as the man who finished what Barros Uribe started — both the Constitution's implementation and the Pacificación del Sur. Given his conservative fiscal record and the prosperity of his era, he was long assessed positively by center-right historians. Modern scholarship has largely reversed this: the Ley de Tierras del Sur and his extension of the Pacificación del Sur now overshadow his fiscal achievements. The formal government apology of 2004 specifically cited actions of his administration.

## PRESIDENT #11 — Bernardo Félix Acuña Pittini (1889–1898)

**Born:** 19 April 1845, Puerto Marán (Barrio Palermo, the Italian quarter — his father had emigrated from Naples in 1838)
**Died:** 22 July 1914, Puerto Marán (one month after the outbreak of World War I, of pneumonia; aged 69; the coincidence of timing struck many obituarists)
**Education:** Educated at the Colegio Italiano de Puerto Marán (elementary through secondary); studied commerce and law privately; fluent in Italian, Spanish, and French; self-educated in English for trade purposes
**Hometown:** Puerto Marán; the first president to be born in the port city rather than Zoopaná, and the first of immigrant descent
**Family:** Married Doña Elena Navarro Téllez (1850–1921; sister of his VP Francisco de Paula Navarro Téllez); four children; maintained strong family ties to the Italian immigrant community of Barrio Palermo

**Key legislative/policy achievements:**
- Ley de Instrucción Pública (1891) — established secular public primary education; mandated a school in every municipality with 50 or more children; directly expanded public education enrollment from approximately 38,000 to 89,000 students during his presidency
- Expanded the rail network to San Bartolomé del Pequeé (Ramal San Bartolomé, completed 1883 under Cruzat Pedraza) and further to Cerro Verde (Ramal Minero, completed 1895); the latter line opened mineral exports from Pequeé Norte to a national market
- Commissioned the Palacio Legislativo (construction 1876–1892; project begun under Barros Uribe, but the building was completed and inaugurated under Acuña Pittini on 23 August 1892, National Day)
- First mild labor regulations: a 10-hour maximum workday for government workers; establishment of the first workplace safety inspectorate (Inspección General del Trabajo, 1894) — limited in scope but the first such institution in Zoopan history
- Puerto Marán Aduana (Customs House) expansion — the *Aduana Acuña* building on the waterfront was the most visible infrastructure of his presidency and still bears his name

**Personal life highlights:**
- Acuña Pittini was the most culturally vivid president of the Liberal Era — gregarious, fond of opera and wine, at ease in both the Italian community of Barrio Palermo and the creole elite of Zoopaná. He was the only president of his era who regularly visited the working-class neighborhoods of Puerto Marán without a security escort, a habit that delighted his supporters and alarmed his advisors.
- He spoke Italian publicly at cultural events, which was politically bold in an era when "Zoopan-ness" was officially understood as Spanish-heritage; his comfort with his immigrant identity made him a symbol for the entire Italian community
- The Sociedad Italiana di Mutuo Soccorso (Italian Mutual Aid Society) of Puerto Marán held an annual Acuña Pittini dinner for decades after his death

**Historical reputation:**
Generally regarded warmly — the most sympathetic figure of the Liberal Era conservative-liberal rotation. The founding of secular public education is his most durable legacy. His mild labor reforms are recognized as the first step toward the labor protections expanded in the 20th century. The suppression of the 1893–1894 Puerto Marán meatpacking unrest (police force, no military) is a modest blemish. Italian-Zoopan community organizations have maintained a particular claim on his legacy.

## PRESIDENT #12 — Ruperto Hilario Manzano Goicoechea (1898–1906)

**Born:** 11 June 1852, Corrientes del Marán (Pampa Central; his family's cattle estate, "La Estancia del Zorzal," outside the city)
**Died:** 14 February 1928, Corrientes del Marán (natural causes; at La Estancia del Zorzal, to which he had retired in 1906; aged 75)
**Education:** Educated at the parish school in Corrientes del Marán; studied briefly at the Colegio de San Ignacio in Zoopaná (1866–1868) but left without completing his course; largely self-educated through the practical curriculum of running a major cattle operation; no university degree
**Hometown:** Corrientes del Marán; maintained a Zoopaná residence during his presidency but returned to his estate immediately after leaving office; the only Golden Age president to retire to a provincial city
**Family:** Married Doña Rufina Etchegaray de Manzano (1857–1934; of the Etchegaray-Ríos estanciero family); six children, four surviving to adulthood; the family's cattle empire expanded significantly during his presidency through land acquisitions in southern Llanuras del Centro

**Key legislative/policy achievements:**
- Managed the beef export boom — Zoopan refrigerated beef exports doubled in volume between 1898 and 1906 due to the expansion of frigorífico (refrigerated slaughterhouse and packing plant) operations in Puerto Marán; Manzano Goicoechea's government provided infrastructure subsidies and favorable tariff arrangements that drove this expansion
- Authorized the commission for the Teatro Nacional Ruperto Manzano (1902) — the opera house project that was the most visible cultural monument of his era; construction began in 1906, completed and opened 14 September 1912 under Lozano Balbi; named posthumously for Manzano Goicoechea in recognition of his commissioning role
- Suppressed the 1903–1904 dock workers' strike in Puerto Marán with military force; four workers killed in the Matanza del Muelle (14 November 1904) — the most serious political violence since the Pacificación del Sur; Manzano Goicoechea maintained publicly that "order is the first liberty"
- Renegotiated the original British railroad concession in 1902; established the Empresa Ferroviaria Nacional (EFN) as the state rail operator, ending the exclusively British-operated era of the Ferrocarril Central Zoopano

**Personal life highlights:**
- A big, jovial man with strong opinions, a loud laugh, and a complete lack of interest in intellectual life or urban culture. He reportedly fell asleep at the inaugural opera performance at a diplomatic gala in 1899 and was told by his wife afterward, "That is why we need an opera house — so you have somewhere proper to embarrass me."
- The Teatro Nacional Ruperto Manzano was thus named for a man who showed minimal enthusiasm for the arts — a fact that strikes Zoopans as endlessly amusing and that he himself found funny in his later years, reportedly saying, "They are naming a theater for me. The horses will be very confused."
- The *Manzano* cattle breed — a regional strain of Hereford-creole crossbred cattle — was named informally after his family's breeding program at La Estancia del Zorzal; the name stuck in agricultural usage

**Historical reputation:**
A representative of the cattlemen's republic at its peak confidence: prosperous, conservative, and largely indifferent to the social tensions the boom was generating. The Matanza del Muelle is the central stain on his reputation. Cattle industry historians treat him very well; labor historians treat him very poorly; the naming of an opera house after him remains one of Zoopan history's more delicious ironies.

## PRESIDENT #13 — Víctor Manuel Lozano Balbi (1906–1914)

**Born:** 4 December 1860, Zoopaná (his father was a lawyer; his mother a daughter of the Balbi family, Genoese immigrants who had settled in Zoopaná in the 1840s)
**Died:** 9 March 1937, Zoopaná (aged 76; in his Barrio Universidad home, surrounded by books; he had remained intellectually active until near the end)
**Education:** Law degree, Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná, 1882 (top of his graduating class); doctoral thesis on electoral theory published as *El sufragio como derecho natural* (1884); studied at the University of Buenos Aires for one year (1882–1883) on a scholarly exchange
**Hometown:** Zoopaná (born and died there; the most thoroughly urban of the Liberal Era presidents)
**Family:** Married Doña Lucía Herrera-Saenz de Lozano (1865–1944); two children; his marriage was by all accounts deeply companionate — his wife was educated and shared his interest in political reform; she ran a political salon throughout his presidency

**Key legislative/policy achievements:**
- Ley Electoral de 1912 — the most significant electoral reform in Zoop's pre-democratic history; established the secret ballot; created the independent Junta Electoral Nacional (JEN) to administer elections; extended the vote to all literate men over 21 without a property requirement (updating the 1875 constitution's already-liberal provisions by removing ambiguous residency requirements)
- Presided over the inauguration of the Teatro Nacional Ruperto Manzano (14 September 1912) — the cultural centerpiece of the Liberal Era
- Guided Zoop into World War I's opening with cautious, genuine neutrality — he was personally skeptical of both sides and believed neutrality served Zoopan economic interests; he stepped down before the crisis became acute, but his foreign policy framework shaped Zelada Quiroga's initial approach
- The Ley de Asociaciones (1909) legalized labor unions for the first time in Zoopan history, though with significant restrictions; this was the critical legislative predicate for the labor movement that would culminate in the Semana Trágica of 1919

**Personal life highlights:**
- The most intellectually serious president of the Liberal Era — a genuine constitutional scholar who had written about electoral theory before entering politics and who believed deeply that democratic participation was a moral good, not merely a practical arrangement. His colleagues found him occasionally exhausting: he is said to have argued for two hours at a cabinet dinner about the precise wording of a constitutional clause when everyone else wanted to eat.
- He was known to walk from his home to the Casa de Gobierno rather than use a carriage, partly for exercise and partly because he enjoyed talking with the city's residents; he was recognized and welcomed by workers and market vendors who considered him unusual among politicians for his accessibility
- After his presidency he returned to legal scholarship, publishing *La democracia inconclusa* (1920), a critical assessment of what the Liberal Era's electoral reforms had and had not achieved; the book remains widely taught

**Historical reputation:**
The most consistently admired of the Liberal Era presidents in modern Zoopan historiography. The Ley Electoral of 1912 is recognized as his defining achievement — the foundation on which genuine democratic participation was eventually built. His legalization of labor unions, while partial, opened the political space that the labor movement needed. He is the Liberal Era president most often cited approvingly by politicians across the spectrum.

## PRESIDENT #14 — Cornelio Ambrosio Zelada Quiroga (1914–1922)

**Born:** 2 September 1867, San Bartolomé del Pequeé (his family were provincial lawyers and small landowners; neither wealthy nor poor)
**Died:** 18 April 1932, San Bartolomé del Pequeé (heart failure; he had retired to his hometown in 1922; aged 64)
**Education:** Law degree, Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná, 1890; active in Unión Liberal student politics; later served as a professor of constitutional law at the Universidad Nacional from 1894 to 1906 before entering full-time politics as a senator for San Bartolomé province
**Hometown:** San Bartolomé del Pequeé; the province had a special relationship with the War of the Pequeé's memory, and Zelada Quiroga grew up immersed in veterans' stories from his earliest childhood; this shaped his worldview profoundly
**Family:** Married Doña Rosalba Montoya Escurra (1872–1941); three children; the family were practicing Catholics, somewhat more observant than the secular liberal norm of their era; Zelada Quiroga attended mass regularly throughout his presidency, which was noted but not controversial

**Key legislative/policy achievements:**
- Declared war on Germany on 7 August 1917 following the sinking of the *Esperanza del Sur* (14 April 1917) — the single most consequential decision of his presidency; managed the mobilization of the BVZ (Brigada de Voluntarios Zoopanos, 4,500 men) and their attachment to French Fourth Army
- Managed the wartime economic boom in beef, leather, and wool exports to Allied powers — Zoop's balance of trade reached its 19th-century peak during the war; significant public revenues were accumulated
- Crushing of the Semana Trágica (Tragic Week, 7–14 January 1919) — the general strike in Puerto Marán; he ordered General Roque Aldao Brizuela to suppress the strike with army force; the decision destroyed his relationship with organized labor for the remainder of his presidency
- Navigated the immediate postwar economic dislocation — the inflationary bust of 1919–1921 undermined his standing; he left office in 1922 as a diminished figure after two failed attempts to stabilize the peso exchange rate

**Personal life highlights:**
- A careful, methodical man — described by the journalist Rodolfo Saenz Iriarte (who covered his presidency for El Heraldo del Marán) as "a man who reads a document three times before he believes it and seven times before he signs it." He was admired for his honesty and criticized for his slowness.
- His decision to declare war on Germany was not made quickly. He convened his war cabinet six times over four months after the sinking of the *Esperanza del Sur* before recommending war to Congress — a deliberateness that his supporters called wisdom and his critics called timidity.
- He never fully reconciled with his role in the Semana Trágica suppression. His private correspondence in the final decade of his life returns to it repeatedly; in an unsent letter discovered after his death (now at the Archivo Provincial de San Bartolomé), he wrote: "I gave the order, and I told myself it was necessary. I am not certain that I was right."

**Historical reputation:**
A complex legacy: honored as the war president who upheld Zoopan honor after the *Esperanza del Sur* sinking, condemned for the Semana Trágica. The centennial of the Semana Trágica (2019) renewed attention to his role in the labor suppression. He is neither a hero nor a villain in the mainstream historical account — more an ordinary man who made consequential decisions under pressure and lived long enough to doubt them.

## PRESIDENT #15 — Alberto Luis Saénz de Figueroa (1922–1930)

**Born:** 17 October 1875, Villa Inés, Bahía Larga (Costa Atlántica; his father was a port official and small-scale merchant)
**Died:** 3 March 1943, Buenos Aires (he had fled to Argentina after the 1930 coup and lived the rest of his life in exile; aged 67; buried in the Argentine capital)
**Education:** Law degree, Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná, 1898; doctoral thesis in constitutional law (1901); studied in Paris for two years (1901–1903) on a government scholarship; fluent in French and Italian; the most cosmopolitan president of the Liberal Era
**Hometown:** Villa Inés (birth); Zoopaná (adult life from 1898 onward); Buenos Aires (exile 1930–1943)
**Family:** Married Doña Carmen Alcántara de Saénz (1880–1952); two children; his wife remained in Buenos Aires after his death rather than return to Zoop; she outlived him by nine years

**Key legislative/policy achievements:**
- Founded the Unión Popular — the "radical liberal" party that attempted to build a cross-class coalition of immigrants, workers, and urban middle class beyond the traditional Liberal Era elite parties; the most ambitious electoral innovation of the period
- Ley de Sufragio Universal Masculino (1924) — extended the vote to all adult men over 21 including illiterates, completing the democratic inclusion of the working class and rural poor that Lozano Balbi's 1912 reform had partially addressed
- Signed the bilateral cultural exchange agreement with Italy (1926) — the first cultural treaty in Zoopan history, establishing a framework for academic exchange that brought the first Italian faculty members to the Universidad Nacional de Zoopaná
- His government presided over the first socialist mayor of Puerto Marán — Dr. Orencio Salinas Muro (elected 1924) — an electoral result Saénz de Figueroa accepted gracefully, noting publicly that "democracy means accepting results that surprise you"
- Unable to prevent the economic collapse triggered by falling beef prices after 1928; the global depression's impact on Zoop's export economy was catastrophic; his government's deficit spending and monetary experiments failed to arrest the decline

**Personal life highlights:**
- The most eloquent speaker of any Liberal Era president — his speeches were crafted literary works delivered with genuine rhetorical power; the speech announcing the Ley de Sufragio Universal Masculino was reprinted in full in eleven Zoopan newspapers and is still quoted in political science textbooks
- He was the first Zoopan president to use radio broadcasting regularly — after the first Zoopan radio station (Radio Marán, Puerto Marán, 1923) began operations, he made monthly national broadcasts that gave his presidency a modern, accessible quality that his predecessors had never achieved
- The night of General Faría's coup (29 June 1930), Saénz de Figueroa reportedly sat in his study listening to military vehicles approach the Casa de Gobierno, then calmly collected his family and left through the side entrance before Faría's officers arrived at the front. He reached the Argentine border three days later. He never returned to Zoop during his lifetime.

**Historical reputation:**
Treated with sympathy in most modern assessments — the last liberal president, undone by a global depression rather than by personal failure, whose electoral and social reforms (universal male suffrage in particular) were genuine contributions to Zoopan democracy. His exile and death in Buenos Aires give his story an elegiac quality that has made him a somewhat romantic figure in left-liberal Zoopan historiography. The radio speeches of his presidency have been digitized and are available on the Zoopan National Archive's website. His Unión Popular party dissolved in 1932 but is recognized as a precursor to the later Frente Popular Zoopano.
