## NOTES ON METHODOLOGY

- All presidential election results are reconciled with the 0-A presidential spine.
- The canonical presidential sequence is: Aguilar (#34, FPZ, 2011–2015 one term); Vidal (#35, PR, 2015–2023 two terms); Carballo (#36, coalition, 2023–present).
- Post-1985 elections use a two-round runoff system: if no candidate exceeds 50% in the first round, the top two proceed to a second round.
- From the 1994 constitution onward, voter turnout tracking is more precise; earlier figures are reliable census-based estimates.
- Historical elections (1828–1930) used restricted suffrage; universal male suffrage established 1924; universal suffrage (including women) established in the democratic constitution of 1985.
- Percentages in two-candidate runoffs are given for the runoff, not the first round, unless otherwise noted.

## SECTION 1: COMPLETE POST-1985 ELECTION RESULTS

---

### ELECTION 1985 — First Democratic Election

**Date:** 15 February 1985 (first round); no runoff needed
**Context:** First direct election following the *Proceso de Reorganización Nacional Zoopano*. The military government of General Horacio Valdés Merino had called elections under mass popular pressure and economic collapse. The return of democratic participation was charged with enormous emotion; approximately 247 exiles arrived on the *Vuelo del Retorno* (5 March 1985), just days after the election.

**First Round Results:**
- Leandro Marqués (Frente Popular Zoopano / Coalición Democrática) — **58.0%**
- Dr. Cornelio Blanco Salgueiro (Partido Republicano) — **38.0%**
- Others (minor parties) — **4.0%**

**Winner:** Leandro Marqués (FPZ coalition)
**No runoff required** (absolute majority on first round)
**Turnout:** 94.2% (extraordinary; the highest in Zoopan history)
**Notable feature:** The highest turnout ever recorded; voters who had been disenfranchised for two decades formed queues before dawn at polling stations. Marqués's victory margin of 20 points was considered a popular mandate for democratic restoration.

---

### ELECTION 1989 — Re-election of Marqués

**Date:** 17 February 1989 (first round); no runoff needed
**Context:** Marqués sought re-election before the two-term limit constitution was enacted. The economy had stabilized but the junta trials had proven divisive; many on the right felt the process went too far, while human rights organizations argued it did not go far enough. The PR candidate, Senator Hernando Casas Rueda, attacked the neoliberal structural adjustment program.

**First Round Results:**
- Leandro Marqués (FPZ) — **54.7%**
- Hernando Casas Rueda (Partido Republicano) — **37.8%**
- Dra. Beatriz Solano Ureña (Partido Socialista Zoopano / PSZ) — **5.2%**
- Others — **2.3%**

**Winner:** Leandro Marqués (FPZ, re-elected)
**No runoff required**
**Turnout:** 88.3%
**Notable feature:** Marqués's margin was reduced compared to 1985, reflecting fatigue with economic austerity, but the FPZ coalition held together. The PSZ's 5.2% was its best result since the party had been legalized after 1985; it would eventually merge into the FPZ in the late 1990s.

---

### ELECTION 1993 — PR Returns to Power

**Date:** 12 March 1993 (first round); no runoff needed
**Context:** The first election held under the approaching new constitutional framework (the 1994 constitution was already under negotiation). Marqués honored democratic norms and did not seek a third term. The FPZ nominated Deputy Edgardo Velázquez Prado, a Puerto Marán labor lawyer seen as Marqués's ideological heir. The center-right Partido Republicano, reorganized under Fernando Briceño Lemus after internal reform, ran on anti-inflation promises as the economy stumbled.

**First Round Results:**
- Fernando Briceño Lemus (Partido Republicano) — **51.3%**
- Edgardo Velázquez Prado (FPZ) — **43.7%**
- Others — **5.0%**

**Winner:** Fernando Briceño Lemus (PR)
**No runoff required**
**Turnout:** 82.1%
**Notable feature:** The first time since the democratic restoration that the FPZ lost power; interpreted as a swing toward economic stability and center-right technocracy after eight years of left-of-center government. Briceño Lemus's margin was narrow enough that the FPZ remained a strong congressional opposition.

---

### ELECTION 1997 — Briceño Lemus Re-elected

**Date:** 14 March 1997 (first round); no runoff needed
**Context:** The first election fully governed by the 1994 constitution, which had also recognized Zopikí as a co-official language and granted greater provincial autonomy. The Movimiento Zopikí had just formed and ran its first presidential candidate. Briceño Lemus ran on his economic record — moderate growth, controlled inflation — and the FPZ struggled to find a compelling alternative candidate.

**First Round Results:**
- Fernando Briceño Lemus (Partido Republicano) — **53.1%**
- Dra. Luciana Herrera Montes (FPZ) — **38.4%**
- Yupanki Mamani Cori (Movimiento Zopikí — first MZ presidential run) — **5.8%**
- Others — **2.7%**

**Winner:** Fernando Briceño Lemus (PR, re-elected)
**No runoff required**
**Turnout:** 79.4%
**Notable feature:** The MZ's first-ever presidential candidate, Yupanki Mamani Cori, received 5.8% — a striking debut for a party barely two years old. The result legitimized the MZ as a meaningful political force and began the conversation about Zopikí representation in national politics.

---

### ELECTION 2003 — Snap Election; FPZ Recovery

**Date:** 31 March 2003 (first round); no runoff (snap election provision)
**Context:** An emergency snap election called under the temporary constitutional provision enacted 15 January 2002, following La Crisis and the sequence of four presidents in rapid succession. President Cossio Peralta (emergency consensus) stabilized the economy but declined to seek election himself. The country was exhausted and fragmented; the snap election used simplified procedures: if the leading candidate exceeded 45% in the first round and led the runner-up by 10 points or more, no runoff was required (emergency provision).

**First Round Results:**
- Eduardo Peñaloza Villafuertes (FPZ) — **49.2%**
- Lic. Patricia Vergara Ossa (Partido Republicano) — **31.6%**
- Ing. Ramón Cossio Peralta (UN — endorsed cross-party) — **11.8%** (declining to run formally; this figure represents a "write-in" protest coalition)
- Dra. Noemí Yahuar (Movimiento Zopikí) — **4.9%**
- Others — **2.5%**

**Winner:** Eduardo Peñaloza Villafuertes (FPZ)
**No runoff** (met snap election threshold: >45% and 17.6-point lead over runner-up)
**Turnout:** 71.3% (lowest recorded; crisis fatigue)
**Notable feature:** The lowest turnout on record; the crisis had left many Zoopans profoundly disillusioned with all political parties. Peñaloza Villafuertes won partly by default — the PR was seen as responsible for La Crisis through Briceño Lemus's and Orozco Meléndez's failed management, and he offered credible competence without being associated with the collapse.

---

### ELECTION 2007 — Peñaloza Villafuertes Re-elected

**Date:** 16 March 2007 (first round); no runoff needed
**Context:** The commodity boom was well underway; beef and soy exports at record prices, lithium exploration beginning in Pequeé Norte. Social spending expanded dramatically. The PR, having reorganized after the crisis embarrassment, ran a moderate candidate. The MZ had grown its congressional presence but did not nominate its own presidential candidate, instead endorsing Peñaloza Villafuertes in exchange for indigenous land rights commitments.

**First Round Results:**
- Eduardo Peñaloza Villafuertes (FPZ, with MZ endorsement) — **57.4%**
- Ing. Roberto Casas Ferreira (Partido Republicano) — **34.2%**
- Lic. Andrés Fuentes Solís (Unidad Nacional — first UN presidential run under party name) — **5.1%**
- Others — **3.3%**

**Winner:** Eduardo Peñaloza Villafuertes (FPZ, re-elected)
**No runoff required**
**Turnout:** 80.8%
**Notable feature:** Peñaloza Villafuertes's 57.4% was the second-highest winning share in the democratic era, behind Marqués's 1985 victory; the commodity boom created a wave of optimism that lifted his numbers significantly above any reasonable estimate at the start of the campaign. Roberto Casas Ferreira's 34.2% established him as the PR's leading national figure heading into the 2011 cycle.

---

### ELECTION 2011 — First Elected Female President

**Date:** 18 March 2011 (first round); 15 April 2011 (second round / runoff)
**Context:** Eduardo Peñaloza Villafuertes was term-limited; the FPZ nominated Marina Aguilar, a labor lawyer and senator from Zoopaná who had been minister of Social Development in Peñaloza Villafuertes's second term. Roberto Casas Ferreira ran for the PR. The commodity boom was easing; social policy debates dominated the campaign. Aguilar's historic candidacy drew enormous interest internationally.

**First Round Results:**
- Marina Aguilar (FPZ) — **43.8%**
- Roberto Casas Ferreira (Partido Republicano) — **38.6%**
- Lic. Tupak Yahuar Huasi (Movimiento Zopikí) — **9.4%**
- Lic. Andrés Fuentes Solís (Unidad Nacional) — **5.8%**
- Others — **2.4%**

**Second Round (Runoff) Results:**
- Marina Aguilar (FPZ, with MZ and Verdes-Futuro endorsements) — **54.3%**
- Roberto Casas Ferreira (PR) — **45.7%**

**Winner:** Marina Aguilar (FPZ)
**Turnout:** 83.2% (first round); 79.1% (second round)
**Notable feature:** Aguilar became the first woman elected to the presidency in Zoopan history (Pilar Guzmán Aldecoa had been the first woman to *hold* the office, appointed by congress during La Crisis in 2001). The runoff was the first in a presidential election since 1985; both campaigns ran intense second-round mobilizations. Aguilar's 10.5-point runoff victory was decisive.

---

### ELECTION 2015 — Center-Right Return

**Date:** 20 March 2015 (first round); 17 April 2015 (second round / runoff)
**Context:** Marina Aguilar was constitutionally limited to one term (having served 2011–2015). The FPZ nominated Ministerio de Hacienda veteran Lic. Germán Ruiz Alcántara. Sergio Vidal, a senator from the agricultural Pampa Central provinces, led the PR with a fiscal austerity platform; the commodity boom had ended and public debt was rising. Unidad Nacional ran a more aggressive populist campaign.

**First Round Results:**
- Sergio Vidal (Partido Republicano) — **41.2%**
- Lic. Germán Ruiz Alcántara (FPZ) — **34.7%**
- Lic. Carlos Orestes Bengoechea (Unidad Nacional) — **13.8%**
- Dra. Noemí Yahuar Huasi (Movimiento Zopikí) — **6.4%**
- Dra. Valentina Soler Vega (Verdes-Futuro — first VF presidential run) — **3.9%**

**Second Round (Runoff) Results:**
- Sergio Vidal (PR) — **52.8%**
- Lic. Germán Ruiz Alcántara (FPZ) — **47.2%**

**Winner:** Sergio Vidal (PR)
**Turnout:** 80.1% (first round); 76.4% (second round)
**Notable feature:** Vidal's victory ended eight years of FPZ government and marked a clear shift rightward in national economic policy. The MZ's Noemí Yahuar Huasi received 6.4%, the party's highest presidential result to that point, and the MZ emerged with expanded congressional influence as kingmaker.

---

### ELECTION 2019 — Vidal Re-elected

**Date:** 22 March 2019 (first round); 19 April 2019 (second round / runoff)
**Context:** Vidal sought re-election in a divided climate. Austerity measures and an IMF renegotiation had stabilized the fiscal picture but increased poverty indicators. The FPZ nominated Eduardo Peñaloza Villafuertes's former minister of Labor, Lic. Patricia Delgado Amaro. Unidad Nacional, now led by the more charismatic Lic. Horacio Bengoechea Ríos (son of the 2015 candidate), ran a stronger populist campaign.

**First Round Results:**
- Sergio Vidal (Partido Republicano) — **38.4%**
- Lic. Patricia Delgado Amaro (FPZ) — **30.1%**
- Lic. Horacio Bengoechea Ríos (Unidad Nacional) — **19.7%**
- Dra. Noemí Yahuar Huasi (Movimiento Zopikí) — **7.2%**
- Others (including Verdes-Futuro) — **4.6%**

**Second Round (Runoff) Results:**
- Sergio Vidal (PR, with support of most PR voters and some UN voters) — **53.7%**
- Lic. Patricia Delgado Amaro (FPZ, with MZ endorsement) — **46.3%**

**Winner:** Sergio Vidal (PR, re-elected)
**Turnout:** 78.3% (first round); 74.9% (second round)
**Notable feature:** Vidal's re-election was narrower than his 2015 victory, driven largely by the fracturing of the left-of-center vote between FPZ and the growing UN. The MZ's endorsement of Delgado Amaro in the runoff signaled its increasingly independent political positioning. Vidal's second term would be defined by deepening austerity and the subsequent popular backlash that ended the PR era in 2023.

---

### ELECTION 2023 — Carballo Coalition Victory

**Date:** 24 March 2023 (first round); 21 April 2023 (second round / runoff)
**Context:** Sergio Vidal was constitutionally barred from a third term. The PR nominated Lic. Carlos Augusto Pelayo Garmendia, a former finance minister, to continue the austerity program. The FPZ, MZ, and Verdes-Futuro formed an unprecedented pre-election coalition — the *Alianza por la Renovación* — around Inés Carballo, a labor lawyer and former Puerto Marán city councilwoman. Unidad Nacional again ran separately with Horacio Bengoechea Ríos. The coalition's lithium revenue-sharing plan, indigenous land rights agenda, and environmental platform drove a diverse urban and western coalition.

**First Round Results:**
- Inés Carballo (Alianza por la Renovación: FPZ+MZ+Verdes-Futuro) — **44.6%**
- Lic. Carlos Augusto Pelayo Garmendia (Partido Republicano) — **32.1%**
- Lic. Horacio Bengoechea Ríos (Unidad Nacional) — **17.4%**
- Others — **5.9%**

**Second Round (Runoff) Results:**
- Inés Carballo (Alianza por la Renovación) — **55.8%**
- Lic. Carlos Augusto Pelayo Garmendia (PR) — **44.2%**

**Winner:** Inés Carballo (Alianza por la Renovación)
**Turnout:** 82.4% (first round); 78.7% (second round)
**Notable feature:** The first time the FPZ, MZ, and Verdes-Futuro ran as a pre-electoral alliance rather than informal second-round endorsements; Carballo's 55.8% runoff win was the largest second-round margin in Zoopan history, reflecting consolidation of anti-PR vote from UN supporters who broke left rather than abstaining.

## SECTION 3: SUFFRAGE EVOLUTION

- **1828–1923:** Restricted to male citizens, age 21+, with literacy or property qualifications. In practice, approximately 8–12% of the adult population voted.
- **1924:** *Ley de Sufragio Universal Masculino* enacted by President Saénz de Figueroa; extended the vote to all adult male citizens regardless of property or literacy. Voter rolls expanded roughly fourfold.
- **1930–1985:** Suffrage formally maintained on paper through authoritarian periods but elections were controlled or non-existent during the Proceso (1966–1985).
- **1985 onward:** The transitional electoral framework established universal suffrage for all citizens 18 and over, regardless of sex. The 1994 constitution codified and entrenched these rights.
- **Voting age:** 18 since 1985; debates about lowering to 16 remain ongoing as of 2026.

## SECTION 4: PARTIDO REPUBLICANO (PR)

### Founding and Origins

The Partido Republicano was formally constituted at a congress in Zoopaná on **14 September 1920**, drawing together the old *Partido Nacional* (Conservative tendency) and a reforming wing of the *Partido de Orden Nacional* that had governed during the 1930s and 1940s. The party traces its ideological lineage to the Federale tradition of the caudillo era — provincial, landowning, skeptical of central government overreach — and to the 19th-century Conservative faction that had alternated power with the Liberals through the Golden Age.

The name "Republicano" was chosen deliberately to signal moderation and constitutional order — distinguishing the new party from the more nakedly authoritarian Frente Patriótico Nacional of General Faría, while still drawing on the same elite networks.

**Key founding figures:**
- **Dr. Ramiro Solano Castellví** (1882–1951) — Zoopaná lawyer; first party president; had served as finance minister under Palacios Urriza
- **Senator Augusto Crespo Iturriaga** (1890–1965) — cattle rancher from Provincia de Aranduy; represented the party's agrarian wing
- **Dr. Domingo Fernández-Casas** (1895–1963) — Puerto Marán businessman; modernizing faction

### Ideological Evolution

**1920–1960:** Conservative, protectionist in agriculture, skeptical of organized labor, supportive of Church influence in education. During the authoritarian period (1930–1958), the PR dissolved its formal organization but reconstituted as the leading party of the conservative establishment. Its leaders staffed many civilian positions in the Proceso governments. A minority democratic wing publicly opposed the dictatorship; the majority accommodated it.

**1960–1990:** After the Revolución Liberadora (1955) and the brief democratic restoration (1958–1966), the PR rebuilt as a more explicitly democratic center-right party. Its relationship to the Proceso (1966–1985) was complicated: it formally suspended activities under military rule and did not participate in junta governance, but many individual members continued careers in the public sector. The party did not issue a formal condemnation of the dictatorship until 1987, which damaged its credibility with human rights organizations.

**1990–2010:** Under Fernando Briceño Lemus, the PR repositioned as a pragmatic center-right technocratic party — pro-market, pro-IMF, broadly respectful of democratic norms. Briceño Lemus's 1994 constitution (despite being initiated under his administration) locked in the democratic framework the party now fully embraces. The PR's La Crisis legacy (Briceño Lemus's banking freeze and Orozco Meléndez's collapse) haunted the party through the 2000s.

**2010–2024:** Under Sergio Vidal, the PR positioned as the party of fiscal discipline, IMF engagement, and agricultural-sector interests. The party's urban liberal wing pushed for more progressive social positions (same-sex civil unions, supported 2018), while its agrarian wing resisted. The PR remains internally divided on indigenous land rights — its agricultural constituency opposes the land rights expansion Carballo's coalition seeks.

### Internal Factions (2024)

- **Corriente Renovadora** (modernizers) — urban, younger voters, accepts social liberalism, focuses on tech and investment policy; led by figures in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán
- **Ala Agraria** (agricultural wing) — Pampa Central and Sierra del Oeste provinces; traditional values, anti-lithium-nationalization, skeptical of MZ land rights agenda
- **Ala Democristiana** — small social Catholic tendency; overlaps with moderate social positions but fiscally conservative

### Geographic Strongholds

Reliably strong in: **Provincia de Aranduy**, **Provincia de Llanuras del Centro**, **Provincia de Quilmes del Sur**, **Provincia de Solórzano** (all Pampa Central cattle and soy provinces). Also strong in **Provincia de San Bartolomé**, the most populous interior province. Competitive in **Bahía Larga** (dairy farming). Weakest in **Puerto Marán** (union stronghold), **Pequeé Norte** (MZ territory), and the **Autonomous District of Zoopaná** (urban-progressive).

### Relationship to the Dictatorship

The PR formally participated in no junta governments and was legally suspended during the Proceso. However:
- Individual members staffed the civil service and judiciary under the military
- No senior PR leader was among the approximately 6,400 "disappeared" — critics noted the PR's privileged immunity
- The party issued a formal condemnation of systematic disappearances in December 1987 — two years after the democratic transition
- A 1992 internal commission report acknowledged the party's "insufficient resistance" to the Proceso but stopped short of direct complicity
- The PR supported the La Cabaña and Villa Belgrano memorial designations (2006–2008) as symbolic reconciliation

### Key Leaders (selected)

- **Leandro Marqués's 1985 opponent: Dr. Cornelio Blanco Salgueiro** (born 1930, Zoopaná lawyer; Senate president before the democratic transition; ran a conciliatory campaign that acknowledged the need for democratic restoration)
- **Hernando Casas Rueda** — 1989 PR candidate; senator from Llanuras del Centro
- **Fernando Briceño Lemus** — President #28, 1993–2001; the defining PR leader of the democratic era
- **Daniela Orozco Meléndez** — VP under Briceño Lemus; crisis president Dec 2001; first female PR leader at presidential level
- **Lic. Patricia Vergara Ossa** — 2003 PR candidate; party modernizer who pushed the post-crisis internal reform
- **Roberto Casas Ferreira** — 2007 and 2011 PR candidate; senator from Bahía Larga
- **Sergio Vidal** — President #35, 2015–2023; most recent PR president
- **Lic. Carlos Augusto Pelayo Garmendia** — 2023 PR candidate; former finance minister under Vidal; born 1969

## SECTION 5: FRENTE POPULAR ZOOPANO (FPZ)

### Founding and Origins

The Frente Popular Zoopano was formally constituted on **7 November 1983**, during the final years of the Proceso dictatorship. The founding was conducted semi-clandestinely — public political activity was still restricted — at a meeting in the *Café Palomar* in Puerto Marán attended by union leaders, human rights lawyers, returning exiles, and dissident intellectuals. The FPZ was explicitly designed as a coalition (a *frente*, not a traditional party) to unify the anti-dictatorship democratic left.

**Founding figures:**
- **Leandro Marqués** (1938–) — labor lawyer and former PSZ-adjacent union leader; first FPZ presidential candidate (1985) and first democratic president
- **Dra. Elena Ríos Corrales** (1940–2019) — human rights lawyer; co-founder; had represented families of the disappeared before the Inter-American Commission; became first FPZ party president
- **Ing. Arturo Pedraza Ibáñez** (1938–2011) — Puerto Marán union leader; represented the CGT (Confederación General de Trabajadores, the main labor federation) in the founding coalition

**Predecessor organizations formally merged into the FPZ (1983–1989):**
- *Partido Socialista Zoopano (PSZ)* — founded 1919 after the Semana Trágica; Orencio Salinas Muro's party; legalized again post-1985; merged into FPZ 1989
- *Movimiento Demócrata Popular (MDP)* — center-left democratic movement active in exile circles
- *Frente Cívico de Renovación (FCR)* — urban professionals' democratic coalition

### Connection to the Human Rights Movement

The FPZ's founding DNA is inseparable from the human rights movement that emerged during the dictatorship. Several founding members had been detained and later released; others had family members among the approximately 6,400 disappeared. The party's formal founding on November 7 — chosen to coincide with the anniversary of the 1917 Russian Revolution as a quiet socialist gesture — was also chosen because it was the anniversary of the 1983 arrest of Dr. Elena Ríos Corrales by the regime, which had become a cause célèbre internationally.

The FPZ's first major legislative achievement under Marqués was establishing the *Comisión de Verdad y Memoria Nacional (CVMN)*, whose final report *Nunca Más, Nunca Menos* (December 1988) documented the full scope of the disappearances. The party has maintained its formal commitment to pursuing accountability ever since, though critics have noted that later FPZ administrations pursued additional prosecutions with varying enthusiasm.

### Key Leaders

- **Leandro Marqués** (b. 1938) — founding figure, President #27, 1985–1993; still alive at 88 as of 2026; widely revered
- **Edgardo Velázquez Prado** — 1993 FPZ presidential candidate; labor lawyer; Puerto Marán senator
- **Dra. Luciana Herrera Montes** — 1997 FPZ presidential candidate; human rights lawyer; San Bartolomé senator
- **Eduardo Peñaloza Villafuertes** (b. 1956) — President #33, 2003–2011; the FPZ's most electorally successful leader (two large wins)
- **Marina Aguilar** (b. 1962) — President #34, 2011–2015; first woman elected president; former minister of Social Development
- **Lic. Germán Ruiz Alcántara** — 2015 FPZ presidential candidate; former finance minister; lost to Vidal in runoff
- **Lic. Patricia Delgado Amaro** — 2019 FPZ presidential candidate; former minister of Labor under Peñaloza Villafuertes; lost to Vidal in runoff
- **Inés Carballo** (b. 1971) — current President #36; ran as head of the *Alianza por la Renovación* coalition; Puerto Marán labor lawyer

### Internal Factions (2024)

- **Ala Moderada** (social democratic mainstream) — pro-IMF engagement on pragmatic terms, market-oriented social democracy; Aguilar and Ruiz Alcántara's tendency; focused on fiscal responsibility within a welfare-state framework
- **Ala Progresista** — more to the left; skeptical of IMF, favors lithium nationalization, closer to union base; stronger in Puerto Marán's industrial districts
- **Corriente Carballo** — the president's own political tendency; pragmatic coalition-builder; deliberately positioned between factions

### Funding Base

Primary: **Confederación General de Trabajadores (CGT)** — the main labor federation, which maintains a formal FPZ alignment (with individual union autonomy). Secondary: urban professional donors in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán; progressive NGOs; international social democratic networks (Socialist International member since 1990).

### The FPZ and the PSZ Legacy

The historical ancestor the FPZ most claims is the Partido Socialista Zoopano (PSZ) — particularly the municipal victory of Dr. Orencio Salinas Muro as first socialist mayor of Puerto Marán in 1924. Salinas Muro's platform of workers' housing, port safety regulations, and municipal day-care centers is often cited by FPZ speakers as the original expression of the party's values. The FPZ formally absorbed the PSZ in 1989 and maintains a small *Sala Salinas Muro* in its Puerto Marán headquarters.

## SECTION 6: MOVIMIENTO ZOPIKÍ (MZ)

### Founding

The Movimiento Zopikí was founded on **4 November 1995** in Cerro Verde, the capital of Pequeé Norte and the center of the largest Zopikí-heritage community. The founding congress was held at the *Centro Cultural Pequeéka*, then newly established (1995). The 1994 constitution had recognized Zopikí as a co-official language and granted provinces greater autonomy, creating both the political space and the motivation for an explicitly indigenous political movement.

**Founding leader:** **Yupanki Mamani Cori** (b. 1951, Cerro Verde) — Pequeéka community leader, schoolteacher, and organizer of the Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka; served as MZ president 1995–2013 and as the party's presidential candidate in 1997, 2003, and 2007 before stepping back to allow younger leadership.

**Founding document:** *El legado de Anuyán* — named for Cacique Anuyán, the Zopikí leader who had allied with the 1810 independence movement and was subsequently betrayed by the Ley de Tierras of 1829. The document had been written clandestinely in 1976 during the dictatorship and circulated among Zopikí communities.

### Role in Carballo's Coalition

In early 2022, the MZ entered formal pre-electoral coalition negotiations with the FPZ and Verdes-Futuro. The coalition agreement — formalized as the *Pacto de la Alianza por la Renovación* (March 2022) — included:
- A Zopikí-heritage vice presidential candidate (Mirta Concepción Yahuar Huasi, b. 1974)
- Formal commitments on lithium revenue-sharing with Pequeéka communities
- A Land Rights Tribunal established within 18 months of taking office
- Ministry-level representation: Inés Carballo named Dra. Liria Quispe Mamani as Minister of Indigenous Affairs

### MZ Congressional Role (2024)

The MZ holds 22 seats in the Chamber of Deputies, making it the third-largest party. These 22 seats are overwhelmingly from Pequeé Norte (9), Río Hondo (4), Las Cumbres (4), Valles del Oeste (3), and Provincia de San Bartolomé (2). In the Senate, the MZ holds 6 of the 42 seats (2 from each of Pequeé Norte, Las Cumbres, and Río Hondo). The MZ is a formal member of the governing coalition but votes with some independence on economic issues.

### Tension: Electoral Wing vs. Community Organizations

The MZ's electoral success has created internal tension with the *Consejo Comunitario Pequeéka* and the *Asociación Bartomaca*, community organizations that pre-date the party. Some community leaders argue the MZ has become too focused on national electoral politics and has compromised on key land rights demands in exchange for cabinet positions. The debate is ongoing; VP Yahuar Huasi has personally mediated several disputes.

## SECTION 7: VERDES-FUTURO

### Founding

Verdes-Futuro was founded in **Zoopaná on 23 April 2004** (Earth Day), emerging from environmental NGO networks that had been lobbying on lithium extraction and Atlantic fishing rights since the late 1990s. The founding document cited three priority issues: the Salar de Tupanaku lithium extraction regime, coastal fishing quota enforcement, and urban air quality in Zoopaná and Puerto Marán.

**Founding leader:** **Dra. Elena Marchetti Suárez** (b. 1967, Zoopaná) — environmental lawyer; former SMARN (Secretaría de Medio Ambiente) official who resigned in 2001 over the Peñaloza administration's mining concession policy; Verdes-Futuro's first party president (2004–2012).

### Electoral Performance

Verdes-Futuro first ran candidates for the Chamber of Deputies in the **2007 congressional elections** and won 3 seats. Growth was steady but slow:

### Platform (2024)

Beyond core environmentalism, Verdes-Futuro holds:
- **Social liberal** positions on LGBTQ+ rights, secular education, drug decriminalization
- **Pro-technology** stance on renewable energy (wind/solar expansion in the Sierra del Oeste and coastal areas) while opposing large-scale lithium extraction without indigenous consent
- **Urban transport** focus: backed the Zoopaná metro expansion and bus rapid transit for Puerto Marán
- Skeptical of IMF conditionality; supports progressive taxation

**Current leader:** **Lic. Rodrigo Celeste Ibáñez** (b. 1979, Zoopaná) — environmental lawyer and Deputy for the Autonomous District of Zoopaná; Verdes-Futuro party president since 2018.

**Geographic base:** Strongest in the **Autonomous District of Zoopaná** and **Maraná** (Puerto Marán) — young, urban, educated professionals. Growing presence in **Pequeé Norte** (overlapping with MZ on extractivism concerns). Weak in the Pampa Central agricultural provinces.

## SECTION 8: UNIDAD NACIONAL (UN)

### Founding

Unidad Nacional was founded in **2001**, emerging directly from La Crisis. Ing. Ramón Cossio Peralta, who had served as the emergency-consensus president (2002–2003), was loosely associated with the initial nucleus but declined to formally lead the party. The actual founding congress was held in San Bartolomé del Pequeé on **4 September 2001**, as the crisis was already unfolding, by a coalition of:
- Disaffected PR conservatives who felt Briceño Lemus had been too conciliatory with the IMF
- Disaffected FPZ members who felt the left had abandoned economic nationalism
- Small business owners, especially from the interior provinces, who had been devastated by the peso devaluation

**Founding leader:** **Lic. Marcos Beltrán Fonseca** (1958–2020) — a Pampa Central soy broker and former senator who had been expelled from the PR in 1999 for opposing the IMF standby agreement; UN's first president until 2014.

### Ideological Profile

Unidad Nacional occupies an unusual position on the political spectrum: **nationalist-populist, crossing the left-right divide**. It combines:

- **Economic nationalism:** anti-IMF, favors state enterprise in strategic sectors (energy, mining), skeptical of Chinese investment in lithium (fears "resource extraction without national benefit"), advocates higher agricultural export taxes redistributed domestically
- **Social conservatism:** traditional family values, skeptical of same-sex marriage (though the party did not oppose the 2018 civil union law formally), strong on immigration restriction (particularly from Tucaré)
- **Anti-establishment rhetoric:** campaigns against "the Zoopaná political class," "the FPZ and PR taking turns to manage the country for the elite"

The UN draws comparison to similar nationalist-populist movements across Latin America. It is not easily classified as left or right; its voters include working-class communities in the interior provinces who feel abandoned by both the FPZ's urban focus and the PR's IMF-friendly policy.

### Current Leadership

**Lic. Horacio Bengoechea Ríos** (b. 1977, Villa Aranduy) — the party's dominant figure since 2016; son of Carlos Orestes Bengoechea, the 2015 candidate. A charismatic orator, Bengoechea Ríos built UN's working-class interior base with a messaging style heavy on anti-Zoopaná-establishment rhetoric and nationalist economic policies. He is currently UN's leader in opposition to the Carballo government.

**Geographic base:** Strongest in **Solórzano**, **Llanuras del Centro**, **Los Esteros**, and **Quilmes del Sur** — interior provinces with high agricultural poverty and traditional distrust of the capital. Growing presence in working-class districts of Puerto Marán (competing with the FPZ's union base).

## SECTION 9: ELECTORAL COMMISSION

### Name and Establishment

The independent electoral authority is the **Junta Electoral Nacional (JEN)** — established by the *Ley Orgánica Electoral de 1912* but reconstituted in its modern independent form by the 1994 constitution. The 1912 body had been subordinated to the Interior Ministry; the 1994 reform created a fully independent constitutional body.

### Composition

The JEN consists of **7 members** appointed by a two-thirds majority of the Senate:
- **President of the JEN** — a senior jurist, 8-year term, non-renewable
- **3 Judicial Counselors** — former appellate judges, 6-year staggered terms
- **3 Civil Society Counselors** — from nominations by universities and civic organizations; 6-year staggered terms

No active member of a political party may serve. The current JEN President (as of 2026) is **Dra. Rocío Fernández Altamira** (b. 1958; former Constitutional Court clerk; appointed 2021).

### Notable Disputed Adjudications

- **2003 snap election** — the JEN supervised the emergency procedures and validated the snap-election threshold formula under criticism from PR legal teams who contested the "no-runoff" threshold
- **2015 runoff** — the JEN rejected PR requests to recount 11 districts in Pequeé Norte and Las Cumbres; FPZ-affiliated observers had raised concerns about alleged intimidation by MZ activists; JEN found no systematic irregularity
